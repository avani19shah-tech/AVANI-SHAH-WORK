Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 2 · Structure & Functions of Cells

Q10. Describe the supporting cells (glia) of the nervous system.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Neurons could not survive or function without the supporting cells known as neuroglia or simply glia (from the Greek for "glue"). Once thought merely to hold neurons together, glia are now recognized as active partners in nearly every neural process — insulating axons, regulating the chemical environment, defending against infection, and even modulating synaptic transmission. Glia are extremely numerous (roughly equal to or greater than the number of neurons) and, unlike most neurons, retain the ability to divide. This answer describes the types of glia in the central and peripheral nervous systems, their structure and functions, and their clinical importance.

1. Glia of the Central Nervous System

(a) Astrocytes

Star-shaped and the most abundant glia. Their functions include: providing structural support; regulating the ionic and chemical environment (buffering K⁺, recycling neurotransmitters such as glutamate); forming part of the blood–brain barrier with their end-feet; supplying nutrients (glucose/lactate) to neurons; and contributing to synaptic transmission (the "tripartite synapse") and repair (glial scarring).

(b) Oligodendrocytes

Form the myelin sheath around axons in the CNS. A single oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of several different axons, speeding conduction (saltatory conduction).

(c) Microglia

The immune cells of the CNS. Derived from the immune system, they act as phagocytes, clearing debris, dead cells and pathogens, and mediate the brain's inflammatory response.

(d) Ependymal cells

Line the ventricles and central canal; they help form and circulate cerebrospinal fluid (as part of the choroid plexus) and their cilia move the CSF.

2. Glia of the Peripheral Nervous System

(a) Schwann cells

Form the myelin around peripheral axons — but, unlike oligodendrocytes, each Schwann cell myelinates only one segment of one axon. They also aid peripheral nerve regeneration, which is why peripheral nerves recover better than central ones.

(b) Satellite cells

Surround and support the cell bodies of neurons in peripheral ganglia, regulating their environment.

Clinical relevance:
  • Multiple sclerosis — autoimmune destruction of oligodendrocyte myelin in the CNS.
  • Guillain–Barré syndrome — destruction of Schwann-cell myelin in the PNS.
  • Gliomas — the commonest primary brain tumours arise from glia (they divide, unlike neurons).
  • Neuroinflammation — activated microglia and reactive astrocytes are increasingly implicated in neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's) and even in mood disorders.
Exam-ready summary: Glia are the supporting cells of the nervous system. In the CNS: astrocytes (support, chemical regulation, BBB, nutrition), oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin, several axons each), microglia (immune/phagocytic) and ependymal cells (CSF). In the PNS: Schwann cells (myelinate one axon, aid regeneration) and satellite cells (support ganglion cell bodies). Glia divide (source of gliomas) and are central to demyelinating diseases (MS = oligodendrocytes; Guillain–Barré = Schwann) and neuroinflammation.

Conclusion

Far from being passive "glue," glia are indispensable partners of neurons — insulating, feeding, protecting and regulating them, and actively shaping neural signalling. Their disorders (demyelination, tumours, inflammation) are among the most important in clinical neuroscience. For the clinical psychologist, appreciating glial function deepens the biological understanding of both neurological disease and the emerging links between neuroinflammation and psychiatric disorders.
