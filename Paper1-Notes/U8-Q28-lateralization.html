Q28. Explain the lateralization of functions in the brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Lateralization (hemispheric specialization) is the principle that the two cerebral hemispheres, though anatomically similar, are functionally different — each specialized for certain kinds of processing. It is one of the two great organizing principles of neuropsychology (the other being localization). Although the hemispheres work together, connected by the corpus callosum, the left hemisphere is usually dominant for language and analytic processing, while the right excels at spatial and holistic processing. This answer explains lateralization, the evidence for it (especially split-brain research), the functions of each hemisphere, its relation to handedness, and its clinical importance.

1. The Concept

Lateralization means that a function is more strongly represented in, or dependent on, one hemisphere. It does not mean a hemisphere works alone — normal cognition requires both, communicating via the corpus callosum. The degree of lateralization varies between functions and individuals.

2. Evidence for Lateralization

  • Lesion studies: left-hemisphere damage typically causes aphasia (language loss); right-hemisphere damage typically causes spatial deficits and neglect.
  • Split-brain studies (Sperry & Gazzaniga): after the corpus callosum is cut, an object shown only to the left visual field (→ right hemisphere) can be picked out by the left hand but cannot be named, because language is in the disconnected left hemisphere. This directly demonstrated hemispheric specialization.
  • Wada test: anaesthetizing one hemisphere (via carotid injection) reveals which side controls language.
  • Dichotic listening and functional imaging confirm the pattern.

4. Lateralization and Handedness

Language is left-lateralized in ~95% of right-handers and ~70% of left-handers (many left-handers are still left-dominant or have bilateral language). Handedness itself reflects motor lateralization: the left hemisphere controls the right (dominant) hand in most people.

5. The "Dominant vs Non-Dominant" Terminology

The left hemisphere was historically called "dominant" because language is so obvious, but the right hemisphere is equally important — it is "dominant" for spatial and emotional processing. Modern usage prefers "language-dominant" to avoid implying one hemisphere is superior.

Clinical relevance: Lateralization guides diagnosis and rehabilitation. Left-hemisphere stroke → aphasia, apraxia, right-sided weakness, and often depression/catastrophic reaction. Right-hemisphere stroke → left-sided neglect (ignoring the left side of space/body), anosognosia (denial of the deficit), impaired prosody and spatial disorientation, sometimes with inappropriate cheerfulness. Recognizing lateralized patterns helps localize lesions and tailor therapy (e.g., neglect rehabilitation for right-hemisphere patients).
Exam-ready summary: Lateralization = the two hemispheres are functionally specialized, connected by the corpus callosum. The left is usually dominant for language, analytic/sequential processing, verbal memory, calculation and praxis; the right for visuospatial processing, holistic/gestalt processing, faces, prosody, music and emotion, including spatial attention (its damage causes neglect). Evidence comes from lesions, split-brain studies (Sperry & Gazzaniga), the Wada test and imaging. Language is left-lateralized in ~95% of right-handers. Clinically, left-hemisphere damage → aphasia/right weakness; right-hemisphere damage → neglect, anosognosia and spatial deficits.

Conclusion

Lateralization reveals that the brain achieves efficiency by dividing labour between two specialized but cooperating hemispheres. The left–language/analytic and right–spatial/holistic distinction, established most convincingly by split-brain research, is fundamental to interpreting the very different syndromes of left- and right-hemisphere damage, and is therefore indispensable to the clinical psychologist's assessment and rehabilitation of brain-injured patients.
