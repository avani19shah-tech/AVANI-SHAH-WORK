Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q23. Explain the role of neurotransmitters and neuromodulators in various other aspects of behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Beyond learning and memory, neurotransmitters and neuromodulators shape virtually every aspect of behaviour — mood and emotion, sleep and arousal, motivation and reward, anxiety and stress, movement, appetite, pain, aggression and social behaviour. No single chemical "controls" a behaviour; rather, each behaviour emerges from the interplay of several transmitter systems. Understanding these roles is central to biological psychiatry, because it explains both the symptoms of mental disorders and the actions of the drugs used to treat them. This answer surveys the role of the major transmitters across the principal domains of behaviour.

1. Mood and Emotion

Mood is regulated chiefly by the monoamines: serotonin (emotional stability, well-being), noradrenaline (energy, drive) and dopamine (pleasure, motivation). A functional deficit of serotonin and noradrenaline underlies the monoamine hypothesis of depression, and correcting it is the basis of antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs).

2. Sleep and Arousal

The sleep–wake cycle is controlled by several systems: acetylcholine and orexin promote wakefulness and REM; noradrenaline and histamine promote alertness; adenosine builds up during waking to promote sleep (caffeine blocks it); serotonin regulates sleep architecture; and melatonin signals the circadian timing of sleep.

3. Motivation and Reward

Dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway is the central chemical of reward, reinforcement and "wanting." It drives goal-directed behaviour and is hijacked by all addictive drugs. Endogenous opioids contribute the "liking"/pleasure component.

4. Anxiety and Stress

GABA is the brain's calming, anti-anxiety transmitter (enhanced by benzodiazepines). Noradrenaline drives hyperarousal and the fight-or-flight response. CRH and the stress hormone cortisol initiate and sustain the stress response; their overactivity is linked to anxiety disorders and depression.

5. Movement

Movement depends on a balance in the basal ganglia between dopamine (facilitates movement — lost in Parkinson's disease) and acetylcholine, with GABA providing inhibition. Disruption produces movement disorders such as Parkinson's (dopamine deficit) and Huntington's chorea (GABA/striatal loss).

6. Appetite and Feeding

Neuropeptide Y and endocannabinoids stimulate appetite; serotonin and CCK promote satiety; leptin and insulin signal energy status. This chemistry underlies eating disorders and obesity.

7. Pain

Substance P transmits pain signals; the endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins) and endocannabinoids suppress them — the basis of natural and drug-induced analgesia.

8. Aggression and Social Behaviour

Low serotonin is associated with impulsivity and aggression; testosterone and vasopressin modulate aggression; and oxytocin promotes trust, bonding and prosocial behaviour.

Clinical relevance: This mapping directly explains psychiatric treatment: SSRIs (serotonin) for depression/anxiety/OCD; benzodiazepines (GABA) for anxiety; antipsychotics (dopamine block) for psychosis; L-DOPA (dopamine) for Parkinson's; stimulants (dopamine/noradrenaline) for ADHD; opioids for pain; and orexin antagonists for insomnia. It also explains drug side-effects and addiction (dopamine reward). The clinical psychologist uses this framework to understand symptoms and medication within a biopsychosocial formulation.
Exam-ready summary: Behaviour arises from the interplay of transmitter systems. Mood — serotonin, noradrenaline, dopamine. Sleep/arousal — ACh, orexin, noradrenaline, adenosine, melatonin. Reward/motivation — dopamine (mesolimbic), opioids. Anxiety/stress — GABA (calming), noradrenaline, CRH/cortisol. Movement — dopamine vs acetylcholine balance, GABA. Appetite — NPY/endocannabinoids (↑), serotonin/CCK (↓). Pain — substance P (transmit), endorphins/endocannabinoids (relieve). Aggression/social — low serotonin (aggression), oxytocin/vasopressin (bonding). This mapping explains psychiatric symptoms and the actions of psychotropic drugs.

Conclusion

Neurotransmitters and neuromodulators orchestrate the full range of behaviour, from mood and sleep to movement, appetite and social bonding, each behaviour emerging from a characteristic chemical balance. This neurochemical map is the foundation of psychopharmacology and of the biological component of the biopsychosocial model, making it essential knowledge for the clinical psychologist in understanding both disorder and treatment.
