Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q17. Discuss the role of acetylcholine in behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Acetylcholine (ACh) was the first neurotransmitter to be discovered (by Otto Loewi in 1921) and remains one of the most important. It functions both in the peripheral nervous system — at the neuromuscular junction and in the autonomic system — and in the central nervous system, where it plays a crucial role in attention, arousal, learning and memory. Its clinical importance is enormous: the loss of cholinergic neurons is a defining feature of Alzheimer's disease. This answer describes the synthesis and breakdown of ACh, its receptors, its major pathways, its behavioural functions, and its clinical relevance.

1. Synthesis and Breakdown

ACh is synthesized from choline and acetyl-CoA by the enzyme choline acetyltransferase (ChAT). After release it is rapidly broken down in the synaptic cleft by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) into choline (which is taken back up) and acetate. This rapid breakdown allows precise, brief signalling — and drugs that block AChE (cholinesterase inhibitors) prolong ACh action.

2. Receptors

ACh acts on two receptor families:

  • Nicotinic receptors — ionotropic (fast); found at the neuromuscular junction, autonomic ganglia and in the CNS. Stimulated by nicotine.
  • Muscarinic receptors — metabotropic (slow, G-protein); found in the CNS and at parasympathetic target organs. Stimulated by muscarine, blocked by atropine.

3. Major Cholinergic Pathways

  • Neuromuscular junction — ACh causes skeletal muscle contraction (voluntary movement).
  • Autonomic nervous system — ACh is the transmitter of the parasympathetic ("rest and digest") system and of all preganglionic fibres.
  • Basal forebrain (nucleus basalis of Meynert) → cortex and hippocampus — critical for attention, learning and memory.
  • Pontomesencephalic (brainstem) nuclei → thalamus and cortex — regulate arousal and REM sleep.

4. Role in Behaviour

  • Learning and memory: ACh from the basal forebrain is essential for encoding new memories and for attention to relevant stimuli. Boosting ACh enhances, and blocking it impairs, memory.
  • Attention and arousal: cholinergic activity increases cortical alertness and the ability to focus on and process information.
  • Sleep–wake cycle: ACh is highly active during waking and REM (dreaming) sleep, and low during deep sleep.
  • Movement: ACh drives all voluntary muscle contraction at the neuromuscular junction.
  • Autonomic functions: slows the heart, stimulates digestion, constricts pupils (parasympathetic).
Clinical relevance:
  • Alzheimer's disease — degeneration of cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain is a hallmark, correlating with memory loss. Treatment uses cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) to raise ACh and modestly improve cognition.
  • Anticholinergic effects — drugs that block muscarinic receptors (some antidepressants, antipsychotics, antihistamines) cause dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, confusion and memory impairment, especially in the elderly.
  • Myasthenia gravis — autoimmune attack on nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing muscle weakness; treated with cholinesterase inhibitors.
  • Nicotine addiction — nicotine stimulates cholinergic (and reward) systems.
Exam-ready summary: Acetylcholine (ACh), the first transmitter discovered, is made from choline + acetyl-CoA by ChAT and broken down by acetylcholinesterase. It acts on nicotinic (fast, ionotropic) and muscarinic (slow, metabotropic) receptors. Pathways: neuromuscular junction (movement), parasympathetic system, and basal-forebrain projections to cortex/hippocampus (attention, learning, memory) plus brainstem arousal/REM circuits. Behaviourally it drives memory, attention, arousal, sleep and movement. Loss of cholinergic neurons causes Alzheimer's memory loss (treated with cholinesterase inhibitors); anticholinergic drugs impair memory; myasthenia gravis attacks nicotinic receptors.

Conclusion

Acetylcholine bridges the peripheral control of movement and the central control of memory and attention. Its degeneration in Alzheimer's disease and its manipulation by cholinergic and anticholinergic drugs make it one of the most clinically significant neurotransmitters for the clinical psychologist, especially in the assessment and understanding of dementia and drug-induced cognitive impairment.
