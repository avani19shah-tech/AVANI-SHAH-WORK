Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 9 · Frontal Lobe Syndrome

Q33. Describe the disturbances of emotion and personality in frontal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Among the most striking and socially disabling consequences of frontal lobe damage are changes in emotion and personality. The prefrontal cortex, especially its orbitofrontal and medial regions, regulates emotional expression, social behaviour, motivation and the use of emotion in decision-making. When damaged, a person's intellect and language may remain intact, yet their character transforms — they may become impulsive and tactless, or apathetic and emotionless. This answer describes the emotional and personality disturbances of frontal lobe syndrome, distinguishing the orbitofrontal and medial sub-syndromes, the famous case of Phineas Gage, and the clinical significance.

1. Why the Frontal Lobe Controls Emotion and Personality

The prefrontal cortex connects densely with the limbic system (amygdala, hypothalamus) and regulates emotional impulses, matching them to social context. It also uses emotional "signals" to guide decisions (Damasio's somatic-marker hypothesis). Damage therefore releases or flattens emotion and impairs socially appropriate, forward-looking behaviour.

2. The Orbitofrontal (Disinhibition) Syndrome

Damage to the orbitofrontal cortex produces a disinhibited, "pseudopsychopathic" picture:

  • Impulsivity and poor judgment — acting without thinking of consequences.
  • Social disinhibition — tactlessness, coarse or inappropriate behaviour, sexual or verbal indiscretion.
  • Emotional lability and irritability — rapid, shallow mood swings; sudden anger.
  • Witzelsucht — a tendency to make facetious, silly jokes and puns.
  • Reduced empathy and social insight — difficulty appreciating others' feelings and the impact of one's behaviour.

3. The Medial / Anterior Cingulate (Apathy) Syndrome

Damage to the medial frontal cortex/anterior cingulate produces an apathetic, "pseudodepressed" picture:

  • Apathy and abulia — profound loss of drive, initiative and spontaneity.
  • Flat affect and emotional indifference — reduced emotional responsiveness.
  • Reduced speech and movement, in the extreme reaching akinetic mutism (awake but neither moving nor speaking).

This can be mistaken for depression, but the patient typically lacks the subjective sadness and guilt of true depression.

4. Impaired Emotional Decision-Making

Frontal (especially ventromedial) patients make poor real-world decisions despite normal intellect. On the Iowa Gambling Task they continue to choose high-risk options despite mounting losses, failing to be guided by the emotional "somatic markers" that normally warn of bad choices. This links emotion, cognition and the frontal lobe.

5. The Classic Case — Phineas Gage (1848)

Phineas Gage, a railway foreman, survived an iron rod driven through his orbitofrontal cortex. His intellect, memory, speech and movement were preserved, but his personality was transformed — from responsible, capable and well-liked to impulsive, profane, unreliable and socially inappropriate ("no longer Gage"). This foundational case demonstrated that the frontal lobe is essential for personality, social conduct and emotional regulation.

Clinical relevance: These changes are among the most distressing for families and the most disabling for social and occupational functioning, often more so than the cognitive deficits. They are seen in traumatic brain injury (the orbitofrontal cortex is highly vulnerable), behavioural-variant frontotemporal dementia (which characteristically presents with disinhibition or apathy and loss of empathy), ACA stroke and tumours. Crucially, patients frequently lack insight, so they under-report their difficulties, and the changes can be misdiagnosed as primary psychiatric illness (mania, depression, personality disorder). Family education and behavioural management are central.
Exam-ready summary: The prefrontal cortex regulates emotion, social behaviour and emotion-based decisions (via limbic links and somatic markers). Damage causes two contrasting pictures: orbitofrontal = disinhibition ("pseudopsychopathic") — impulsivity, tactlessness, emotional lability, Witzelsucht, reduced empathy; medial/anterior cingulate = apathy/abulia ("pseudodepressed") — loss of drive, flat affect, in the extreme akinetic mutism. Emotional decision-making fails (Iowa Gambling Task). The classic case is Phineas Gage (orbitofrontal injury → personality change with preserved intellect). Insight is often lost, and the changes mimic psychiatric disorders; seen in TBI, frontotemporal dementia and ACA stroke.

Conclusion

Disturbances of emotion and personality show that the frontal lobe is the seat of the socially and emotionally regulated self. Damage can either release behaviour (disinhibition) or extinguish it (apathy), while sparing intellect — a dissociation dramatically illustrated by Phineas Gage. Because these changes are socially devastating, easily mistaken for psychiatric illness, and accompanied by poor insight, they demand sensitive assessment, family support and behavioural rehabilitation from the clinical psychologist.
