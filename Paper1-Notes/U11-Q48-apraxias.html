Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q48. Describe the apraxias.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Apraxia is the inability to carry out learned, skilled, purposeful movements despite intact strength, sensation, coordination, comprehension and cooperation. The patient knows what to do and can physically move, but cannot organize the movement correctly on command. Apraxia reflects a disorder of the motor programs for skilled action, which are stored and organized chiefly in the left (dominant) parietal lobe and executed through frontal motor systems. This answer defines apraxia, distinguishes it from weakness and agnosia, describes its major types, the tests used, and its clinical significance.

1. Definition and Distinctions

Apraxia is a failure of skilled action not explained by:

  • Weakness/paralysis — strength is intact.
  • Sensory loss or incoordination (ataxia).
  • Poor comprehension — the patient understands the request.
  • Inattention or unwillingness.

It reflects a breakdown in the programming and organization of movement.

2. Ideomotor Apraxia

The commonest type: the patient cannot perform a single learned gesture on command (e.g., "show me how to wave goodbye," "pretend to brush your teeth," "salute"). Errors include using a body part as the tool (e.g., using a finger as a toothbrush) and clumsy, mislocated movements. Interestingly, the action may be performed automatically in the real situation. It results from damage to the left parietal lobe or its connections to motor cortex.

3. Ideational Apraxia

A disorder of the concept or sequence of a complex, multi-step action using real objects. The patient cannot carry out a sequence in the correct order (e.g., preparing tea — may put the tea in before the water, or use objects in the wrong order). It reflects loss of the idea/plan of the action and is seen with more extensive left parietal or diffuse damage (e.g., dementia).

4. Constructional Apraxia

Inability to copy, draw or assemble shapes and constructions (e.g., copying a cube, drawing a clock, arranging blocks) — a disorder of organizing spatial elements into a whole. It is common with parietal damage; right-parietal cases tend to produce disorganized, spatially distorted drawings, left-parietal cases oversimplified ones.

5. Other Types

  • Dressing apraxia — inability to dress correctly (orienting clothes to the body), typically with right parietal damage; overlaps with neglect.
  • Buccofacial (orofacial) apraxia — inability to perform facial/oral movements on command (e.g., blow out a match, whistle, cough).
  • Gait apraxia — inability to initiate and organize walking despite normal leg strength (frontal / normal-pressure hydrocephalus).

6. Assessment

Apraxia is tested by asking the patient to: perform gestures to command (wave, salute), pantomime tool use (brushing teeth), imitate the examiner's gestures, carry out multi-step sequences with real objects, and copy drawings/constructions. Errors of the correct type, with intact strength and comprehension, indicate apraxia.

Clinical relevance: Apraxia significantly impairs daily activities (dressing, using tools and utensils, self-care) even when strength is normal, so it is important for rehabilitation and for judging independence. It localizes damage — ideomotor/ideational to the left parietal, constructional and dressing to the parietal (often right) lobe — and is common after stroke and in dementias (especially corticobasal degeneration and Alzheimer's). The clinical psychologist assesses apraxia to explain functional difficulties and to guide occupational-therapy interventions.
Exam-ready summary: Apraxia = inability to perform learned, skilled, purposeful movements despite intact strength, sensation, coordination and comprehension — a disorder of motor programs, chiefly in the left parietal lobe. Types: ideomotor (cannot perform a single gesture to command — e.g., wave, pantomime tool use; uses body part as tool); ideational (cannot sequence a multi-step action with real objects); constructional (cannot copy/assemble shapes — parietal); dressing (right parietal); buccofacial (oral gestures); and gait apraxia (frontal/NPH). Tested by gesture-to-command, imitation, object use and copying. It disables daily activities and localizes lesions.

Conclusion

The apraxias are disorders of skilled action in which the patient can move but cannot correctly organize purposeful, learned movements — from single gestures (ideomotor) to complex sequences (ideational) and spatial constructions (constructional). Because they disable everyday activities despite intact strength, and because they localize damage largely to the parietal lobe, their recognition and assessment are essential for the clinical psychologist in explaining functional difficulties and planning rehabilitation.
