Q52. Describe the disturbances of reading ability (alexia / acquired dyslexia).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Alexia (or acquired dyslexia) is the loss or impairment of the ability to read, caused by brain damage, in a person who was previously literate. Reading is a complex process that converts visual symbols into language and meaning, involving the occipital visual areas, the connections to the language areas of the dominant hemisphere, and the parietal angular gyrus. Because reading has several component processes, it can break down in distinct ways, and the study of acquired dyslexias has been central to cognitive neuropsychology (revealing the "dual-route" model of reading). This answer describes the reading process and the major disturbances of reading.

1. The Reading Process and the Dual-Route Model

Reading requires converting visual word-forms into sound and meaning. The influential dual-route model proposes two routes:

  • Lexical (direct) route — recognizes the whole familiar word and retrieves its meaning/pronunciation directly (needed for irregular words like "yacht").
  • Phonological (sublexical) route — sounds out the word letter-by-letter using spelling-to-sound rules (needed for new/unfamiliar words and non-words).

Reading depends on occipital visual cortex, the visual word-form area (left occipito-temporal), and the parietal angular gyrus linking vision to language.

2. Peripheral (Perceptual) Alexias

  • Pure alexia (alexia without agraphia / "word blindness") — a classic disconnection syndrome: damage to the left occipital cortex plus the splenium of the corpus callosum cuts visual information off from the language areas. The patient can write but cannot read their own writing, and reads slowly letter-by-letter. Writing, speech and comprehension are otherwise intact.
  • Neglect dyslexia — misreading one side of words (usually the left), associated with right-parietal neglect.

3. Central (Linguistic) Dyslexias

These reflect damage to one of the reading routes:

  • Surface dyslexia — damage to the lexical route: the patient reads by sounding out, so regular words are fine but irregular words are "regularized" (reads "yacht" as "yatched").
  • Phonological dyslexia — damage to the phonological route: the patient reads familiar words (lexical route intact) but cannot sound out unfamiliar words or non-words.
  • Deep dyslexia — a severe form with semantic errors (reads "dog" as "cat"), inability to read non-words, and difficulty with abstract/function words — reflecting damage to both routes with reliance on a degraded semantic route.

4. Alexia with Agraphia

Damage to the dominant angular gyrus causes combined loss of reading and writing (the angular gyrus is the "reading/writing centre"), often with other parietal signs (e.g., Gerstmann's features).

5. Assessment

Reading is assessed by having the patient read aloud and comprehend words, comparing regular vs irregular words and real words vs non-words, and analysing the error type (regularization, semantic errors, letter-by-letter reading) to identify the dyslexia subtype and localize the lesion.

Clinical relevance: The acquired dyslexias are a triumph of cognitive neuropsychology — the double dissociation between surface and phonological dyslexia proved the dual-route model of reading. Clinically, the subtype localizes the lesion (pure alexia → left occipital + splenium; alexia with agraphia → angular gyrus) and, importantly, guides model-based rehabilitation targeting the specific damaged route. Reading and writing assessment is a routine part of the neuropsychological examination.
Exam-ready summary: Alexia (acquired dyslexia) is acquired impairment of reading. Reading uses a lexical (whole-word) and a phonological (sounding-out) route (dual-route model). Peripheral: pure alexia (alexia without agraphia — left occipital + splenium disconnection; can write but not read, reads letter-by-letter) and neglect dyslexia. Central: surface dyslexia (lexical route damaged — regularizes irregular words), phonological dyslexia (phonological route damaged — cannot read non-words), and deep dyslexia (both routes; semantic errors like "dog"→"cat"). Alexia with agraphia = dominant angular gyrus. The surface/phonological double dissociation proved the dual-route model and guides targeted rehabilitation.

Conclusion

Disturbances of reading reveal that reading is not a single skill but a set of component routes that can fail independently, producing the peripheral alexias (pure alexia, neglect dyslexia) and the central dyslexias (surface, phonological, deep). Their study established the dual-route model of reading and provided cognitive neuropsychology with its classic double dissociations, while clinically they localize lesions and guide targeted rehabilitation — knowledge of direct use to the clinical psychologist.
