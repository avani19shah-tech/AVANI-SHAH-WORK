Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 1 · Anatomy of the Brain

Q1. Describe the major anatomical sub-divisions of the human brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The human brain, weighing about 1300–1400 g, is the control centre of the entire nervous system. To study such a complex organ systematically, anatomists divide it into major sub-divisions based on embryological development. Early in gestation the neural tube expands at its rostral end into three primary vesicles — the prosencephalon (forebrain), mesencephalon (midbrain) and rhombencephalon (hindbrain). Two of these further subdivide, giving five secondary vesicles that mature into all the adult brain structures. This developmental scheme is the clearest and most examinable way to describe the brain's gross organization, and it maps neatly onto function — from the higher cognition of the forebrain to the vital reflexes of the hindbrain.

1. Prosencephalon (Forebrain)

The forebrain is the largest and most highly developed division, responsible for the "higher" mental functions that distinguish humans. It subdivides into the telencephalon and diencephalon.

(a) Telencephalon

This gives rise to the two cerebral hemispheres and their contents: the cerebral cortex (seat of perception, voluntary movement, language, reasoning and personality), the basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus — for movement selection and habit learning), and the limbic structures such as the hippocampus (memory) and amygdala (emotion). It is the substrate of conscious experience and complex behaviour.

(b) Diencephalon

Deep to the hemispheres, the diencephalon includes the thalamus (the great sensory and motor relay to the cortex), the hypothalamus (master regulator of homeostasis and the endocrine system), the epithalamus (pineal gland — melatonin, circadian rhythm) and the subthalamus (motor control).

2. Mesencephalon (Midbrain)

The midbrain is the short segment connecting forebrain and hindbrain. It comprises the tectum (roof), which carries the superior colliculi (visual reflexes/orienting) and inferior colliculi (auditory reflexes); and the tegmentum (floor), containing the substantia nigra (dopamine, movement — degenerates in Parkinson's disease), the red nucleus (motor coordination), and parts of the reticular formation and cranial-nerve nuclei (III, IV). It also contains the cerebral aqueduct linking the third and fourth ventricles.

3. Rhombencephalon (Hindbrain)

(a) Metencephalon

This forms the pons (a relay bridge carrying tracts between cortex and cerebellum, with cranial-nerve nuclei and respiratory centres) and the cerebellum ("little brain"), which coordinates movement, posture, balance and motor learning, and contributes to some cognitive functions.

(b) Myelencephalon

This becomes the medulla oblongata, the most caudal part continuous with the spinal cord. It houses the vital autonomic centres controlling respiration, heart rate and blood pressure, plus reflex centres for coughing, swallowing and vomiting. Damage here is rapidly fatal.

4. The Brainstem and Overall Hierarchy

The brainstem (midbrain + pons + medulla) is a functional grouping that connects the brain to the spinal cord, carries all ascending/descending tracts, houses ten of the twelve cranial-nerve nuclei, and contains the reticular formation governing arousal and consciousness. Broadly, function ascends in a hierarchy: the hindbrain manages survival reflexes, the midbrain manages arousal and orienting, and the forebrain manages cognition, emotion and voluntary action.

Clinical relevance: Each division produces characteristic lesion effects — forebrain damage affects cognition, personality and voluntary movement; midbrain (substantia nigra) degeneration causes Parkinson's disease; cerebellar damage causes ataxia; and medullary damage disrupts breathing and cardiac function, often fatally. Knowing the sub-divisions lets the clinical psychologist anticipate the type of deficit a given lesion will produce.
Exam-ready summary: Developmentally the brain forms three primary vesicles — prosencephalon, mesencephalon, rhombencephalon — that give five secondary vesicles: telencephalon (cortex, basal ganglia, limbic), diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus), mesencephalon (midbrain: colliculi, substantia nigra), metencephalon (pons, cerebellum) and myelencephalon (medulla). Function ascends from survival reflexes (hindbrain) through arousal (midbrain) to higher cognition (forebrain).

Conclusion

The embryological sub-division of the brain provides an orderly framework linking structure to function. It shows that the human brain is built in layers of increasing complexity, from the life-sustaining medulla to the cognitively sophisticated cerebral cortex. This foundational map underlies all further study of localization, lesion syndromes and neuropsychological assessment.
