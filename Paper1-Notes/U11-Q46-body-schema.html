Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q46. Describe the disturbances of body schema perception.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The body schema is the brain's internal representation of the body — its parts, their positions, and their relationship to one another and to space. It allows us to know where our limbs are, to recognize our body as our own, and to act on and orient within it. This representation is built and maintained chiefly by the parietal lobe, integrating touch, proprioception and vision. Damage to the parietal lobe therefore produces fascinating and clinically important disturbances of body schema — the patient may ignore, deny, or fail to identify parts of their own body. This answer describes the body schema and the major disorders that follow its disruption.

1. What is the Body Schema?

The body schema is a dynamic, largely unconscious map of the body maintained mainly in the parietal lobe (especially the right, and the superior parietal lobule), integrating somatosensory, proprioceptive and visual information. It underlies our sense of body ownership, the knowledge of limb position, and the ability to localize and name body parts. Its disruption produces disorders that seem strange because they involve the patient's own body.

2. Hemineglect (Unilateral Spatial Neglect)

Most often after right parietal damage, the patient ignores the left side of their body and of space — failing to wash, dress or attend to the left side, eating food only from the right of the plate, and drawing only the right half of objects. Although related to spatial attention, it has a strong body-schema component (personal neglect).

3. Anosognosia

Anosognosia is the denial or unawareness of a deficit — classically, a patient with a left hemiplegia (from right parietal/hemisphere damage) denies that the paralysed limb is weak or even that it belongs to them. This lack of insight severely complicates rehabilitation.

4. Asomatognosia and Somatoparaphrenia

  • Asomatognosia — loss of awareness of, or failure to recognize, part of one's own body (e.g., neglecting or not recognizing the left arm as one's own).
  • Somatoparaphrenia — a delusional belief that a limb belongs to someone else ("that arm is my brother's").

5. Autotopagnosia

Autotopagnosia is the inability to localize or name parts of one's own body (or others') on command — e.g., cannot point to the elbow when asked — despite intact language and sensation. It reflects loss of the structured body map.

6. Gerstmann's Syndrome

Damage to the dominant (left) angular gyrus of the parietal lobe produces the tetrad of Gerstmann's syndrome:

  • Finger agnosia — inability to identify or name individual fingers.
  • Right–left disorientation — inability to distinguish right from left.
  • Agraphia — impaired writing.
  • Acalculia — impaired calculation.

Finger agnosia and right–left disorientation are body-schema components of this syndrome.

Clinical relevance: Body-schema disorders are dramatic, common after right-hemisphere stroke, and have major consequences for safety and rehabilitation. A patient with neglect and anosognosia may not recognize their disability, may injure the neglected side, and may resist therapy because they deny the problem. Recognizing these disorders is essential for the clinical psychologist to plan realistic rehabilitation, ensure safety, and support insight. Gerstmann's syndrome localizes damage to the dominant angular gyrus.
Exam-ready summary: The body schema is the parietal lobe's internal map of the body (mainly right parietal). Its disorders include: hemineglect (ignoring the left side of body/space, right parietal); anosognosia (denial of a deficit, e.g., of hemiplegia); asomatognosia/somatoparaphrenia (failure to recognize a limb as one's own / believing it belongs to another); autotopagnosia (cannot localize/name body parts); and Gerstmann's syndrome (finger agnosia, right–left disorientation, agraphia, acalculia — dominant angular gyrus). These are common after right-hemisphere stroke and gravely affect insight, safety and rehabilitation.

Conclusion

Disorders of body schema perception reveal that our sense of our own body is an active construction of the parietal lobe. Their disruption produces the striking phenomena of neglect, denial and misidentification of one's own body — disorders that are common, disabling and often accompanied by loss of insight. Their recognition is vital for the clinical psychologist in planning safe and realistic rehabilitation after parietal (especially right-hemisphere) damage.
