Q24. Discuss the relationship between the structure and function of the brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The central question of neuropsychology is how the physical structure of the brain gives rise to psychological function — how tissue produces thought, emotion, perception and action. The relationship between structure and function is the foundational assumption of the entire discipline: that mental abilities depend on specific neural substrates, so that when a structure is damaged, its function is lost in a predictable way. Yet the exact nature of this relationship has been debated for two centuries, ranging from strict localization to holistic views. This answer discusses the historical positions on the structure–function relationship, the modern network/connectionist synthesis, and its clinical implications.

1. The Core Premise

Neuropsychology assumes that the mind is a product of the brain and that different mental functions depend on different neural structures. Evidence comes from the lesion method (damage to a region removes a function), from stimulation studies, and from modern functional imaging (an active region "lights up" during a task). The consistency of these findings establishes that structure and function are systematically related.

2. Historical Positions

(a) Strict Localization

The view that each psychological function has a discrete "centre." It began with Gall's phrenology and was given scientific credibility by Broca (1861), whose patient's left frontal lesion caused loss of speech, and by Wernicke, who localized comprehension. Strength: explains focal syndromes. Weakness: too simple for complex functions.

(b) Equipotentiality and Mass Action

Karl Lashley, from lesion studies in rats, argued that the cortex acts as a whole (equipotentiality) and that the severity of deficit depends on the amount of tissue destroyed (mass action), not its precise location. Strength: recognizes the distributed nature of complex learning. Weakness: cannot explain the very specific deficits seen after focal human lesions.

(c) Connectionism / Networks

The modern view, anticipated by Wernicke and developed by Geschwind and others, holds that functions are supported by distributed networks of interconnected regions. A "function" is an emergent property of a system, and a symptom can result from damage to a node or to the connections between nodes (a disconnection syndrome).

3. The Modern Synthesis

Contemporary neuropsychology integrates these positions:

  • Elementary functions (primary sensation, movement) are strongly and reliably localized to specific regions.
  • Complex functions (language, memory, attention, executive control) are organized as distributed but non-random networks, in which each region makes a specific contribution.
  • Luria's concept of functional systems captures this: a complex function is carried out by a chain of cooperating brain regions, so it can be disrupted in different ways by damage at different points in the chain.

4. Plasticity and the Dynamic Relationship

The structure–function relationship is not fixed. Neural plasticity allows functions to reorganize after damage (e.g., recovery of language, or the reassignment of cortex after limb loss). This means structure shapes function, but function (experience) can also reshape structure — the basis of learning and of rehabilitation.

Clinical relevance: The structure–function relationship is the logical basis of clinical neuropsychology: because functions map onto structures, a pattern of preserved and impaired abilities allows the clinician to infer the site and extent of brain damage, distinguish organic from functional disorders, and predict recovery. Plasticity provides the rationale for cognitive rehabilitation — retraining can help other regions take over lost functions.
Exam-ready summary: Neuropsychology assumes the mind is a product of the brain, with functions mapped onto structures (shown by lesions, stimulation and imaging). Historically: strict localization (Gall, Broca, Wernicke — one centre per function), equipotentiality/mass action (Lashley — cortex acts as a whole, deficit depends on amount of tissue), and connectionism/networks (functions are distributed; disconnection syndromes). The modern synthesis: elementary functions are localized, complex functions are distributed networks (Luria's functional systems), and the relationship is dynamic because of plasticity — the basis of recovery and rehabilitation.

Conclusion

The relationship between brain structure and psychological function is neither purely localized nor purely holistic but a combination: simple functions are pinned to particular places, while complex functions arise from the coordinated activity of distributed networks. This nuanced, plastic relationship is the intellectual foundation of neuropsychology, justifying both the localization of lesions and the possibility of rehabilitation.
