Q45. Describe the disturbances in sensory functions in parietal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The parietal lobe lies behind the central sulcus and contains the primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus) and the somatosensory association cortex. It is the brain's centre for bodily sensation — touch, pressure, temperature, pain, and especially the higher, integrative sensory functions such as recognizing objects by touch and localizing stimuli on the body. Damage to the parietal lobe therefore produces a distinctive pattern of sensory disturbance in which basic sensation may be relatively preserved while the cortical, discriminative and integrative aspects of sensation are lost. This answer describes the somatosensory organization of the parietal lobe and the sensory disturbances that follow its damage.

1. Somatosensory Organization of the Parietal Lobe

The primary somatosensory cortex (S1, postcentral gyrus, BA 3,1,2) receives touch, proprioception, temperature and pain from the opposite side of the body, arranged as a homunculus (with disproportionately large hand, lips and tongue). The somatosensory association cortex (superior parietal lobule) integrates these into higher-order perception, enabling recognition of objects by touch and awareness of the body in space.

2. Elementary vs Cortical Sensation

A key principle: parietal damage often spares basic (primary) sensation — the patient can still feel a touch, a pinprick or temperature — but impairs the cortical/discriminative and integrative sensory functions that require the cortex to analyse and combine sensory information. These "cortical sensory" deficits are the signature of parietal damage.

3. Cortical Sensory Disturbances

  • Astereognosis (tactile agnosia) — inability to recognize objects by touch (e.g., a coin or key placed in the hand with eyes closed) despite intact basic sensation.
  • Agraphesthesia — inability to identify numbers or letters written on the skin.
  • Impaired two-point discrimination — inability to tell whether one or two nearby points are touching the skin.
  • Impaired point localization — inability to say exactly where on the body a touch was applied.
  • Loss of position sense and discriminative touch — impaired proprioception and fine tactile discrimination.

4. Tactile Extinction (Sensory Inattention)

A striking sign, especially with right parietal damage: when both sides of the body are touched simultaneously, the patient reports feeling only the touch on the ipsilateral (same, unaffected) side and "extinguishes" (ignores) the touch on the opposite side — even though a single touch on that side is felt normally. This reflects a disorder of attention rather than of basic sensation.

5. Altered Pain and Temperature

Parietal (and thalamic) damage can alter pain and temperature perception, sometimes producing distorted or unpleasant sensations. (Severe spontaneous pain is more typical of thalamic damage — the "thalamic pain syndrome.")

Clinical relevance: These deficits localize damage to the contralateral parietal lobe and must be distinguished from peripheral nerve or spinal sensory loss (where basic sensation is impaired). Astereognosis, agraphesthesia and impaired two-point discrimination are quick, informative bedside tests of parietal cortical function. Tactile extinction, common after right parietal stroke, is part of the neglect syndrome and significantly affects safety and rehabilitation. Recognizing cortical sensory loss helps the clinical psychologist interpret a patient's difficulties and plan compensatory strategies.
Exam-ready summary: The parietal lobe contains the primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus, homuncular map) and association cortex integrating sensation. Parietal damage typically spares basic sensation but impairs cortical/discriminative functions: astereognosis (cannot recognize objects by touch), agraphesthesia (cannot identify figures written on skin), impaired two-point discrimination and point localization, and loss of position sense. Right parietal damage causes tactile extinction (ignoring simultaneous double touch on the affected side) — an attentional deficit. These localize to the contralateral parietal lobe and are distinguished from peripheral/spinal loss (which impairs basic sensation).

Conclusion

Sensory disturbances in parietal lobe syndrome are characteristically cortical — the patient feels stimuli but cannot recognize, localize or discriminate them, as in astereognosis, agraphesthesia and tactile extinction. This dissociation between preserved basic sensation and impaired higher sensory processing localizes damage to the parietal lobe and is important for the clinical psychologist in assessment, safety planning and rehabilitation.
