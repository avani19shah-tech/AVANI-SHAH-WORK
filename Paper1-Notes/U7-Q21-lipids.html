Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q21. Discuss the role of lipid messengers (endocannabinoids) in behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Not all chemical messengers in the brain are water-soluble amines or peptides; some are lipids (fats). The most important lipid-signalling system is the endocannabinoid system, discovered through research into how cannabis affects the brain. Endocannabinoids are naturally occurring lipid molecules that act on the same receptors as the active ingredient of cannabis (THC). They have a unique mode of action — they are retrograde messengers, released "backwards" from the receiving neuron to regulate the sending neuron — and they modulate appetite, pain, mood, memory and stress. This answer describes the endocannabinoid system, its receptors and unique signalling, its behavioural roles, and its clinical relevance.

1. The Endocannabinoids

The two main endogenous cannabinoids are anandamide (from the Sanskrit ananda, "bliss") and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG). Unlike classical transmitters, they are not stored in vesicles; they are synthesized "on demand" from membrane lipids when a neuron is active, and are broken down by enzymes (FAAH for anandamide).

2. Receptors

  • CB1 receptors — abundant in the brain (cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, cerebellum, hypothalamus); responsible for the behavioural and psychoactive effects.
  • CB2 receptors — mainly in immune cells and the periphery; involved in inflammation and immune function.

Both are metabotropic (G-protein-coupled) receptors, giving slow, modulatory effects.

3. Unique Retrograde Signalling

The defining feature of endocannabinoids is that they act as retrograde messengers: they are produced by the postsynaptic (receiving) neuron and travel backwards across the synapse to CB1 receptors on the presynaptic (sending) terminal, where they reduce the release of neurotransmitter. This provides a feedback brake — the receiving cell tells the sending cell to "turn down" — which regulates and protects synapses from over-activity.

4. Behavioural Roles

  • Appetite: stimulate feeding (the "munchies" of cannabis); the hypothalamic endocannabinoid system promotes eating and energy storage.
  • Pain: reduce pain perception (analgesia), acting at multiple levels of the pain pathway.
  • Mood and stress: modulate anxiety and stress responses; the system generally buffers stress, and its dysregulation is linked to anxiety and depression.
  • Memory: by dampening synapses, especially in the hippocampus, they contribute to the forgetting/extinction of memories — helping to erase unneeded or fearful memories (relevant to PTSD).
  • Movement and motor control: via the basal ganglia and cerebellum.
  • Reward: interact with the dopamine reward system, contributing to the pleasurable and addictive properties of cannabis.
Clinical relevance: The endocannabinoid system is a target of growing therapeutic interest. Cannabis/THC acts on CB1 receptors, producing euphoria, altered perception, increased appetite and memory impairment, and heavy use is linked to psychosis risk in vulnerable people. Cannabinoid-based medicines are used for chronic pain, chemotherapy-induced nausea and appetite loss (e.g., in cancer and HIV/AIDS), and spasticity in multiple sclerosis. The system's role in extinction learning has implications for treating PTSD and anxiety. Understanding it helps the clinical psychologist assess the effects of cannabis use and emerging cannabinoid therapies.
Exam-ready summary: The main lipid messengers are the endocannabinoids — anandamide and 2-AG — synthesized "on demand" from membrane lipids (not stored in vesicles) and broken down by enzymes (FAAH). They act on CB1 (brain) and CB2 (immune) receptors. Uniquely, they are retrograde messengers: made by the postsynaptic neuron, they travel backwards to reduce presynaptic transmitter release, providing feedback regulation. Behaviourally they modulate appetite (stimulate), pain (reduce), mood/stress (buffer), memory (promote forgetting/extinction), movement and reward. Clinically they mediate cannabis effects, are used for pain, nausea and appetite, and are relevant to PTSD and psychosis risk.

Conclusion

The endocannabinoid system represents a distinctive, lipid-based mode of neural communication with a unique retrograde mechanism that provides on-demand feedback regulation of synapses. Through this, it shapes appetite, pain, mood, memory and reward. Its discovery has opened new therapeutic avenues and clarified the effects of one of the world's most widely used psychoactive substances, making it an increasingly important system for the clinical psychologist to understand.
