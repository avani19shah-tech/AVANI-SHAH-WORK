Q13. Describe the action potential and its conduction (communication within a neuron).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The action potential is the rapid, brief electrical impulse — the "nerve impulse" — by which a neuron transmits information along its axon. It is a sudden reversal of the resting membrane potential, generated when the neuron is depolarized to a critical level called threshold. The action potential is the fundamental signal of the nervous system: it is how sensory information travels to the brain, how the brain sends commands to muscles, and how neurons communicate over long distances. This answer describes its ionic mechanism, its phases, its all-or-none nature, the refractory period, and how it is conducted along the axon.

1. Initiation — Reaching Threshold

Incoming excitatory signals (EPSPs) depolarize the neuron. If depolarization at the axon hillock reaches the threshold (≈ −55 mV), voltage-gated ion channels open and an action potential is triggered. Below threshold, nothing happens; at or above it, a full action potential always fires — this is the all-or-none principle.

2. The Phases of the Action Potential

  1. Depolarization (rising phase): Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ rushes into the cell down its electrochemical gradient. The inside rapidly becomes positive, reaching about +40 mV.
  2. Repolarization (falling phase): Na⁺ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ flows out of the cell, restoring the negative interior.
  3. Hyperpolarization (undershoot): K⁺ channels close slowly, so the membrane briefly becomes more negative than rest before returning to −70 mV. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump then restores the original ion distribution.

3. The All-or-None Principle

An action potential either occurs fully or not at all — its size does not vary with stimulus strength once threshold is reached. Therefore, stimulus intensity is coded not by the size of the impulse but by the frequency of firing (a strong stimulus produces more impulses per second) and by the number of neurons recruited.

4. The Refractory Period

  • Absolute refractory period: during the impulse (Na⁺ channels inactivated), no new action potential can be generated, whatever the stimulus. This ensures impulses are discrete and travel in one direction.
  • Relative refractory period: during hyperpolarization, a new impulse can occur only with a stronger-than-usual stimulus.

The refractory period limits the maximum firing rate and enforces one-way conduction.

5. Conduction Along the Axon

The action potential regenerates itself along the axon: the local current from one active region depolarizes the next region to threshold, so the impulse propagates without losing strength. Two modes:

  • Continuous conduction — in unmyelinated axons the impulse moves point-by-point (slower).
  • Saltatory conduction — in myelinated axons the myelin insulates the membrane, so the impulse "jumps" from one node of Ranvier to the next (where ion channels are concentrated). This is much faster and more energy-efficient.

Conduction velocity increases with axon diameter and the presence of myelin.

6. From Impulse to Communication

When the action potential reaches the axon terminal, it triggers Ca²⁺ entry and the release of neurotransmitter — converting the electrical signal into a chemical one at the synapse (communication between neurons). Thus the action potential is the link between intracellular electrical signalling and intercellular chemical signalling.

Clinical relevance: Disorders of the action potential and its conduction cause important clinical problems. Demyelination (multiple sclerosis) slows or blocks saltatory conduction, causing weakness, sensory loss and fatigue. Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels to abolish pain signals. Epilepsy involves abnormal, excessive neuronal firing. Some antiepileptic and mood-stabilizing drugs act by modulating Na⁺ channels. Understanding the impulse thus illuminates both disease and pharmacology.
Exam-ready summary: The action potential is an all-or-none electrical impulse triggered when depolarization reaches threshold (≈ −55 mV). Phases: (1) depolarization — voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ enters, inside → +40 mV; (2) repolarization — Na⁺ channels inactivate, K⁺ channels open, K⁺ exits; (3) hyperpolarization undershoot, then the Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores rest. The refractory period ensures one-way, discrete impulses and codes intensity by frequency. It is conducted continuously (unmyelinated) or by faster saltatory conduction jumping between nodes of Ranvier (myelinated). At the terminal it triggers neurotransmitter release.

Conclusion

The action potential is the elementary signal of the nervous system — a rapid, self-regenerating, all-or-none impulse produced by the sequential opening of sodium and potassium channels. Its speed, reliability and one-way conduction, especially the saltatory conduction made possible by myelin, allow information to travel accurately across the body. Its disorders (demyelination, epilepsy) and its pharmacological manipulation (anaesthetics, antiepileptics) make it a topic of direct clinical importance.
