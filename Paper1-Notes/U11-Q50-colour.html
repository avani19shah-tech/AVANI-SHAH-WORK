Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q50. Describe the disturbances of colour perception.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Colour perception is processed in specialized regions of the occipital and occipito-temporal cortex (notably area V4), which extract colour information from the visual scene, and in higher areas that link colours to knowledge and names. Damage to these regions produces disturbances of colour perception that are distinct from the inherited colour-blindness of the retina. These cerebral colour disorders — losing colour vision, being unable to recognize or name colours — are informative examples of how a specific visual attribute is processed by a dedicated brain region. This answer describes the neural basis of colour perception and the disorders that follow cortical damage.

1. The Neural Basis of Colour Perception

Colour vision begins in the retinal cones (three types tuned to different wavelengths) and is relayed to the primary visual cortex, then to the specialized colour area V4 in the ventral occipito-temporal cortex, which constructs the perception of colour and maintains colour constancy (seeing an object's colour as stable despite changing light). Higher areas link colours to memory and names. Damage at different levels produces different disorders.

2. Cerebral Achromatopsia

Cerebral achromatopsia is the loss of colour vision due to cortical (V4) damage — the world appears in shades of grey ("like a black-and-white film"), despite normal retinal cones. It may affect the whole visual field (bilateral damage) or half the field (hemiachromatopsia, unilateral damage). It is distinct from congenital (retinal) colour-blindness.

3. Colour Agnosia and Colour Anomia

  • Colour agnosia — the patient can perceive colours (e.g., match them) but cannot recognize or associate them with objects (e.g., cannot say a banana is yellow) — a disorder of colour knowledge.
  • Colour anomia — the patient can perceive and match colours but cannot name them, or cannot point to a named colour — a language/disconnection problem, often part of a disconnection syndrome (e.g., pure alexia with colour anomia from left occipital + splenial lesions).

4. Colour Constancy Deficits

V4 damage can impair colour constancy, so an object's perceived colour changes abnormally with lighting — the brain fails to correct for the illumination.

Clinical relevance: Cortical colour disorders localize damage to the ventral occipito-temporal cortex and, when combined with other signs (e.g., prosopagnosia, alexia), help define syndromes and disconnection patterns. They must be distinguished from congenital (retinal) colour-blindness by history and testing. Though less disabling than many deficits, they can affect daily tasks and, when part of a disconnection syndrome (e.g., pure alexia with colour anomia), point to specific lesion combinations that the clinical psychologist and neurologist need to recognize.
Exam-ready summary: Colour is processed by area V4 in the ventral occipito-temporal cortex (maintaining colour constancy) and by higher areas linking colour to knowledge/names. Disorders: cerebral achromatopsia (loss of colour vision from V4 damage — world appears grey; may be hemiachromatopsia), distinct from congenital retinal colour-blindness; colour agnosia (perceives but cannot recognize/associate colours with objects); colour anomia (perceives and recognizes but cannot name colours — a disconnection/language problem, e.g., with pure alexia); and colour constancy deficits. Distinguishing perception vs recognition vs naming localizes the disorder.

Conclusion

Disturbances of colour perception demonstrate that colour is processed by a dedicated cortical region (V4) and can be selectively lost, unrecognized or unnameable — producing achromatopsia, colour agnosia and colour anomia respectively. These cortical disorders, distinct from congenital colour-blindness, localize damage to the ventral occipito-temporal cortex and, especially within disconnection syndromes, are useful to the clinical psychologist in understanding the organization of vision.
