Q14. Explain communication between neurons through neurotransmitters (synaptic transmission).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

While communication within a neuron is electrical (the action potential), communication between neurons is predominantly chemical, occurring at specialized junctions called synapses through the release of neurotransmitters. This chemical mode of communication allows the nervous system flexibility, integration and modulation — properties that a purely electrical system could not provide. Synaptic transmission is also the site of action of virtually every psychiatric drug. This answer describes the structure of the synapse, the step-by-step process of chemical transmission, the types of postsynaptic responses, the criteria and classification of neurotransmitters, and how transmission is terminated.

1. Structure of the Synapse

A chemical synapse has three parts:

  • Presynaptic terminal — the axon ending, containing synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter.
  • Synaptic cleft — a narrow gap (~20 nm) separating the two neurons.
  • Postsynaptic membrane — the receiving surface (usually a dendritic spine), studded with receptors.

2. The Process of Chemical Transmission (step by step)

  1. The action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal.
  2. It opens voltage-gated calcium (Ca²⁺) channels; Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.
  3. Ca²⁺ causes synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter into the cleft by exocytosis.
  4. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  5. Receptor binding opens ion channels, changing the postsynaptic membrane potential (a postsynaptic potential).
  6. The signal is terminated so it does not act indefinitely.

3. Postsynaptic Responses and Receptor Types

The effect depends on the receptor:

  • Excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP): depolarizes the postsynaptic cell (e.g., Na⁺ in), making it more likely to fire.
  • Inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP): hyperpolarizes the cell (e.g., Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out), making it less likely to fire.

Receptors are of two kinds:

  • Ionotropic (direct): the receptor is an ion channel — fast, brief effect (e.g., nicotinic ACh, GABA_A, glutamate AMPA/NMDA).
  • Metabotropic (indirect): the receptor activates a G-protein and second-messenger cascade — slower, longer, modulatory effect (e.g., muscarinic ACh, most dopamine and serotonin receptors).

4. Integration — Summation

A neuron receives thousands of EPSPs and IPSPs. It integrates them:

  • Spatial summation — inputs from many synapses at the same time.
  • Temporal summation — inputs from one synapse in rapid succession.

If the net depolarization at the axon hillock reaches threshold, the neuron fires an action potential. Thus the neuron acts as a tiny decision-making unit.

5. Neurotransmitter Criteria and Classification

A substance qualifies as a neurotransmitter if it is synthesized in the neuron, stored in vesicles, released on stimulation, binds specific receptors, and is inactivated. Major classes: acetylcholine; monoamines (dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin); amino acids (glutamate — excitatory; GABA, glycine — inhibitory); peptides (endorphins, substance P); and gases/lipids (nitric oxide, endocannabinoids).

6. Termination of Transmission

To allow crisp signalling, transmitter action is stopped by:

  • Reuptake — transporters carry the transmitter back into the presynaptic terminal (e.g., serotonin, dopamine) — the target of SSRIs.
  • Enzymatic degradation — enzymes break it down (e.g., acetylcholinesterase for ACh; MAO for monoamines).
  • Diffusion away from the cleft.
Clinical & pharmacological relevance: The synapse is the target of nearly all psychotropic drugs — SSRIs block serotonin reuptake, MAO inhibitors block breakdown, benzodiazepines enhance GABA (IPSP), antipsychotics block dopamine receptors, and cholinesterase inhibitors prolong ACh in Alzheimer's disease. Synaptic dysfunction is implicated in depression, schizophrenia and anxiety. Understanding synaptic transmission is therefore indispensable to the clinical psychologist's grasp of drug effects and the biology of disorders.
Exam-ready summary: Neurons communicate chemically at synapses (presynaptic terminal, cleft, postsynaptic membrane). Sequence: action potential → Ca²⁺ influx → vesicle exocytosis → transmitter diffuses → binds postsynaptic receptors → produces an EPSP (excitatory) or IPSP (inhibitory) → the cell integrates inputs by spatial/temporal summation → fires if threshold is reached → transmission is terminated by reuptake, enzymatic degradation or diffusion. Receptors are ionotropic (fast, direct) or metabotropic (slow, G-protein). This synapse is the site of action of virtually all psychiatric drugs.

Conclusion

Synaptic transmission converts the neuron's electrical impulse into a chemical message and back again, giving the nervous system its flexibility, integrative power and susceptibility to modulation. Because the synapse is where drugs act and where many disorders originate, mastery of chemical neurotransmission is one of the most clinically relevant topics in the biological foundations of behaviour.
