Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 1 · Anatomy of the Brain

Q2. Describe the surface anatomy of the cortical regions of the brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The cerebral cortex is the outer 2–4 mm sheet of grey matter covering the cerebral hemispheres. It is the most recently evolved and most functionally sophisticated part of the brain, responsible for perception, voluntary movement, language, memory and abstract thought. Its surface anatomy — the pattern of folds and the division into lobes — provides the essential map that clinicians use to localize function and lesion. This answer describes the folded structure of the cortex, its major landmarks (sulci and fissures), the four lobes and the insula, and the key functional areas on the cortical surface.

1. The Folded Structure: Gyri and Sulci

The cortex is highly convoluted to pack a large surface area (about 2200 cm²) into the limited cranial cavity — roughly two-thirds of the cortex is hidden within its folds. The raised ridges are called gyri (singular gyrus), the shallow grooves between them are sulci, and the deepest grooves are fissures. This folding is highly consistent between individuals, allowing named landmarks to be identified reliably.

2. Major Landmarks (Fissures and Sulci)

  • Longitudinal (interhemispheric) fissure: the deep midline groove separating the two hemispheres; at its floor lies the corpus callosum.
  • Central sulcus (fissure of Rolando): runs down the lateral surface and separates the frontal lobe (in front) from the parietal lobe (behind). It is the boundary between the motor and sensory strips.
  • Lateral (Sylvian) fissure: a prominent groove that separates the temporal lobe below from the frontal and parietal lobes above; the insula is buried within it.
  • Parieto-occipital sulcus: on the medial surface, separating parietal from occipital lobe.

3. The Four Lobes and the Insula

(a) Frontal lobe

Anterior to the central sulcus and above the lateral fissure. Its surface carries the precentral gyrus (primary motor cortex), the premotor and supplementary motor areas, Broca's area (in the dominant hemisphere, for speech production), the frontal eye fields, and the large prefrontal cortex for executive function and personality.

(b) Parietal lobe

Behind the central sulcus. Its surface carries the postcentral gyrus (primary somatosensory cortex), and association cortex in the superior and inferior parietal lobules (including the supramarginal and angular gyri) that support spatial processing, body schema and, in the dominant hemisphere, aspects of language and calculation.

(c) Temporal lobe

Below the lateral fissure. Its superior surface carries Heschl's gyrus (primary auditory cortex) and, posteriorly, Wernicke's area (language comprehension). The inferior/medial surface is concerned with visual recognition and memory.

(d) Occipital lobe

At the posterior pole. It contains the primary visual cortex around the calcarine sulcus (on the medial surface) and visual association areas.

(e) Insula

A fifth "hidden" cortical region deep within the lateral fissure, covered by opercula of the surrounding lobes. It is involved in interoception, taste, autonomic regulation and emotional awareness.

4. Functional Surface Map — Primary Areas

Between these primary areas lie vast association cortices that integrate information for perception, language, memory and reasoning — these make up most of the human cortical surface and account for our cognitive sophistication.

Clinical relevance: Because the surface map is consistent, a neuropsychologist can predict the deficit from the affected gyrus — e.g., a lesion of the precentral gyrus causes contralateral weakness, of Broca's area causes non-fluent aphasia, and of the calcarine cortex causes a visual field defect. Surface landmarks also guide neurosurgery and the interpretation of imaging.
Exam-ready summary: The cortex is a folded sheet of gyri (ridges), sulci (grooves) and fissures. The longitudinal fissure divides the hemispheres; the central sulcus separates frontal from parietal; the lateral fissure marks off the temporal lobe. The four lobes (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital) plus the buried insula carry the primary motor (precentral), somatosensory (postcentral), auditory (Heschl's) and visual (calcarine) areas, plus Broca's and Wernicke's language areas, all surrounded by integrative association cortex.

Conclusion

The surface anatomy of the cortex is the clinician's essential atlas: its folds increase processing capacity, its landmarks define the lobes, and its primary and association areas localize function. Mastery of this surface map is the first step in localizing lesions and in interpreting the neuropsychological consequences of cortical damage.
