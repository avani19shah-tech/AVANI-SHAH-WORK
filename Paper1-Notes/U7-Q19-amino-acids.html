Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q19. Discuss the role of amino acid neurotransmitters (glutamate, GABA, glycine) in behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The amino acid neurotransmitters are the brain's fast, "workhorse" chemicals, present at the great majority of synapses in the central nervous system. Whereas the monoamines and acetylcholine modulate broad states, the amino acids carry out the moment-to-moment excitation and inhibition on which all neural computation depends. The principal excitatory transmitter is glutamate, the principal inhibitory transmitter is GABA, and glycine provides inhibition in the spinal cord and brainstem. The balance between glutamate and GABA determines overall brain excitability. This answer discusses each amino acid transmitter, its receptors and behavioural roles, and their clinical importance.

1. Glutamate — the Main Excitatory Transmitter

Glutamate is the most abundant excitatory transmitter, used at the majority of fast excitatory synapses. It acts on several receptors:

  • AMPA and kainate receptors — ionotropic, mediate fast excitation (Na⁺ influx).
  • NMDA receptor — ionotropic but special: it is a coincidence detector requiring both glutamate binding and prior depolarization to open (removing a Mg²⁺ block), admitting Ca²⁺. This is the key to synaptic plasticity.
  • Metabotropic glutamate receptors — slower, modulatory.

Behavioural role: glutamate underlies learning and memory (through Long-Term Potentiation, LTP, at NMDA receptors), perception, cognition and movement. Its excess, however, causes excitotoxicity — over-excitation floods cells with Ca²⁺ and kills them, contributing to neuronal death in stroke, epilepsy and neurodegeneration.

2. GABA — the Main Inhibitory Transmitter

GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid), made from glutamate, is the principal inhibitory transmitter in the brain. It acts on:

  • GABA_A receptors — ionotropic, open Cl⁻ channels to hyperpolarize (inhibit) the neuron; the site of action of benzodiazepines, barbiturates and alcohol.
  • GABA_B receptors — metabotropic, slower inhibition.

Behavioural role: GABA dampens neural excitability, producing calming, anxiolytic, sedative and anticonvulsant effects. Adequate GABA function prevents runaway excitation (seizures) and controls anxiety.

3. Glycine

Glycine is an inhibitory transmitter mainly in the spinal cord and brainstem, where it opens Cl⁻ channels to inhibit motor neurons and regulate reflexes. (It also acts as a required co-agonist at the NMDA receptor, contributing to excitation there.) Blockade of glycine (e.g., by strychnine) causes uncontrolled muscle spasms.

4. The Excitation–Inhibition Balance

The overall excitability of the brain depends on the balance between glutamate (excitation) and GABA (inhibition). Too much excitation (or too little inhibition) leads to seizures and excitotoxicity; too much inhibition leads to sedation and unconsciousness. Maintaining this balance is essential for normal function.

Clinical relevance:
  • Anxiety & epilepsy: reduced GABA function is linked to both; benzodiazepines and many antiepileptics work by enhancing GABA.
  • Alcohol: enhances GABA and inhibits glutamate/NMDA — explaining sedation, and withdrawal seizures when GABA function rebounds.
  • Schizophrenia: the glutamate hypothesis proposes NMDA-receptor hypofunction; drugs that block NMDA (ketamine, PCP) produce psychosis-like states.
  • Stroke & neurodegeneration: glutamate excitotoxicity contributes to cell death.
  • Rapid-acting antidepressants: ketamine (an NMDA modulator) has revealed a role for glutamate in mood.
Exam-ready summary: The amino acids are the brain's fast transmitters. Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter (AMPA, kainate, and the coincidence-detecting NMDA receptor); it underlies learning/memory (LTP) but in excess causes excitotoxicity. GABA (made from glutamate) is the main inhibitory transmitter (GABA_A → Cl⁻ influx), producing calming, anxiolytic, sedative, anticonvulsant effects — the target of benzodiazepines, alcohol and antiepileptics. Glycine inhibits in the spinal cord/brainstem and co-activates NMDA. Overall brain excitability reflects the glutamate–GABA balance; its disturbance underlies epilepsy, anxiety, and (via NMDA hypofunction) schizophrenia.

Conclusion

The amino acid transmitters glutamate and GABA are the fundamental excitatory and inhibitory forces of the brain, with glycine adding inhibition in the lower CNS. Their balance governs everything from a single computation to overall consciousness, and their disturbance underlies epilepsy, anxiety and psychosis. Because they are the target of anxiolytics, antiepileptics and emerging antidepressants, the amino acids are of major clinical importance to the psychologist.
