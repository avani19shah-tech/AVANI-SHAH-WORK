Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q47. Describe the agnosias.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Agnosia (from the Greek for "not knowing") is the inability to recognize or identify stimuli despite intact basic sensation, attention, intelligence and language. The patient perceives the stimulus but cannot attach meaning to it — famously described as "perception stripped of its meaning." Agnosias are modality-specific: a patient may fail to recognize an object by sight yet recognize it by touch or sound. They result from damage to the association cortex of the parietal, temporal and occipital lobes, where sensory information is integrated and linked to knowledge. This answer defines agnosia, distinguishes it from related disorders, and describes the major types across the sensory modalities.

1. Definition and Distinctions

Agnosia is a failure of recognition not explained by:

  • Sensory loss — the patient can see, hear or feel the stimulus.
  • Attention deficit — the patient attends to it.
  • Naming/language problem (anomia) — the patient cannot recognize it even non-verbally.
  • General intellectual decline.

It is thus a specific disorder of linking perception to meaning, from association-cortex damage.

2. Visual Agnosias

  • Apperceptive visual agnosia — failure to form a coherent whole percept; cannot copy or match shapes (a problem of perception–integration).
  • Associative visual agnosia — the percept is formed (can copy the object) but cannot be linked to meaning; "sees but does not recognize."
  • Prosopagnosia — selective inability to recognize faces (fusiform face area, right/bilateral occipito-temporal).
  • Achromatopsia / colour agnosia — inability to perceive or recognize colours.
  • Simultanagnosia — inability to perceive more than one object at a time (part of Balint's syndrome, bilateral parieto-occipital).

3. Auditory Agnosias

  • Auditory agnosia — cannot recognize the meaning of non-verbal sounds (e.g., a dog barking) despite intact hearing.
  • Pure word deafness — cannot comprehend spoken words though reading and own speech are intact.
  • Amusia — cannot recognize music/melodies (right temporal).

4. Tactile Agnosia

Astereognosis (tactile agnosia) — inability to recognize objects by touch despite intact basic sensation (parietal association cortex).

5. Body-Related Agnosias

  • Autotopagnosia — cannot localize/name parts of one's own body.
  • Finger agnosia — cannot identify individual fingers (part of Gerstmann's syndrome).
  • Anosognosia — unawareness/denial of one's own deficit.

Clinical relevance: Agnosias are striking dissociations that must be distinguished from sensory loss, naming problems and dementia through careful, modality-specific testing (e.g., asking the patient to recognize vs name vs copy). They localize damage to specific association areas and significantly affect daily functioning and safety (e.g., prosopagnosia impairs social recognition; astereognosis impairs handling objects). They occur in stroke, tumours, trauma and degenerative disease, and guide compensatory rehabilitation strategies.
Exam-ready summary: Agnosia = failure to recognize stimuli despite intact sensation, attention, intelligence and language ("perception stripped of meaning"), from association-cortex damage. Visual: apperceptive (cannot form percept) vs associative (percept without meaning), prosopagnosia (faces), achromatopsia (colour), simultanagnosia (one object at a time). Auditory: auditory agnosia, pure word deafness, amusia (temporal). Tactile: astereognosis (parietal). Body: autotopagnosia, finger agnosia, anosognosia (parietal, esp. right). They are modality-specific dissociations that localize lesions and must be distinguished from sensory loss and anomia.

Conclusion

The agnosias reveal that recognition is a distinct step beyond sensation, carried out by the association cortex, and that it can fail selectively for one modality while others are spared. These remarkable disorders — from prosopagnosia to astereognosis — localize damage to specific brain regions and require careful, dissociation-based assessment by the clinical psychologist to distinguish them from sensory, language and intellectual impairments.
