Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 1 · Anatomy of the Brain

Q4. Explain the anatomical connectivity among the various brain regions.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The brain is not a collection of isolated centres but an integrated network in which regions constantly communicate. This communication depends on white matter — the myelinated axons that appear white and make up the interior of the cerebrum. These axons are organized into three great classes of fibre tracts: commissural fibres linking the two hemispheres, association fibres linking regions within one hemisphere, and projection fibres linking the cortex with sub-cortical and spinal structures. Understanding this "wiring diagram" is crucial, because many neuropsychological syndromes result not from damage to a centre but from damage to the connections between centres — the so-called disconnection syndromes.

1. Commissural Fibres — Connecting the Two Hemispheres

Commissural fibres cross the midline to connect corresponding areas of the left and right hemispheres, allowing them to share information and coordinate.

  • Corpus callosum — the largest commissure (~200 million fibres), a broad band with four parts (rostrum, genu, body, splenium) connecting most of the cortex.
  • Anterior commissure — connects parts of the temporal lobes and olfactory regions.
  • Posterior commissure and hippocampal commissure — smaller connections.

Sectioning the corpus callosum ("split-brain" operation, done for severe epilepsy) reveals that the disconnected hemispheres can process information independently — the foundation of research on hemispheric specialization.

2. Association Fibres — Connecting Regions Within a Hemisphere

Association fibres link areas within the same hemisphere and are essential for integrating information across cortical regions. They are of two lengths:

  • Short association (U/arcuate) fibres — connect adjacent gyri.
  • Long association fibres — connect distant lobes:
    • Superior longitudinal / arcuate fasciculus — connects Broca's and Wernicke's areas; essential for repeating speech. Its damage causes conduction aphasia.
    • Uncinate fasciculus — links frontal and temporal lobes (emotion–cognition integration).
    • Cingulum — runs within the cingulate gyrus, part of the limbic circuit.
    • Inferior longitudinal fasciculus — links occipital and temporal lobes (visual recognition).

3. Projection Fibres — Connecting Cortex with Lower Structures

Projection fibres run vertically between the cortex and sub-cortical structures, brainstem and spinal cord. They carry motor commands down (efferent/descending, e.g., the corticospinal tract) and sensory information up (afferent/ascending, e.g., the thalamocortical radiations). These fibres funnel through the compact internal capsule between the thalamus and basal ganglia — a region where even a small lacunar stroke can produce a dense hemiplegia because so many fibres are packed together.

4. The Network / Connectome View

Modern neuroscience, aided by diffusion tensor imaging (DTI/tractography), views the brain as a connectome — a network of nodes (regions) and edges (tracts) organized into functional networks such as the default-mode network, the salience network and the executive-control network. This confirms that complex functions like language, memory and attention are distributed across connected regions rather than housed in single spots.

Clinical relevance — disconnection syndromes: Damage to connecting tracts, sparing the "centres," still causes deficits. Examples: conduction aphasia (arcuate fasciculus — fluent speech and good comprehension but poor repetition); alexia without agraphia (splenium + left occipital lesion — can write but cannot read); and split-brain phenomena. Recognizing these teaches that a symptom may reflect a broken connection, not a damaged region — vital for accurate localization.
Exam-ready summary: Brain regions communicate via white-matter tracts of three types: commissural (corpus callosum, anterior/posterior commissures — between hemispheres), association (short U-fibres and long tracts like the arcuate, uncinate, cingulum, inferior longitudinal — within a hemisphere), and projection (corticospinal, thalamocortical — cortex ↔ subcortex/cord, funnelling through the internal capsule). This wiring makes the brain a distributed network (connectome); damage to the connections produces disconnection syndromes such as conduction aphasia and alexia without agraphia.

Conclusion

Anatomical connectivity transforms discrete brain regions into a unified processing system. The three fibre classes — commissural, association and projection — carry information across, within and beyond the hemispheres. Appreciating this connectivity explains why the brain functions as an integrated network and why disconnecting its pathways produces distinctive clinical syndromes that the neuropsychologist must recognize.
