Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q51. Describe the disturbances of writing ability (agraphia).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Agraphia is the acquired loss or impairment of the ability to write, caused by brain damage, in a person who was previously literate. Writing is a complex skill that requires linguistic knowledge (spelling), the motor programs to form letters, and spatial organization on the page — so writing can break down in several different ways depending on which component is damaged. Writing is closely linked to language, and its centres lie mainly in the dominant (left) parietal lobe (especially the angular gyrus) and frontal regions. This answer describes the components of writing, the types of agraphia, and their clinical significance.

1. The Components of Writing

Writing requires: (a) linguistic processing — retrieving the correct spelling of words; (b) motor programming — the learned movements to form letters (graphomotor); and (c) spatial organization — arranging letters and words correctly on the page. Damage to any component produces a different form of agraphia.

2. Types of Agraphia

(a) Pure agraphia

Isolated impairment of writing with relatively preserved reading, speech and comprehension — rare; linked to lesions in the dominant frontal (Exner's area) or parietal region.

(b) Aphasic (linguistic) agraphia

Writing disturbance accompanying aphasia, reflecting the underlying language disorder — e.g., writing is non-fluent and effortful in Broca's aphasia, or fluent but with spelling errors and jargon in Wernicke's aphasia. Two linguistic subtypes parallel the reading routes:

  • Surface (lexical) agraphia — can spell regular words phonetically but misspells irregular words (writes them as they sound).
  • Phonological agraphia — can spell familiar words but cannot spell unfamiliar words or non-words (cannot use sound-to-letter rules).

(c) Apraxic agraphia

Impaired letter formation due to loss of the motor programs for writing — letters are poorly formed, distorted or illegible, despite intact spelling knowledge and normal strength. It reflects a form of apraxia (parietal/premotor).

(d) Spatial (constructional) agraphia

Impaired spatial layout of writing — abnormal spacing, sloping lines, writing on only one side of the page, and repeated strokes. It is common with right parietal damage and often accompanies neglect.

3. Agraphia in Gerstmann's Syndrome

Agraphia is one component of Gerstmann's syndrome (from dominant angular gyrus damage), together with acalculia, finger agnosia and right–left disorientation — a useful localizing cluster.

4. Assessment

Writing is assessed by asking the patient to write spontaneously, to dictation, and by copying; and by comparing writing of regular vs irregular words and non-words. Analysing the type of error (spelling vs letter formation vs spatial arrangement) identifies the subtype and localizes the lesion.

Clinical relevance: Because writing depends on language, motor and spatial systems, the pattern of agraphia helps localize damage (e.g., spatial agraphia → right parietal/neglect; agraphia + acalculia + finger agnosia + right–left disorientation → dominant angular gyrus/Gerstmann's). Agraphia impairs communication and daily function and often accompanies aphasia or neglect. The clinical psychologist assesses writing as part of the language and neuropsychological examination and to guide rehabilitation.
Exam-ready summary: Agraphia is acquired impairment of writing (in a previously literate person). Writing needs linguistic (spelling), motor (letter formation) and spatial (layout) components. Types: pure agraphia (isolated); aphasic/linguistic agraphia (with aphasia — subtypes: surface/lexical = misspells irregular words; phonological = cannot spell non-words); apraxic agraphia (poorly formed letters, intact spelling — parietal/premotor); and spatial/constructional agraphia (abnormal layout — right parietal, with neglect). Agraphia is part of Gerstmann's syndrome (with acalculia, finger agnosia, right–left disorientation — dominant angular gyrus). Error analysis localizes the lesion.

Conclusion

Agraphia demonstrates that writing is a composite skill that can fail at the linguistic, motor or spatial level, each producing a characteristic error pattern that localizes the underlying damage. Whether occurring purely, with aphasia, as apraxic or spatial agraphia, or within Gerstmann's syndrome, careful analysis of writing errors is a valuable localizing and functional tool for the clinical psychologist.
