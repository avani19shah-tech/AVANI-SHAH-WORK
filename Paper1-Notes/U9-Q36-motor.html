Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 9 · Frontal Lobe Syndrome

Q36. Describe the disturbances of motor functions in frontal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The frontal lobe is the motor lobe of the brain: it contains the primary motor cortex, the premotor and supplementary motor areas, and the prefrontal regions that plan and regulate action. Its damage therefore produces a range of motor disturbances — from simple paralysis to complex disorders of the sequencing, initiation and control of movement, and the re-emergence of primitive reflexes. These signs are important both as localizing features and because they reflect the frontal lobe's role in translating intention into organized action. This answer describes the motor disturbances of frontal lobe syndrome from the elementary to the complex.

1. Contralateral Weakness / Paralysis

Damage to the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus, BA 4) causes contralateral hemiparesis or hemiplegia — weakness or paralysis of the opposite side of the body, because the corticospinal tract crosses. The distribution follows the motor homunculus (e.g., a lesion of the medial motor strip weakens the leg — as in ACA stroke). There may be upper-motor-neuron signs (spasticity, brisk reflexes, extensor plantar response).

2. Loss of Fine Motor Skill and Coordination

Damage to the premotor and supplementary motor areas impairs the planning and coordination of complex, skilled movements, reducing dexterity and the smooth execution of learned motor sequences.

3. Motor Perseveration and Impaired Sequencing

A hallmark of frontal damage is difficulty switching between and sequencing movements:

  • Motor perseveration — inability to stop a movement or to shift to a new one; the patient repeats the same action.
  • Failure on Luria's motor sequencing tasks — e.g., the "fist–edge–palm" (Luria three-step) test and alternating hand-movement sequences, which frontal patients cannot perform smoothly.

4. Apraxia

Apraxia is the inability to carry out learned, skilled, purposeful movements despite intact strength, sensation and comprehension. The patient cannot perform a gesture on command (e.g., "show me how to wave" or "pretend to use a comb") though they may do it automatically. Frontal/premotor damage contributes to apraxia (ideomotor type), reflecting a disorder of motor programming rather than execution.

5. Primitive (Frontal Release) Reflexes

Frontal damage releases reflexes normally suppressed after infancy — the frontal release signs:

  • Grasp reflex — involuntary grasping of an object placed in the palm.
  • Snout and sucking reflexes — pouting/sucking to perioral stimulation.
  • Palmomental reflex — chin muscle twitch on stroking the palm.
  • Rooting reflex.

Their reappearance signals frontal lobe pathology.

6. Disorders of Gait and Gaze

  • Frontal gait apraxia ("magnetic gait") — difficulty initiating walking, with short, shuffling, hesitant steps and feet seeming stuck to the floor, despite normal leg strength (seen in normal-pressure hydrocephalus and frontal disease).
  • Gaze disturbances — damage to the frontal eye fields impairs voluntary eye movements toward the opposite side (the eyes may deviate toward the lesion acutely).
  • Urinary incontinence — with medial frontal damage, from loss of cortical control over bladder function.
Clinical relevance: Motor signs help localize and confirm frontal pathology. Contralateral weakness with a certain distribution points to the motor strip; frontal release signs and gait apraxia point to diffuse or medial frontal disease (e.g., normal-pressure hydrocephalus, frontotemporal dementia); perseveration and apraxia on Luria's tasks are quick bedside indicators of frontal dysfunction. These deficits guide rehabilitation (physiotherapy for weakness, occupational therapy for apraxia and dexterity) and inform prognosis.
Exam-ready summary: Frontal motor disturbances run from simple to complex: (1) contralateral weakness/paralysis with upper-motor-neuron signs from primary motor cortex damage (homuncular distribution); (2) loss of fine skill and coordination (premotor/SMA); (3) motor perseveration and impaired sequencing (Luria fist–edge–palm test); (4) apraxia — inability to perform learned skilled movements despite intact strength; (5) frontal release signs (grasp, snout, sucking, palmomental reflexes); and (6) gait apraxia, gaze palsies (frontal eye fields) and urinary incontinence (medial frontal). These localize and confirm frontal pathology and guide rehabilitation.

Conclusion

Motor disturbances in frontal lobe syndrome span the whole hierarchy of movement — from the paralysis of primary motor cortex damage, through the apraxia and perseveration of premotor damage, to the release of primitive reflexes and disorders of gait and gaze. Together they reflect the frontal lobe's central role in converting intention into organized action, and they provide the clinical psychologist and neurologist with valuable localizing signs and clear targets for rehabilitation.
