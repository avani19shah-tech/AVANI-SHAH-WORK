Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 1 · Anatomy of the Brain

Q3. Describe the interior structures of the cortical and sub-cortical regions of the brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Beneath the folded cortical surface lies a wealth of interior (deep) structures — nuclei of grey matter, white-matter tracts and the ventricular spaces — that are essential to movement, emotion, memory and homeostasis. While the cortex handles conscious perception and higher cognition, these sub-cortical structures act as relays, filters, modulators and generators of behaviour. This answer describes the interior organization of the cerebrum, then the principal sub-cortical structures: the basal ganglia, the limbic system, the diencephalon (thalamus and hypothalamus), and the deep white matter.

1. Interior of the Cortex — Grey and White Matter

A section through the cerebrum shows an outer rim of grey matter (the cortex — neuronal cell bodies, appearing grey) and an inner mass of white matter (myelinated axons, appearing white because of the fatty myelin). The white matter carries the connecting tracts described in a later question. Embedded within the white matter are islands of deep grey matter — the sub-cortical nuclei.

2. The Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia are a group of interconnected deep nuclei that regulate the initiation and selection of voluntary movement and contribute to habit learning and reward. They comprise:

  • Caudate nucleus and putamen (together the striatum) — the main input structures.
  • Globus pallidus — the main output structure.
  • Functionally linked: the substantia nigra (dopamine source) and subthalamic nucleus.

They operate through cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical loops that facilitate desired movements and inhibit unwanted ones.

3. The Limbic System

The limbic system is a ring of interconnected structures deep to the cortex that governs emotion, motivation and memory:

  • Hippocampus — consolidation of new declarative (episodic) memories.
  • Amygdala — emotional processing, especially fear and threat evaluation.
  • Cingulate gyrus — attention, emotion regulation, pain.
  • Fornix and mammillary bodies — memory circuit (Papez circuit).
  • Parts of the hypothalamus and septal nuclei.

4. The Diencephalon

(a) Thalamus

A large paired egg-shaped mass at the centre of the brain, the thalamus is the great relay station: virtually all sensory information (except smell) synapses here before being directed to the appropriate cortical area. It also relays motor signals and participates in arousal and consciousness.

(b) Hypothalamus

A small but vital structure below the thalamus, the hypothalamus is the master regulator of homeostasis — controlling temperature, hunger, thirst, circadian rhythms, and the "four Fs" (feeding, fighting, fleeing, mating). It commands the autonomic nervous system and, through the pituitary gland, the endocrine system, thereby linking brain and body.

(c) Epithalamus and subthalamus

The epithalamus contains the pineal gland (melatonin, sleep–wake rhythm); the subthalamus contributes to motor control.

5. Deep White Matter and Ventricles

The internal capsule is a compact band of projection fibres between the basal ganglia and thalamus carrying motor and sensory tracts to and from the cortex — a strategically important region where a small lesion causes major deficits. The ventricles (paired lateral, third and fourth) are the interior CSF-filled cavities.

Clinical relevance: Basal ganglia disease produces movement disorders with psychiatric features — Parkinson's (nigral loss → bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, depression) and Huntington's (caudate atrophy → chorea, cognitive/psychiatric decline). Hippocampal damage causes amnesia; amygdala damage blunts fear; hypothalamic lesions disrupt appetite, temperature and hormones. These structures are therefore central to clinical neuropsychology.
Exam-ready summary: Interior to the cortex, grey-matter nuclei sit within white matter. The basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, + substantia nigra and subthalamic nucleus) regulate movement selection. The limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate, fornix, mammillary bodies) governs memory and emotion. The diencephalon provides the thalamus (sensory/motor relay), hypothalamus (homeostasis and endocrine control), epithalamus (pineal) and subthalamus. The internal capsule carries projection fibres, and the ventricles hold CSF.

Conclusion

The interior structures of the brain form the machinery beneath conscious experience — relaying information (thalamus), maintaining the internal milieu (hypothalamus), enabling smooth movement (basal ganglia), and storing memories and generating emotions (limbic system). Their dysfunction produces some of the most important neuropsychiatric syndromes, making them essential knowledge for the clinical psychologist.
