Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q18. Discuss the role of the monoamines (dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin) in behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The monoamines are a family of neurotransmitters that share a single amine group and are among the most behaviourally and clinically important chemicals in the brain. They fall into two groups: the catecholamines — dopamine, noradrenaline and adrenaline (all synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine) — and the indoleamine serotonin (synthesized from tryptophan). Each is produced by a small cluster of neurons in the brainstem that projects diffusely throughout the brain, acting largely as a modulator of mood, arousal, reward and movement. Because psychiatric drugs overwhelmingly target these systems, the monoamines are central to biological psychiatry. This answer discusses their synthesis, pathways, behavioural roles and clinical significance.

1. Synthesis and Inactivation

Catecholamines are made along the pathway: tyrosine → L-DOPA → dopamine → noradrenaline → adrenaline. Serotonin is made from tryptophan → 5-HTP → serotonin (5-HT). All monoamines are inactivated by reuptake into the terminal and by the enzymes monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) — the targets of MAO-inhibitor antidepressants.

2. Dopamine (DA)

Arises from the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (VTA). Four pathways:

  • Mesolimbic — reward, pleasure, motivation; overactivity → positive symptoms of psychosis; central to addiction.
  • Mesocortical — cognition, motivation; underactivity → negative symptoms of schizophrenia.
  • Nigrostriatal — movement control; degeneration → Parkinson's disease.
  • Tuberoinfundibular — inhibits prolactin release.

Behavioural role: reward and reinforcement, motivation ("wanting"), voluntary movement, and executive/cognitive drive.

3. Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine, NA)

Arises mainly from the locus coeruleus in the pons.

Behavioural role: arousal, alertness and vigilance; the central "fight-or-flight" response; attention and concentration; and mood. Underactivity is linked to depression and inattention (a target of SNRIs and atomoxetine); overactivity to anxiety, panic and hyperarousal in PTSD.

4. Serotonin (5-HT)

Arises from the raphe nuclei of the brainstem and projects very widely.

Behavioural role: regulation of mood, sleep, appetite, impulse control, aggression and sexual behaviour. Low serotonergic function is associated with depression, anxiety, obsessive–compulsive disorder, impulsivity, aggression and suicide — hence the widespread use of SSRIs.

Clinical relevance: The monoamine hypothesis of depression proposes a functional deficit of serotonin and noradrenaline — the basis for SSRIs, SNRIs and MAO inhibitors. The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia proposes mesolimbic dopamine excess — the basis for D2-blocking antipsychotics (whose nigrostriatal blockade also causes Parkinsonian side-effects). Dopamine's role in reward underlies all addictive drugs. Understanding the monoamines therefore explains most of psychopharmacology.
Exam-ready summary: The monoamines are the catecholamines (dopamine, noradrenaline, adrenaline — from tyrosine) and serotonin (from tryptophan), inactivated by reuptake, MAO and COMT. Dopamine (substantia nigra/VTA): reward, motivation, movement — excess → psychosis, deficit → Parkinson's, reward → addiction. Noradrenaline (locus coeruleus): arousal, attention, fight-or-flight, mood — linked to depression, anxiety, PTSD. Serotonin (raphe nuclei): mood, sleep, appetite, impulse, aggression — linked to depression, OCD, suicide. They underlie the monoamine hypothesis of depression and the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia, and are the target of most psychiatric drugs.

Conclusion

The monoamines — dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin — are diffuse modulators that set the brain's states of reward, arousal and mood. Their dysregulation is central to the major psychiatric disorders, and their manipulation forms the basis of antidepressant, antipsychotic and stimulant treatment. For the clinical psychologist, the monoamines are the single most important neurochemical family to understand, linking biology to the disorders seen every day in clinical practice.
