Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 9 · Frontal Lobe Syndrome

Q31. Describe the disturbances of regulatory (executive) functions in frontal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The prefrontal cortex is the "executive" of the brain — the region that plans, organizes, initiates, monitors and regulates goal-directed behaviour. When it is damaged, the resulting dysexecutive syndrome is the hallmark of frontal lobe damage. Strikingly, elementary abilities (movement, sensation, even IQ) may be preserved, yet the person becomes unable to organize and regulate their own behaviour in everyday life. This answer describes the regulatory (executive) functions, the specific ways they break down after frontal damage, the tests that reveal these deficits, and their clinical significance.

1. What are Regulatory (Executive) Functions?

Executive functions are the higher-order control processes that direct and coordinate other cognitive abilities toward a goal. They are what allow us to formulate a plan, start it, adjust it flexibly, suppress distractions and inappropriate impulses, and check the result. They are mediated chiefly by the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and its circuits.

2. The Specific Disturbances

(a) Impaired planning and organization

The patient cannot formulate goals, break a task into steps, sequence them, or organize behaviour over time. Everyday tasks that require self-structuring (cooking a meal, managing money, organizing a day) fall apart, even though each individual step could be performed. Revealed by the Tower of London/Hanoi test and by real-life failures.

(b) Loss of cognitive flexibility (perseveration)

The patient cannot shift strategy when circumstances change — they get "stuck." The classic sign is perseveration on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST): the patient keeps sorting by the old rule despite being told it is now wrong, unable to switch set.

(c) Impaired response inhibition

The patient cannot suppress inappropriate or automatic (prepotent) responses. This shows as impulsivity, distractibility, and phenomena of environmental dependency: utilization behaviour (compulsively picking up and using any object within reach) and imitation behaviour (echoing the examiner). Tested by the Stroop test and Go/No-Go tasks.

(d) Impaired abstraction and concept formation

Thinking becomes concrete. The patient interprets proverbs literally ("a stitch in time saves nine" = actual sewing) and struggles to identify similarities or form abstract categories.

(e) Impaired self-monitoring and error correction

The patient fails to notice or correct their own mistakes, showing poor error awareness and reduced insight into their difficulties.

(f) Impaired working memory and initiation

Difficulty holding and manipulating information "online," and either failure to initiate action (with medial damage) or failure to stop it.

Clinical relevance: Executive deficits are easily missed in a structured clinic, because the examiner provides the structure the patient lacks — routine IQ tests may be normal. The characteristic complaint comes from the family: a person who "can pass tests but cannot run their life." These deficits severely affect independent living, work and relationships, and are seen in traumatic brain injury (frontal contusion), frontotemporal dementia, ACA stroke and other frontal pathology. Rehabilitation relies on external structure, aids and routines.
Exam-ready summary: Executive (regulatory) functions — mediated by the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex — plan, initiate, flexibly adjust, inhibit and monitor goal-directed behaviour. Frontal damage produces the dysexecutive syndrome: impaired planning/organization (Tower of London), loss of cognitive flexibility with perseveration (WCST), poor response inhibition (Stroop, Go/No-Go; utilization/imitation behaviour), concrete thinking (proverbs), poor self-monitoring/insight, and impaired working memory. IQ may be preserved, so deficits are missed on routine testing but devastate real-life functioning. Seen in TBI, frontotemporal dementia and ACA stroke.

Conclusion

Disturbance of regulatory (executive) functions is the defining feature of frontal lobe syndrome: it leaves basic abilities intact but destroys the capacity to plan, adapt, inhibit and monitor behaviour toward goals. Because these deficits are subtle on routine testing yet catastrophic in daily life, the clinical psychologist must use specific executive tests and family reports to detect them and design structured, environment-based rehabilitation.
