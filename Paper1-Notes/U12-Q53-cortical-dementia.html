Q53. Describe the neuropsychological profile of cortical dementia.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Dementia is an acquired, usually progressive, decline in cognition severe enough to impair daily functioning, occurring in clear consciousness. A key clinical distinction is between cortical and subcortical dementia, based on the location of the main pathology and the resulting neuropsychological profile. Cortical dementia — the prototype being Alzheimer's disease — arises from damage to the cerebral cortex, especially the temporal and parietal association areas, and characteristically produces the "cortical" deficits of amnesia, aphasia, apraxia and agnosia (the "4 A's"). This answer describes the neuropsychological profile of cortical dementia and contrasts it with the subcortical pattern.

1. What is Cortical Dementia?

Cortical dementia results from pathology affecting the cerebral cortex, particularly the temporal, parietal and later frontal association areas. The prototype is Alzheimer's disease (the commonest dementia), and others include frontotemporal dementia and the language/visual variants. The hallmark is loss of the higher cortical "instrumental" functions.

2. The Core Neuropsychological Profile — the "4 A's"

  • Amnesia — early and prominent anterograde memory loss (difficulty forming new memories) due to medial temporal/hippocampal involvement; progresses to retrograde loss. This is usually the first symptom in Alzheimer's disease.
  • Aphasia — progressive language impairment: word-finding difficulty (anomia), then impaired comprehension and expression, from temporo-parietal damage.
  • Apraxia — inability to perform learned skilled movements (dressing, using tools) despite intact strength, from parietal damage.
  • Agnosia — inability to recognize objects, faces or places despite intact senses, from occipito-temporal/parietal damage.

In addition there is impaired visuospatial ability (getting lost, difficulty copying figures) and, later, executive and judgment decline.

3. Other Features

  • Memory: poor storage/consolidation — recognition is impaired and cueing does not help much (a "true" amnesia), unlike subcortical dementia.
  • Cognitive speed: relatively preserved until late (in contrast to the marked slowing of subcortical dementia).
  • Personality/mood: relatively preserved early (except in frontotemporal dementia); depression may occur.
  • Motor system: normal until late — no early movement disorder (unlike subcortical dementia).
  • Insight: often lost relatively early.

Clinical relevance: Recognizing the cortical profile (early amnesia + the 4 A's) helps diagnose Alzheimer's disease and distinguish it from subcortical dementias and from depression ("pseudodementia"). Neuropsychological assessment documents the pattern and severity, tracks progression, and guides management and family support. In the Indian context, culturally appropriate tools (e.g., the NIMHANS/AIIMS batteries, adapted MMSE/ACE) are used. Early amnesia with poor cueing benefit is a key marker of cortical (Alzheimer-type) dementia.
Exam-ready summary: Cortical dementia (prototype: Alzheimer's disease) arises from cortical (temporo-parietal, then frontal) pathology. Its hallmark is the "4 A's": Amnesia (early anterograde, true storage deficit — cueing doesn't help), Aphasia, Apraxia and Agnosia, plus visuospatial and later executive decline. Cognitive speed, movement and personality are relatively preserved early. It contrasts with subcortical dementia (retrieval memory deficit helped by cues, no 4 A's, marked slowing, early movement disorder, prominent apathy/depression). The pattern aids diagnosis, differentiation from pseudodementia, and rehabilitation planning.

Conclusion

The neuropsychological profile of cortical dementia is defined by early, severe amnesia together with the instrumental cortical deficits of aphasia, apraxia and agnosia — the "4 A's" — reflecting damage to the association cortex. Recognizing this pattern, and contrasting it with the subcortical profile, allows the clinical psychologist to contribute to accurate diagnosis, to distinguish dementia from depression, and to plan appropriate assessment, rehabilitation and family support.
