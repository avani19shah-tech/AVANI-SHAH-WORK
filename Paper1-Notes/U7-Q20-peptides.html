Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 7 · Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

Q20. Discuss the role of peptide transmitters (neuropeptides) in behaviour.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Neuropeptides are short chains of amino acids that act as chemical messengers in the nervous system, most often as neuromodulators rather than fast transmitters. Over a hundred neuropeptides are known, and they are frequently co-released with a classical transmitter to fine-tune its effect. They act on metabotropic (G-protein) receptors, producing slower, longer-lasting and more diffuse effects, and are involved in some of the most important behaviours — pain, pleasure, stress, appetite, social bonding and mood. This answer describes the nature of neuropeptides and discusses the behavioural roles of the major ones.

1. Nature of Neuropeptides

Neuropeptides differ from classical transmitters:

  • They are larger molecules (chains of amino acids), synthesized in the cell body (from larger precursor proteins) rather than at the terminal.
  • They act mainly on metabotropic receptors, giving slow, prolonged, modulatory effects.
  • They are typically co-released with a classical neurotransmitter and are effective at very low concentrations.
  • They are inactivated by enzymatic breakdown (peptidases) rather than reuptake.

2. Major Neuropeptides and Their Behavioural Roles

  • Endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins): produce natural analgesia (pain relief), pleasure, reward and euphoria (e.g., "runner's high"); the target of opioid drugs such as morphine and heroin, which mimic them.
  • Substance P: a transmitter of pain signals from the periphery to the CNS; also implicated in mood and stress.
  • Oxytocin: the "bonding hormone," promotes social attachment, trust, maternal behaviour and pair-bonding, and is released during childbirth and breastfeeding.
  • Vasopressin (ADH): regulates water balance and influences social and aggressive behaviour and pair-bonding (especially in males).
  • Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH): initiates the stress response (HPA axis) and promotes anxiety.
  • Cholecystokinin (CCK): signals satiety (fullness) after eating; also linked to anxiety/panic.
  • Neuropeptide Y (NPY): a powerful stimulant of appetite/feeding and a buffer against stress and anxiety.
  • Orexin (hypocretin): regulates wakefulness and appetite; its loss causes narcolepsy.

Clinical relevance: Neuropeptide systems are targets of important treatments and are implicated in disorders. Opioid peptides underlie both pain management and opioid addiction. Oxytocin is being investigated for autism and social anxiety. CRH overactivity is linked to depression and anxiety. Orexin loss causes narcolepsy, and orexin antagonists are used as sleep aids. Substance P antagonists have been explored for mood and nausea. These systems thus bridge pain medicine, addiction, mood and social behaviour.
Exam-ready summary: Neuropeptides are short amino-acid chains acting mostly as slow, diffuse neuromodulators via metabotropic receptors, often co-released with classical transmitters. Key examples: endogenous opioids (endorphins/enkephalins → analgesia, reward), substance P (pain transmission), oxytocin (bonding, trust), vasopressin (water balance, social/aggressive behaviour), CRH (stress/anxiety), CCK (satiety, panic), NPY (appetite, anti-stress) and orexin (wakefulness, appetite). Clinically they underlie pain management, opioid addiction, stress and mood disorders, autism research (oxytocin) and narcolepsy (orexin).

Conclusion

The neuropeptides form a vast and subtle layer of chemical communication that fine-tunes pain, pleasure, stress, appetite and social behaviour. Acting as slow modulators alongside classical transmitters, they connect the biology of the brain to some of the most human of behaviours — attachment, trust and the response to stress. Their growing role as drug targets makes them increasingly important to the clinical psychologist.
