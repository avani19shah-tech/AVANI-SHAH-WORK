Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 9 · Frontal Lobe Syndrome

Q35. Describe the disturbances of language functions in frontal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The frontal lobe of the language-dominant (usually left) hemisphere is essential for the production and self-regulation of language. It contains Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus, BA 44/45), which controls the motor programming of speech, and prefrontal regions that govern the generation, planning and pragmatic use of language. Frontal damage therefore produces characteristic expressive and generative language disturbances, most importantly Broca's aphasia, while comprehension is relatively preserved. This answer describes the language disturbances of frontal lobe syndrome, the features of Broca's aphasia, reduced verbal fluency, and the regulatory role of language.

1. Broca's (Expressive / Non-Fluent) Aphasia

The classic frontal language disorder, caused by damage to Broca's area in the dominant hemisphere. Its features are:

  • Non-fluent, effortful speech — sparse, slow, laboured output.
  • Agrammatism ("telegraphic" speech) — loss of grammatical words (articles, prepositions, verb endings); the patient uses mainly content words ("Walk... dog... park").
  • Relatively preserved comprehension — the patient understands most of what is said (though complex grammar may be difficult).
  • Impaired repetition and naming.
  • Preserved awareness — the patient knows what they want to say and is typically frustrated by their difficulty (unlike Wernicke's aphasia).

Because Broca's area is adjacent to the motor cortex, Broca's aphasia often accompanies right-sided (especially face/arm) weakness.

2. Reduced Verbal Fluency

Even without frank aphasia, frontal (especially prefrontal) damage impairs the self-generated production of language. On verbal fluency tasks (e.g., naming as many words beginning with "F" as possible in one minute — the FAS test, or naming animals), frontal patients generate few words, reflecting impaired initiation, search and strategy. This is a sensitive sign of frontal/executive dysfunction.

3. Transcortical Motor Aphasia

Damage anterior or superior to Broca's area can cause transcortical motor aphasia: markedly reduced spontaneous speech and difficulty initiating output, but — distinctively — repetition is preserved (because the core language loop is intact). This reflects a problem in driving language rather than in language itself.

4. Impaired Discourse and Pragmatics

Prefrontal damage disturbs the higher, organizational and social use of language:

  • Poorly organized, tangential discourse — difficulty structuring a coherent narrative.
  • Impaired pragmatics — difficulty using language appropriately in social context (turn-taking, relevance, understanding jokes, sarcasm and non-literal meaning).
  • Perseveration in speech — repeating words or phrases.

5. The Regulatory Role of Language (Luria)

Luria emphasized the frontal lobe's role in the verbal regulation of behaviour — using inner speech to guide and control action. Frontal patients may fail to use verbal instructions to regulate their behaviour (e.g., they can say the rule "press when I say red" but cannot use it to control their responses), showing a dissociation between speech and action.

Clinical relevance: Distinguishing Broca's (expressive, frontal) from Wernicke's (receptive, temporal) aphasia is fundamental to localizing left-hemisphere damage. Broca's aphasia is very frustrating and distressing for patients (who retain awareness), affecting communication, mood and social participation, and requires speech–language therapy and psychological support. Reduced verbal fluency is a widely used, sensitive marker of frontal/executive dysfunction in assessment.
Exam-ready summary: Frontal (dominant-hemisphere) damage impairs language production and its regulation, sparing comprehension. The classic disorder is Broca's (non-fluent) aphasia from Broca's area — effortful, agrammatic ("telegraphic") speech, preserved comprehension, impaired repetition/naming, and preserved awareness (frustration), often with right-sided weakness. Prefrontal damage reduces verbal fluency (few words on FAS/animal naming — a sensitive frontal sign). Transcortical motor aphasia (lesion near Broca's) shows reduced spontaneous speech but preserved repetition. There is also impaired discourse/pragmatics and loss of the Lurian verbal regulation of behaviour.

Conclusion

Language disturbance in frontal lobe syndrome is fundamentally a disorder of output and regulation: the patient struggles to produce, initiate and organize speech and to use language to guide behaviour, while understanding remains largely intact. Broca's aphasia and reduced verbal fluency are its signatures, both important for localizing left-frontal damage and for guiding the clinical psychologist's assessment and rehabilitation of communication.
