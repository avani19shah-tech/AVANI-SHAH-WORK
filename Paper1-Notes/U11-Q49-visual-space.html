Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 11 · Parietal & Occipital Syndromes

Q49. Describe the disturbances in visual space perception.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Visual space perception is the ability to perceive the spatial relationships between objects and oneself — location, distance, direction, orientation and the layout of the environment. It depends chiefly on the dorsal visual "where/how" pathway running from the occipital lobe to the parietal lobe, especially the right parietal lobe, which is dominant for spatial processing. Damage to this system produces a range of disturbances in which the patient sees objects but cannot correctly locate, orient or navigate among them. This answer describes the neural basis of visual space perception and the disorders that follow parieto-occipital damage.

1. The Neural Basis

Visual space perception is handled by the dorsal stream from occipital cortex to the parietal lobe, which processes where objects are and how to act on them (in contrast to the ventral "what" stream for object identity). The right parietal lobe is especially important for spatial processing, so right parietal damage produces the most striking spatial deficits.

2. Unilateral Spatial Neglect (Hemispatial Neglect)

The most important spatial disorder, usually from right parietal damage: the patient fails to attend to the left half of space. They may ignore objects, people and food on the left, bump into things on the left, draw only the right half of a picture, and read only the right side of a page. It is a disorder of spatial attention and awareness rather than of vision itself.

3. Balint's Syndrome

From bilateral parieto-occipital damage, the striking triad of:

  • Simultanagnosia — inability to perceive more than one object at a time (cannot see the "whole scene").
  • Optic ataxia — inability to reach accurately for objects under visual guidance (misreaching).
  • Ocular apraxia (gaze apraxia) — inability to voluntarily direct gaze to a target.

4. Other Spatial Disturbances

  • Impaired depth and distance perception — difficulty judging how far away objects are.
  • Impaired judgment of orientation, direction and position — e.g., cannot judge line orientation (tested by the Judgment of Line Orientation test).
  • Topographical disorientation — getting lost in familiar places, inability to learn routes or use maps (impaired spatial navigation).
  • Constructional apraxia — inability to copy or assemble spatial constructions (overlaps with spatial perception).
  • Spatial disorientation — general difficulty locating objects relative to oneself.

5. Assessment

Tested by: line-bisection and cancellation tasks (for neglect), clock and figure copying, the Judgment of Line Orientation test, block design, route-finding and map tasks, and reaching-under-vision tasks.

Clinical relevance: Spatial disorders are common after right-hemisphere stroke and profoundly affect independence and safety — a patient with neglect may fail to dress the left side, collide with objects, or be unsafe crossing roads; topographical disorientation causes getting lost. These deficits are often accompanied by anosognosia (denial), complicating rehabilitation. Recognizing and assessing spatial perception is therefore essential for the clinical psychologist in planning safe, targeted rehabilitation (e.g., neglect therapy, environmental adaptation).
Exam-ready summary: Visual space perception depends on the dorsal "where/how" stream (occipital → parietal), with the right parietal lobe dominant. Disorders: unilateral spatial neglect (ignoring the left half of space, right parietal — line bisection/cancellation deficits); Balint's syndrome (bilateral parieto-occipital: simultanagnosia + optic ataxia + ocular apraxia); impaired depth/distance and orientation judgment; topographical disorientation (getting lost, poor navigation); and constructional apraxia. These are common after right-hemisphere stroke, gravely affect safety and independence, and are often worsened by anosognosia.

Conclusion

Disturbances of visual space perception show that seeing where things are is a distinct function of the dorsal parietal stream, separable from recognizing what they are. Their disorders — especially hemispatial neglect and Balint's syndrome — are common after parieto-occipital (particularly right-hemisphere) damage and have serious consequences for safety and independence, making their assessment and rehabilitation a priority for the clinical psychologist.
