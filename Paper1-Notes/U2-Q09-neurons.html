Paper 1 · Part A · Unit 2 · Structure & Functions of Cells

Q9. Describe the structure and functions of neurons.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The neuron (nerve cell) is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the nervous system — the excitable cell specialized to receive, integrate, conduct and transmit information. The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons, each connected to thousands of others, forming the vast network that underlies all sensation, movement, thought and emotion. Unlike most body cells, neurons are electrically excitable and communicate through electrochemical signals. This answer describes the parts of a neuron, the different types of neurons, their key properties, and how their structure serves their signalling function.

1. Parts of a Neuron

(a) Soma (cell body / perikaryon)

Contains the nucleus and the metabolic machinery (mitochondria, ribosomes/Nissl bodies, endoplasmic reticulum). It maintains the cell and integrates incoming signals from the dendrites.

(b) Dendrites

Short, branching, tree-like extensions that greatly increase the receptive surface. They bear dendritic spines, the main sites where synapses are received. Dendrites carry signals toward the soma.

(c) Axon

A single, usually long process that conducts the action potential away from the soma to other cells. It arises from the axon hillock (the trigger zone where action potentials are generated) and may branch into collaterals.

(d) Myelin sheath

Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating layer formed by oligodendrocytes (CNS) or Schwann cells (PNS), interrupted by gaps called nodes of Ranvier. Myelin dramatically speeds conduction by allowing the impulse to jump from node to node (saltatory conduction).

(e) Axon terminals (terminal buttons / synaptic knobs)

The endings of the axon that contain synaptic vesicles and release neurotransmitter at the synapse to communicate with the next cell.

2. Types of Neurons

By function

  • Sensory (afferent) neurons — carry information from receptors toward the CNS.
  • Motor (efferent) neurons — carry commands from the CNS to muscles and glands.
  • Interneurons — connect neurons within the CNS; the most numerous, they perform integration and processing.

By structure (number of processes)

  • Multipolar — one axon and many dendrites; the commonest CNS type (e.g., motor neurons).
  • Bipolar — one axon and one dendrite (e.g., retina, olfactory).
  • Unipolar/pseudounipolar — a single process that splits (e.g., sensory neurons in dorsal root ganglia).

3. Key Properties of Neurons

  • Excitability (irritability): the ability to respond to a stimulus by generating an electrical signal.
  • Conductivity: the ability to transmit that signal along the axon.
  • Secretion: release of neurotransmitters at the terminal.
  • Longevity but limited division: most neurons last a lifetime but rarely divide, so damage is often permanent (though limited neurogenesis occurs, e.g., in the hippocampus).
  • Plasticity: synapses can strengthen/weaken with experience — the basis of learning.

4. How Structure Serves Function

The neuron's structure is exquisitely matched to its job of signalling: dendrites maximize input, the soma integrates, the axon hillock decides whether to fire, myelin speeds conduction over distance, and the terminals convert the electrical signal into a chemical one at the synapse. Thus a single cell receives thousands of inputs, integrates them, and passes on a coded output.

Clinical relevance: Because neurons rarely regenerate in the CNS, neuronal loss (as in stroke, trauma or neurodegeneration) tends to be permanent, making prevention and rehabilitation crucial. Damage to myelin (e.g., multiple sclerosis) slows conduction and causes sensory, motor and cognitive symptoms. Neuronal plasticity, however, is the biological basis of recovery and of the effectiveness of cognitive rehabilitation and psychotherapy.
Exam-ready summary: The neuron has four functional parts — dendrites (receive input), soma (integrate, house nucleus), axon (conduct the action potential from the axon hillock, insulated by myelin with nodes of Ranvier for saltatory conduction) and terminals (release neurotransmitter). Neurons are classified functionally (sensory, motor, interneuron) and structurally (multipolar, bipolar, unipolar). Their key properties are excitability, conductivity, secretion, longevity with limited division, and plasticity — the last being the biological basis of learning and recovery.

Conclusion

The neuron is a beautifully specialized signalling cell whose every structural feature serves the reception, integration, conduction and transmission of information. Multiplied billions of times and connected into networks, neurons generate all of mental life. Their limited capacity for regeneration but genuine capacity for plasticity together explain both the seriousness of brain injury and the real possibility of recovery — a message of central importance to the clinical psychologist.
