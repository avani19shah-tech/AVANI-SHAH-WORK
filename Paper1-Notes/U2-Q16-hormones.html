Q16. Explain the role of hormones in communication and behaviour (communication between neurons).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Alongside the rapid, wired signalling of neurons, the body uses a slower, chemical, "broadcast" system of communication — the endocrine system, which works through hormones. Hormones are chemical messengers released by endocrine glands directly into the bloodstream, travelling to distant target organs to produce widespread and long-lasting effects on physiology and behaviour. The nervous and endocrine systems are intimately linked through the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, forming the neuroendocrine system. This answer explains what hormones are, how they differ from neurotransmitters, the hypothalamic–pituitary control system, the major hormones and their behavioural effects, with special reference to stress.

1. What Are Hormones?

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by ductless (endocrine) glands into the bloodstream, which carries them throughout the body to act on cells bearing the appropriate receptors. They regulate growth, metabolism, reproduction, stress responses and homeostasis, and they exert powerful effects on mood, motivation and behaviour. Chemically they are of two kinds: peptide/amine hormones (water-soluble, act via surface receptors and second messengers) and steroid hormones (lipid-soluble, cross the cell membrane and act on receptors that alter gene expression).

2. How Hormones Differ from Neurotransmitters

Some molecules serve both roles (e.g., noradrenaline is a neurotransmitter and, from the adrenal medulla, a hormone) — the difference lies in how they are released and act.

3. The Neuroendocrine Link: Hypothalamus and Pituitary

The brain controls the endocrine system through the hypothalamus, which regulates the pituitary gland ("master gland"):

  • The anterior pituitary is controlled by hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones and secretes ACTH (→ adrenal cortisol), TSH (→ thyroid), growth hormone, prolactin, and gonadotrophins (FSH/LH → gonads).
  • The posterior pituitary stores and releases hypothalamic hormones — oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH).

This organization forms hormonal axes (e.g., the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal or HPA axis) regulated by negative feedback: high levels of the final hormone suppress its own release.

4. Major Hormones and Their Behavioural Effects

  • Cortisol (adrenal cortex) — the main stress hormone; mobilizes energy, and in excess/chronic elevation impairs hippocampal memory and is linked to depression and anxiety.
  • Adrenaline/noradrenaline (adrenal medulla) — the "fight-or-flight" response: raise heart rate, alertness and arousal.
  • Thyroid hormones (thyroxine) — regulate metabolism; deficiency causes lethargy, depression and cognitive slowing, excess causes anxiety and restlessness.
  • Sex hormones (oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone) — influence sexual behaviour, mood, aggression and cognition; fluctuations relate to premenstrual, postpartum and menopausal mood changes.
  • Oxytocin — social bonding, trust, maternal behaviour.
  • Melatonin (pineal) — regulates the sleep–wake (circadian) cycle.
  • Insulin — regulates blood glucose (indirectly affecting brain energy and mood).

5. Hormones and the Stress Response

The classic example of hormonal influence on behaviour is the HPA axis: a stressor activates the hypothalamus (CRH) → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex (cortisol). Cortisol prepares the body to cope but, when chronically elevated, damages the hippocampus, impairs memory, and contributes to depression, anxiety and physical illness — a central mechanism in behavioural medicine and the biopsychosocial model of stress.

Clinical relevance: Endocrine disorders frequently masquerade as psychiatric illness — hypothyroidism mimics depression, hyperthyroidism mimics anxiety, Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol) causes depression/psychosis, and Addison's disease causes apathy. The clinical psychologist must consider these "organic" causes. Chronic stress and HPA dysregulation link psychological stress to physical disease, underpinning psychoneuroimmunology and behavioural medicine.
Exam-ready summary: Hormones are chemical messengers released by endocrine glands into the bloodstream to act on distant targets — slower, longer-lasting and more widespread than neurotransmitters. The brain links to the endocrine system via the hypothalamus → pituitary ("master gland"), forming axes (e.g., HPA) regulated by negative feedback. Key hormones and behaviour: cortisol (stress, impairs memory in excess), adrenaline (fight-or-flight), thyroxine (mood/energy), sex hormones (mood, sexual behaviour), oxytocin (bonding), melatonin (sleep). Endocrine disorders (thyroid, cortisol) commonly mimic psychiatric illness, and chronic HPA activation links stress to disease.

Conclusion

Hormones provide the body's slow, sustained, wide-reaching mode of chemical communication, complementing the fast signalling of neurons. Through the neuroendocrine link of the hypothalamus and pituitary, the brain and endocrine system work as one, shaping mood, stress, reproduction, sleep and cognition. Because hormonal disturbances so often present as psychological symptoms, and because stress hormones connect mind and body, an understanding of hormones is essential to the clinical psychologist's biopsychosocial practice.
