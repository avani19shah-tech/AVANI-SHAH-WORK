Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 9 · Frontal Lobe Syndrome

Q32. Describe the disturbances of attentional processes in frontal lobe syndrome.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Attention is the cognitive function that selects and sustains focus on relevant information while filtering out the irrelevant. It is not a single ability but a set of processes, and the frontal lobes — working with the reticular activating system and the parietal cortex — control its higher, effortful aspects. When the frontal lobes are damaged, attention becomes disorganized: the patient is distractible, cannot sustain focus, cannot hold information "in mind," and cannot flexibly direct attention. This answer describes the components of attention, the specific attentional disturbances of frontal lobe syndrome, the tests that reveal them, and their clinical significance.

1. The Components of Attention

  • Sustained attention (vigilance): maintaining focus on a task over time.
  • Selective (focused) attention: concentrating on relevant information while ignoring distractions.
  • Divided attention: attending to more than one thing at once.
  • Alternating attention: switching focus between tasks.
  • Working memory: holding and manipulating information "online" (closely linked to attention).

The frontal lobes provide the top-down control that directs these processes toward goals, working with the arousal system (Luria's Unit 1) that maintains alertness.

2. The Specific Attentional Disturbances

(a) Impaired sustained attention

The patient cannot maintain concentration; performance drifts and lapses over time. They may start a task well but fail to keep it going.

(b) Increased distractibility (impaired selective attention)

The patient is easily pulled off task by irrelevant stimuli — a hallmark of frontal damage. They cannot filter out distractions, so behaviour becomes fragmented and stimulus-bound.

(c) Impaired divided and alternating attention

Difficulty doing two things at once, or switching flexibly between tasks (linked to the executive set-shifting deficit).

(d) Impaired working memory

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is essential for working memory; damage impairs holding and manipulating information (e.g., mental arithmetic, digit span backward, following multi-step instructions).

(e) Poor attentional control and self-regulation

The patient cannot voluntarily direct and sustain attention according to goals, leading to a disorganized, impulsive attentional style.

Clinical relevance: Attentional deficits underlie many downstream problems — poor memory (because material is never properly attended to and encoded), disorganized behaviour and difficulty at work or study. They are prominent after traumatic brain injury and in frontal pathology, and overlap with the presentation of ADHD (which involves frontal–striatal dysfunction). Because attention is the gateway to other cognition, assessing and supporting it (reducing distractions, structuring tasks) is central to rehabilitation.
Exam-ready summary: The frontal lobes provide top-down control of attention (with the arousal system). Frontal damage impairs sustained attention (drifting focus), selective attention (marked distractibility, stimulus-bound behaviour), divided/alternating attention (poor multitasking and switching) and working memory (dorsolateral PFC — poor digit span backward, mental arithmetic). Tested by CPT, Stroop, Trail Making B and digit span backward. Because attention gates encoding, its impairment also worsens memory and disorganizes behaviour; it is prominent in TBI and overlaps with ADHD.

Conclusion

Attentional disturbance is a core and pervasive feature of frontal lobe syndrome, marked especially by distractibility, poor sustained focus and impaired working memory. Because attention is the foundation on which memory, learning and organized behaviour are built, its breakdown has wide-ranging consequences, making its careful assessment and rehabilitation a priority for the clinical psychologist.
