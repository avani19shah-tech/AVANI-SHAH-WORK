Paper 1 · Part B · Unit 12 · Neuropsychological Profiles

Q55. Describe the neuropsychological profile of major mental disorders.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Although the major mental (psychiatric) disorders — schizophrenia, mood disorders and anxiety/OCD — are primarily disorders of thought, mood and behaviour, they are increasingly recognized to involve measurable cognitive (neuropsychological) impairments. These deficits are not incidental: in schizophrenia in particular, cognitive impairment is a core feature that strongly predicts functional outcome. Understanding the neuropsychological profile of these disorders helps in assessment, in distinguishing them from organic brain disease and from each other, and in planning cognitive rehabilitation. This answer describes the neuropsychological profiles of the major mental disorders.

1. Schizophrenia

Cognitive impairment is a core, enduring feature of schizophrenia — often present before onset and predictive of functional outcome (more so than symptoms). The profile is a generalized impairment with particular deficits in:

  • Attention and vigilance — impaired sustained attention.
  • Working memory — reduced capacity to hold and manipulate information.
  • Executive function — poor planning, abstraction and set-shifting (e.g., perseveration on the WCST), reflecting frontal (dorsolateral prefrontal) dysfunction — "hypofrontality."
  • Verbal learning and memory — impaired encoding/organization.
  • Processing speed.
  • Social cognition — impaired theory of mind and emotion recognition, contributing to social difficulty.

2. Mood Disorders

(a) Depression

Depression causes cognitive impairment, especially:

  • Attention and concentration deficits and slowed processing speed.
  • Memory — a retrieval deficit (like a subcortical pattern) with reduced effort; cueing helps.
  • Executive dysfunction and reduced motivation/initiation.
  • Mood-congruent bias — better recall of negative material.
  • "Pseudodementia" — in the elderly, severe depression can mimic dementia; distinguishing features include the depressed patient's "don't know" answers, awareness of deficits and improvement with treatment.

(b) Bipolar disorder

Cognitive deficits (attention, memory, executive function) persist even between episodes (euthymia), indicating a trait component; impulsivity is prominent in mania.

3. Anxiety Disorders and OCD

  • Anxiety disorders: an attentional bias toward threat, impaired concentration, and reduced working memory (worry consumes resources); performance can worsen under stress.
  • OCD: executive dysfunction, cognitive inflexibility, and impaired response inhibition, linked to frontal–striatal (orbitofrontal–caudate) circuit overactivity; some non-verbal memory and organizational deficits.
  • PTSD: deficits in attention, verbal memory and executive function, with intrusive memory and impaired extinction.

4. General Points

  • The deficits are usually milder and more variable than in dementia, and are often partially reversible with treatment (especially in depression).
  • They frequently reflect frontal–subcortical dysfunction (attention, executive, retrieval memory) rather than the cortical "4 A's."
  • Effort, motivation and mood state affect performance, so testing must account for these.
Clinical relevance: Neuropsychological assessment in psychiatric disorders helps (a) distinguish them from organic dementia (e.g., depression vs Alzheimer's — the "pseudodementia" question), (b) document cognitive deficits that impair real-world functioning (especially in schizophrenia, where cognition predicts outcome), and (c) guide cognitive remediation therapy. The clinical psychologist must interpret scores in the light of the patient's mood, motivation and medication, since these strongly influence performance.
Exam-ready summary: Major mental disorders show measurable cognitive deficits, usually of a frontal–subcortical type. Schizophrenia: a core, generalized impairment — attention, working memory, executive function (hypofrontality, WCST perseveration), verbal memory, processing speed and social cognition; predicts outcome. Depression: attention, slowed speed, retrieval memory deficit (cueing helps), executive dysfunction, mood-congruent bias, and "pseudodementia" in the elderly (reversible). Bipolar: deficits persist in euthymia. Anxiety: threat bias, reduced working memory; OCD: executive inflexibility and poor inhibition (frontal–striatal). Deficits are milder than dementia and often partly reversible with treatment.

Conclusion

The major mental disorders are accompanied by characteristic, measurable neuropsychological deficits — most prominently the generalized, outcome-predicting impairment of schizophrenia, the reversible retrieval and executive deficits of depression, and the attentional and inhibitory problems of anxiety and OCD. These profiles, typically of a frontal–subcortical type and sensitive to mood and treatment, help the clinical psychologist distinguish psychiatric from organic conditions, understand functional impairment, and target cognitive remediation.
