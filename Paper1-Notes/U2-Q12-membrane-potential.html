Q12. Explain the resting membrane potential of the neuron (communication within a neuron).

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

Communication within a neuron is electrical, and it begins with the resting membrane potential — the small, steady voltage difference across the neuron's cell membrane when it is not actively signalling. Typically the inside of the neuron is about −70 millivolts (mV) relative to the outside. This charged, "polarized" state is the neuron's readiness position, like a loaded spring, that makes rapid signalling (the action potential) possible. Understanding how the resting potential is created and maintained is fundamental to understanding all neural activity. This answer explains the ionic basis of the resting potential, the forces acting on ions, the role of the sodium–potassium pump, and its significance.

1. What is the Resting Membrane Potential?

A neuron at rest has a difference in electrical charge across its membrane — negative inside relative to outside — of about −70 mV. The membrane is said to be polarized. This potential exists because ions are unevenly distributed across the membrane and the membrane is selectively permeable to them.

2. The Unequal Distribution of Ions

Four ions are important, distributed unevenly by the pump and by their permeabilities:

IonConcentrationNote
Sodium (Na⁺)High outsideWants to enter
Potassium (K⁺)High insideWants to leave
Chloride (Cl⁻)High outside
Large anions (A⁻, proteins)High insideCannot cross → keep inside negative

3. Two Opposing Forces

Each ion is pushed by two forces that together determine its movement:

  • Concentration (diffusion) gradient: ions tend to move from where they are concentrated to where they are scarce (e.g., K⁺ tends to flow out; Na⁺ tends to flow in).
  • Electrical gradient: ions are attracted to opposite charge (the negative interior attracts positive ions).

The point at which these two forces balance for a single ion is its equilibrium potential (given by the Nernst equation). The overall membrane potential, determined by all permeable ions weighted by their permeability, is given by the Goldman equation.

4. Selective Permeability

At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ (because more K⁺ "leak" channels are open). K⁺ therefore tends to leak out down its concentration gradient, leaving the inside negative — this is the main reason the resting potential is close to (but not exactly at) the potassium equilibrium potential.

5. The Sodium–Potassium Pump

The gradients would eventually run down as ions leak. They are maintained by the sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), an active transporter that uses ATP energy to pump 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in. Because it pumps more positive charge out than in, it also contributes slightly (~a few mV) directly to the negativity — it is electrogenic. Without it, the resting potential (and hence all signalling) would collapse.

6. Significance

The resting potential is the prerequisite for neural signalling. It stores potential energy across the membrane so that, when channels open, ions rush in or out to generate the rapid voltage changes of the action potential. Changes from rest are described as depolarization (less negative, excitatory) or hyperpolarization (more negative, inhibitory).

Clinical relevance: Because the resting potential depends on ion gradients, disturbances of electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium) — as in dehydration, kidney disease or endocrine disorders — can impair neural function, causing weakness, confusion, seizures or coma. Many drugs and toxins act on ion channels or the pump; local anaesthetics block Na⁺ channels, and cardiac glycosides inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Understanding the resting potential explains how these agents alter excitability.
Exam-ready summary: The resting membrane potential (~−70 mV, inside negative) arises because (1) ions are unequally distributed (Na⁺/Cl⁻ high outside; K⁺ and large anions high inside); (2) the membrane is selectively more permeable to K⁺, which leaks out leaving the inside negative; and (3) two forces — concentration and electrical gradients — act on each ion (balanced at its equilibrium potential per the Nernst equation). The electrogenic Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in) maintains the gradients. This polarized state is the stored energy that makes the action potential possible.

Conclusion

The resting membrane potential is the quiet, charged state that underlies all neural communication. Created by unequal ion distributions, selective permeability and the tireless sodium–potassium pump, it primes the neuron to fire. Every subsequent electrical event — and therefore every sensation, movement and thought — depends on this fundamental electrochemical foundation, and its disturbance by electrolyte imbalance or drugs has direct clinical consequences.
