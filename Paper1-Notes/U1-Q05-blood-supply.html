Q5. Describe the blood supply to the brain.

10 Marks · Long Essay

Introduction

The brain is metabolically demanding: though only ~2% of body weight, it receives about 15–20% of cardiac output and consumes ~20% of the body's oxygen and glucose. It has almost no energy reserves, so a constant blood supply is critical — interruption for a few minutes causes irreversible neuronal death. Blood reaches the brain through two paired arterial systems that join at the base in a protective ring, the Circle of Willis. Understanding the arterial territories is essential in clinical neuropsychology because stroke (interrupted blood flow) is a leading cause of the focal cognitive and behavioural syndromes psychologists assess and rehabilitate.

1. The Two Arterial Systems

(a) Anterior circulation — the internal carotid arteries

The two internal carotid arteries supply the anterior two-thirds of the brain. Each divides into:

  • Anterior cerebral artery (ACA) — supplies the medial surfaces of the frontal and parietal lobes.
  • Middle cerebral artery (MCA) — the largest branch, supplying most of the lateral surface of the hemisphere, including the motor/sensory strips and the language areas.

(b) Posterior circulation — the vertebrobasilar system

The two vertebral arteries ascend and join to form the single basilar artery, which supplies the brainstem and cerebellum and terminates as the two posterior cerebral arteries (PCA) supplying the occipital lobes and inferomedial temporal lobes.

2. The Circle of Willis

At the base of the brain the anterior and posterior systems join in an arterial ring, the Circle of Willis, formed by the ACAs (joined by the anterior communicating artery), the internal carotids, and the PCAs (joined to the carotid system by the posterior communicating arteries). Its key value is collateral circulation: if one feeding vessel is narrowed or blocked, blood can be rerouted around the ring, partially protecting the brain. (In practice the ring is often incomplete, which affects stroke outcome.)

4. Venous Drainage

De-oxygenated blood drains from cerebral veins into the dural venous sinuses (e.g., superior sagittal, transverse and sigmoid sinuses), and finally into the internal jugular veins. Blockage of a sinus (cerebral venous sinus thrombosis) can cause raised pressure, headache, seizures and focal deficits.

5. Regulation of Cerebral Blood Flow

Cerebral blood flow is tightly regulated by autoregulation (maintaining constant flow across a range of blood pressures) and by local metabolic factors — active regions attract more blood (the basis of functional imaging such as fMRI and PET, which infer neural activity from blood-flow changes).

Clinical relevance: Stroke — ischaemic (blockage) or haemorrhagic (bleed) — is one of the commonest causes of acquired brain damage and of the syndromes clinical neuropsychologists assess. Because each artery has a defined territory, the pattern of deficits (e.g., aphasia + right arm weakness = left MCA) helps localize the lesion. Transient ischaemic attacks (TIAs) are warning signs. Knowledge of vascular territories also guides rehabilitation and prognosis.
Exam-ready summary: The brain is supplied by the anterior circulation (internal carotids → ACA and MCA) and the posterior circulation (vertebral arteries → basilar → PCA), which meet in the Circle of Willis for collateral protection. ACA infarct → contralateral leg weakness + abulia; MCA infarct → face/arm weakness, aphasia or neglect; PCA infarct → hemianopia + memory/visual deficits; basilar infarct → brainstem/cerebellar signs. Venous blood drains via dural sinuses to the internal jugular veins. Flow is autoregulated and increases in active regions (basis of fMRI/PET).

Conclusion

The brain's dual arterial supply, unified by the Circle of Willis, ensures the continuous delivery of oxygen and glucose on which neurons absolutely depend. Because interruption produces territory-specific syndromes, vascular anatomy is directly relevant to the clinical psychologist's task of localizing lesions, understanding stroke-related cognitive deficits, and planning neuropsychological rehabilitation.
