Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Depression

A structured, clinically-oriented training course that teaches you how therapy for depression is conceptualised, planned, delivered, monitored and reviewed — module by module, the way a supervisor would teach it.

Built around evidence-based sources: NICE NG222, the 2022 VA/DoD MDD guideline, the Beck cognitive model, Centre for Clinical Interventions (CCI) therapist & consumer manuals, Cochrane/meta-analytic evidence, and India-specific research (the Healthy Activity Program).

Educational use & scope. This document is a learning resource for a psychology trainee. It is not a substitute for formal supervised training, clinical judgement, or the primary manuals it points to. Orientation — Curriculum Map & How to Use This Course

You told me: "right now I don't know what to do with depression clients and how to deal with them." That is exactly the right place to start, and it is more common among trainees than you might think. The aim of this course is to move you from that uncertainty to a position where you can sit with a depressed client and know — at each moment — what you are trying to accomplish, why you are doing it now, how you will know if it is working, and what to do if it is not. Those four questions run through every module.

How I will teach

Each module follows the same rhythm so it becomes a habit: concept → rationale → evidence → indications/contraindications → how to introduce it → how to run it → common mistakes → troubleshooting → worksheet → worked example → dialogue → homework → review. At the end of each module you get: key concepts, clinical pearls, mistakes to avoid, a practical exercise, a fictional case, a short quiz, suggested reading, and a "you should now be able to…" checklist.

Orientation — The Evidence-Based Hierarchy of Interventions

Not every therapy is equally supported for depression. A responsible clinician chooses interventions in roughly this order, then individualises. Throughout the course I mark evidence strength with tags: Strong Moderate Emerging Limited.

TierInterventionsEvidence & guideline status
First-line, best-establishedCBT (incl. cognitive therapy), Behavioural Activation, Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)Strong Recommended across NICE NG222 and the 2022 VA/DoD MDD guideline; large meta-analytic base. BA is non-inferior to full CBT and simpler to deliver.
Well-supportedProblem-Solving Therapy (PST), Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT — especially relapse prevention in recurrent depression)Moderate–Strong PST has good meta-analytic support; MBCT has strong evidence specifically for preventing relapse in people with ≥3 prior episodes.
Supported / third-waveAcceptance & Commitment Therapy (ACT), short-term psychodynamic therapy, counsellingModerate Included as options in guidelines; evidence base smaller than CBT/BA/IPT.
Targeted / adjunctiveCompassion-Focused Therapy (CFT) & self-compassion approaches (self-criticism/shame), Metacognitive Therapy (rumination)Emerging Promising for specific mechanisms (shame, rumination); fewer large trials in primary MDD.
The single most useful clinical rule of thumb

Severity and complexity guide intensity. For less severe depression, lower-intensity, more behavioural options (guided self-help, BA, brief CBT) are appropriate first. For moderate-to-severe depression, offer a full course of an evidence-based psychological therapy (CBT/BA/IPT), consider combining with medication, and coordinate with a psychiatrist. This mirrors the stepped-care logic in NICE NG222 and the VA/DoD guideline (see References).

A realistic learning sequence

  1. Get the foundations solid (Parts 1–3). Do not touch technique until you can formulate.
  2. Learn structure + behavioural activation (Parts 4–5). BA alone helps many clients and is the safest place for a trainee to begin.
  3. Add cognitive work (Parts 6–8), then problem-solving (Part 9).
  4. Learn to target specific presentations (Parts 10–11) and to recognise when another therapy fits better (Parts 12–13).
  5. Integrate everything into a whole course of therapy (Part 14) and rehearse (Parts 19–20) within your ethical scope (Part 21).

Part 1 — Understanding Depression

Foundations · Module 1

What is the therapist trying to accomplish here?

Before you can treat depression you must be able to recognise it accurately, distinguish it from things that look like it, and identify what is keeping it going right now. Treatment targets maintaining factors — so this module is not academic; it is where your treatment plan is born.

A · Clinical understanding

Definition

Depression is a clinical syndrome — a persistent cluster of mood, cognitive, behavioural, physiological and interpersonal changes that together impair functioning — not simply sadness. The two "gateway" symptoms are persistently low mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia) most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks (DSM-5-TR / ICD-11).

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

Diagnosed when, over the same 2-week period, the person has ≥5 of the following (at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia), representing a change from before, causing distress/impairment, and not better explained by substances, a medical condition, or another disorder:

  • Depressed mood most of the day
  • Markedly diminished interest/pleasure (anhedonia)
  • Significant weight/appetite change
  • Insomnia or hypersomnia
  • Psychomotor agitation or retardation (observable by others)
  • Fatigue / loss of energy
  • Feelings of worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt
  • Reduced concentration or indecisiveness
  • Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, plan or attempt

Specify severity (mild/moderate/severe), with/without psychotic features, single vs recurrent episode. Roughly the same nine-symptom architecture underlies the PHQ-9 (Part 2).

Persistent Depressive Disorder (PDD / dysthymia)

Chronic depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (≥1 year in children/adolescents), with ≥2 additional symptoms (appetite, sleep, energy, self-esteem, concentration, hopelessness). It is often less intense than MDD but more grinding and long-standing; "double depression" describes an MDD episode superimposed on PDD. PDD tends to respond more slowly and often benefits from combined psychotherapy + medication.

Other depressive presentations

  • Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) — mood symptoms tied to the luteal phase.
  • Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder — chronic irritability in children.
  • Depression with seasonal pattern, peripartum onset (antenatal/postnatal), with anxious distress, with melancholic features (profound anhedonia, early waking, marked psychomotor change), with atypical features (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity).
  • Substance/medication-induced and due to another medical condition (see differential).

Indian clinical note

In many Indian settings clients present the physiological/somatic cluster first — "body pain," "gas," "weakness," "heaviness in the head" — rather than naming sadness. This is not denial or poor insight; it is a culturally shaped idiom of distress. We return to this in Part 18.

Course and prognosis

  • MDD is typically episodic and recurrent: a majority who have one episode have another. Risk of recurrence rises with each episode.
  • Untreated episodes commonly last months; treatment shortens episodes and reduces recurrence.
  • Residual symptoms and incomplete recovery strongly predict relapse — which is why relapse prevention (Part 14, session end) matters.
  • Good prognostic signs: acute onset, clear precipitant, good premorbid functioning, engagement, social support. Poorer: chronicity, comorbidity, severe hopelessness, ongoing psychosocial adversity.

Risk factors

Female sex, prior episodes, family history, early adversity/trauma, chronic stress, chronic physical illness and pain, substance use, certain personality features (e.g. high self-criticism, dependency), and socio-economic adversity. Think of these as predisposing and precipitating factors in your formulation (Part 3).

Maintaining factors — the therapist's primary target

Depression is kept going by self-reinforcing cycles. The most treatable are:

  • Behavioural: withdrawal & avoidance → loss of positive reinforcement & mastery → lower mood → more withdrawal. (Targeted by BA.)
  • Cognitive: negative automatic thoughts, biased attention/memory, rumination → sustained low mood. (Targeted by cognitive techniques, rumination work.)
  • Physiological: disrupted sleep & inactivity → fatigue → less activity.
  • Interpersonal: withdrawal & irritability → conflict/isolation → less support. (Targeted by IPT.)

Clinical pearl: you treat maintaining cycles, not "the past." History explains vulnerability; the cycles explain why the person is stuck today, and cycles are where change happens.

Common comorbidities

Anxiety disorders (very common), substance use, PTSD, OCD, personality difficulties, chronic pain and physical illness. Comorbidity affects sequencing (what you treat first) and risk — covered in Part 17.

B · Differential diagnosis

What is the therapist trying to accomplish? To make sure you are treating the right thing. The single most important differential — because it changes everything — is bipolar disorder.

Assessment questions every therapist should ask

"Over the last two weeks, how has your mood been most days?" · "Have you lost interest or pleasure in things you used to enjoy?" · "Tell me about your sleep, appetite and energy." · "How are you seeing yourself, the future?" · "Have there ever been times you felt the opposite — unusually high energy, not needing sleep, doing things others found excessive?" (bipolar screen) · "What was happening in your life when this started?" · "How is this affecting work/family?" · and always, sensitively, the risk questions below.

C · Risk assessment

Non-negotiable

Every depression assessment and, at appropriate points, every session must include risk. As a trainee you always discuss any significant risk with your supervisor the same day, and follow your service's protocol. The techniques below support clinical judgement; they never replace it or replace escalation.

Assess, using a calm, direct, normalising manner (asking about suicide does not plant the idea):

  • Suicidal ideation — passive ("I wish I wouldn't wake up") vs active ("I want to end my life"); frequency, intensity, controllability.
  • Plan, means, preparation, intent — the more specific and available, the higher the concern.
  • Self-harm — methods, frequency, function, whether escalating.
  • Hopelessness — one of the strongest predictors of suicide; ask directly about the future.
  • Previous attempts — strongest single historical predictor; ask what happened, how they were saved, current view of it.
  • Psychosis — command hallucinations, nihilistic/guilt delusions markedly raise risk.
  • Severe functional deterioration — not eating, not self-caring, unable to work/parent.
  • Risk to others — including, rarely, extended suicide involving dependents.
  • Protective factors — reasons for living, responsibilities (children, parents), religious/moral objections, help-seeking, supportive relationships, future orientation. Protective factors reduce but never nullify risk.
Tools you should know (Part 2 & Part 17 expand these)

C-SSRS (Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) — a free, plain-language, clinician-administered set of questions that graduates ideation and behaviour; explicitly not a substitute for full clinical evaluation. Safety Planning Intervention (Stanley & Brown) — a brief, collaborative, prioritised list of coping strategies and contacts. Both are covered practically in Part 17. Links in References.

What should happen when significant risk is identified

  1. Stay with the person and the topic — do not rush past it. Assess systematically.
  2. Match response to level of risk: for high/imminent risk (clear intent, plan, means, little protection) — do not leave them alone, involve family/support with consent where possible, arrange urgent psychiatric assessment / emergency services / hospital as per protocol.
  3. Collaborative safety plan (not a "no-suicide contract," which is ineffective): warning signs, internal coping, people/places for distraction, people to ask for help, professionals/agencies, and means restriction.
  4. Means restriction — reduce access to lethal means (medications, pesticides — highly relevant in India, ligatures, firearms), engaging family.
  5. Document assessment, reasoning, plan, and consultation.
  6. Escalate & consult — supervisor and psychiatrist the same day; know your local crisis pathways.
India — crisis resources to know

Keep locally verified numbers to hand (e.g. the national Tele-MANAS helpline 14416 / 1-800-891-4416, and hospital emergency services). Verify current local crisis lines for your setting; availability changes. If someone is in immediate danger, emergency medical services and the nearest hospital are the priority.

Key concepts

Depression is a syndrome of maintaining cycles across five domains · MDD vs PDD vs other presentations · always screen for bipolarity and medical causes · risk assessment is continuous, systematic and supervised.

Clinical pearls

Treat cycles, not history. · Somatic presentation is common and legitimate. · Hopelessness and prior attempts are your loudest risk signals. · Asking about suicide reduces, not increases, risk.

Therapist mistakes to avoid

Diagnosing MDD without a bipolar/medical screen · pathologising normal sadness or grief · skimming risk when rapport feels fragile · treating symptoms without a maintaining-cycle map.

Part 2 — Psychological Assessment Before Therapy

Foundations · Module 2

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

A good assessment does three jobs at once: it (1) establishes diagnosis and risk, (2) gathers exactly what you need to formulate, and (3) begins building the therapeutic relationship. You are not collecting facts for a file — you are building the map you will treat from.

Formulation-critical vs background

A trap for trainees is exhaustive history-taking that never converts into a plan. Ask: "Does this information change my hypothesis about what is maintaining the depression or what will help?" If yes, it is formulation-critical. If it is simply context (e.g. the exact name of a primary school), it is background — note it, move on.

Measures for depression — appropriate use & interpretation

A scale is never a substitute for clinical assessment. Measures quantify severity and track change; they do not diagnose on their own and they miss context. Use them to complement the interview and to make progress visible (measurement-based care).

PHQ-9 Strong

  • What it assesses: the nine DSM depression symptoms over the past two weeks; self-report; free/public-domain (Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams, 2001).
  • Administration: ~2–5 minutes, client-completed; 9 items rated 0 (not at all) → 3 (nearly every day). A 10th item rates functional difficulty (not scored in the total).
  • Scoring: total 0–27. Bands: 5 mild, 10 moderate, 15 moderately severe, 20 severe. A cut-off of ≥10 has ~88% sensitivity and ~88% specificity for major depression.
  • Interpretation: use bands to gauge severity and to guide intensity of care; a ≥5-point drop is a common marker of meaningful change; aim for remission (often <5).
  • Item 9 (thoughts of being better off dead / self-harm) is a trigger for immediate risk assessment — never let a positive item 9 pass unexplored.
  • Limitations: self-report bias; somatic items overlap with physical illness; not diagnostic alone.

BDI-II (Beck Depression Inventory-II) Strong

  • What it assesses: 21 items, severity of cognitive/affective/somatic symptoms over two weeks; strong emphasis on cognitive symptoms.
  • Administration/scoring: self-report; total 0–63; typical bands 0–13 minimal, 14–19 mild, 20–28 moderate, 29–63 severe.
  • Copyright: proprietary (Pearson) — you must obtain it legitimately; this course does not reproduce items. Item on suicide triggers risk assessment.
  • Limitations: cost/licensing; somatic overlap; self-report.

HAM-D / HDRS (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) Moderate–Strong

  • What it assesses: clinician-rated severity (commonly 17 items); heavier on somatic/sleep items; widely used in research.
  • Administration: semi-structured clinician interview; requires training for reliable rating.
  • Limitations: rater variability; somatic weighting can inflate scores in medically ill patients; less suited to quick session-by-session tracking than PHQ-9.

Other measures worth knowing

QIDS/IDS (self- or clinician-rated), MADRS (clinician-rated, good sensitivity to change), K10/K6 (general distress screening — used in Indian community research), WHO-5 wellbeing. For risk, the C-SSRS (clinician-administered). For process/mechanism, activity records and thought records (Parts 5–6) function as clinical measures too.

Using scores to monitor progress

Administer a brief measure (usually PHQ-9) at intake and at regular intervals (e.g. each session or every 2–3). Plot the trajectory; discuss it collaboratively ("your PHQ-9 has gone from 18 to 11 — what do you make of that?"). Flag non-response (no meaningful change by ~4–6 sessions → review formulation, adherence, comorbidity, and consult supervisor about stepping up or adding medication). Watch item 9 every time.

Part 3 — Case Formulation

Foundations · Module 3

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

A formulation is a shared, testable hypothesis about how this person's depression developed and — crucially — what keeps it going now. It converts assessment into a plan and gives both of you a rationale for every technique. "Formulation drives treatment" is the sentence to tattoo on your clinical mind.

The CBT (maintenance) formulation — the "hot cross bun"

For a specific recent situation, map the interactions between four elements — they feed each other:

ElementExample (client "Rahul", see below)
Situation / triggerFriend messages to meet for tea
Automatic thoughts"I'm boring now. He's just being polite. I'll ruin his evening."
EmotionsSad, anxious, ashamed
PhysiologyHeavy, tired, tight chest
BehaviourMakes an excuse, stays home

The behaviour (avoidance) → confirms "I'm boring/alone" → deepens mood → next invitation feels harder. That loop is the treatment target.

The longitudinal (developmental) formulation

Beneath the moment sit deeper cognitive structures (Part 7). The longitudinal formulation links them:

  • Early experiences (predisposing) →
  • Core beliefs ("I'm worthless / unlovable / a failure") →
  • Intermediate beliefs / rules / assumptions ("If I don't succeed at everything, I'm nothing"; "I must never burden others") →
  • Critical incident(s) (precipitant) activates the beliefs →
  • Automatic thoughts → emotions, physiology, behaviour → maintaining cycles.

Other major evidence-based conceptualisations

Beck's cognitive model Strong

Depression is characterised by the negative cognitive triad (negative view of self, world, future), maintained by negative automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions, and underlying dysfunctional attitudes/schemas. Evidence: robust cross-cultural associations between negative cognition and depressive symptoms; the model has strong empirical support as a framework for the maintenance of episodes.

Behavioural model Strong

Depression follows a loss of response-contingent positive reinforcement (Lewinsohn): fewer rewarding person–environment interactions → low mood → withdrawal → still fewer rewards. Explains anhedonia and inactivity; leads directly to activity scheduling.

Behavioural activation model Strong

Refines the behavioural model: depression is maintained by avoidance and by behaviour that is negatively reinforced (short-term relief) but reduces contact with reward and mastery. Treatment = re-engagement guided by function, not mood. (Martell et al.)

Interpersonal model Strong

Depression arises and is maintained in the context of current interpersonal problems — grief, role transitions, disputes, deficits. Improving these relationships/roles improves mood. (Klerman & Weissman; see Part 12.)

Problem-solving model Moderate

Depression is worsened by ineffective problem orientation and skills under life stress; teaching structured problem-solving reduces symptoms. (D'Zurilla & Nezu; see Part 9.)

Rumination / metacognitive Emerging

Repetitive negative thinking (rumination) maintained by positive/negative metacognitive beliefs about worrying/thinking; treatment targets the process, not content. (Wells; Nolen-Hoeksema.)

Choosing a model

These are lenses, not rivals. Start with a CBT maintenance formulation for almost everyone; lean behavioural when inactivity/avoidance dominate and cognitive engagement is low (severe depression), cognitive when negative thinking is prominent, interpersonal when a relationship/role problem is central, and add rumination-focused work when repetitive thinking is the engine.

Worked examples — formulation → plan

Example A — Rahul (moderate MDD)

Formulation: long-standing belief "my worth = providing for my family" (predisposing) activated by a business downturn (precipitating); maintained by withdrawal, rumination on failure, alcohol-disrupted sleep, and growing marital distance.

Plan that follows directly: (1) risk monitoring + reduce alcohol/means; (2) start with behavioural activation to reverse withdrawal and rebuild mastery/pleasure and routine; (3) add cognitive work on "failure/worthlessness" automatic thoughts, then the underlying assumption; (4) sleep & alcohol as behavioural targets; (5) later, an interpersonal/couple focus on marital distance; (6) relapse-prevention plan. Notice how each element maps to a maintaining factor.

Example B — Priya (recurrent MDD, high self-criticism)

Formulation: core belief "I'm not good enough" from a high-achieving, critical home; rule "I must be perfect or I'm a failure"; third episode, precipitated by a missed promotion; maintained by rumination, self-attacking thoughts, social withdrawal, and perfectionistic avoidance (not starting tasks she can't do perfectly).

Plan: BA + behavioural experiments on perfectionistic rules; cognitive work on self-critical thoughts; given prominent shame/self-attack, integrate self-compassion/CFT techniques (Part 10); MBCT-style relapse prevention (recurrent course).

Example C — Anand (severe MDD, low engagement)

Formulation: severe episode, barely leaving bed, PHQ-9 = 23, profound anhedonia and fatigue, passive suicidal ideation; cognitively too slowed for much thought work yet.

Plan: safety first + psychiatric coordination (consider medication); behavioural activation only to begin (tiny, scheduled activities), defer cognitive restructuring until activation and engagement improve. This is a deliberate sequencing decision the formulation makes obvious.

Part 4 — Complete CBT for Depression: The Therapy & the Relationship

Core CBT · Module 4

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

CBT is not a bag of techniques applied at random; it is a structured, collaborative, time-limited therapy delivered inside a warm relationship. This module teaches the container — the relationship and the session structure — into which every later technique is poured. Treatment is individualised, but the usual arc is: engage & assess → behavioural activation → cognitive work → belief-level work → relapse prevention.

The therapeutic relationship in CBT

Evidence is clear that the alliance predicts outcome across therapies; in CBT the relationship is necessary but works with the techniques, not instead of them.

Anatomy of a standard CBT session

  1. Mood check & brief measure (e.g. PHQ-9). Note item 9.
  2. Bridge from last session ("what stood out from last time?").
  3. Set the agenda collaboratively — 2–3 items, prioritised.
  4. Review homework — always; it signals it matters (see below).
  5. Work the agenda — the main therapeutic tasks.
  6. Set new homework collaboratively, linked to today's work.
  7. Summarise & take feedback — "what was helpful/unhelpful today?"

Agenda setting — effective

Therapist"Let's decide together what's most useful to focus on today. I'd like to review the activity diary and check on sleep. What would you like to add or put first?"

Client"The fight with my brother is on my mind."

Therapist"Let's make that our first item, then the diary. Does that work?"

Ineffective

Therapist"So… how was your week?" (no agenda → session drifts, homework never reviewed, no clear task)

Reviewing homework — why it matters & how

Homework (better framed as "between-session practice" or "experiments") is where change consolidates; skipping the review teaches the client it's optional. Always ask what they learned, not just whether they "did it." If not done, treat that as data (a behavioural experiment about barriers), not a failing.

Psychoeducation

Explaining depression to a client (normalising without minimising)

Therapist"Depression isn't weakness or a character flaw — it's a common, treatable condition that affects mood, thinking, the body and behaviour. When people are depressed, they naturally do less and pull back, which feels sensible but tends to lower mood further. And depression acts like a filter that makes thoughts more negative and more believable. The good news is that we can work on both the doing and the thinking, and mood tends to follow."

Client"So it's not just me being lazy?"

Therapist"Not at all. What looks like laziness is usually depression's low energy and loss of reward. That's exactly what we'll target — gently and step by step."

Explaining the CBT model & the thought–feeling–behaviour link

Therapist"Here's the idea we'll use. In any situation, what we think, how we feel, what our body does, and how we behave all influence each other. Say you're low and a friend texts. The thought 'he's only being polite' leads to feeling ashamed, staying home, and then feeling more alone — which 'proves' the thought. We can step into that cycle at the thinking and the behaviour, and break it. Shall we map one of your recent examples like this?"

Draw the hot-cross-bun with the client's own example. Handing them the drawing is itself de-shaming — the problem becomes an external cycle to tackle together, not a personal defect.

Normalising without minimising — the balance

"Many people with depression feel this" (normalise) must be paired with "and I can see how heavy this is for you" (validate) — never "everyone feels down sometimes" (minimise). The first builds hope; the last breaks trust.

Key concepts

CBT = structured + collaborative + time-limited inside a warm alliance · standard session anatomy (mood → bridge → agenda → homework review → work → new homework → feedback) · collaborative empiricism & guided discovery · psychoeducation externalises the problem into a cycle.

Clinical pearls

Set an agenda every session, even in crisis (adjust it). · Always review homework. · Take feedback at the end — it models the collaborative stance and catches ruptures early.

Mistakes to avoid

Free-floating "supportive chats" with no structure · lecturing the model instead of drawing the client's own example · reassurance instead of validation · never eliciting feedback.

Part 5 — Behavioural Activation (BA)

Core CBT · Module 5

Why start here?

Behavioural activation is often the first and safest engine of change, especially in moderate-to-severe depression where clients are too slowed or hopeless for cognitive work. It is powerful, learnable, and — as a trainee — a superb place to begin. It can be delivered as a complete stand-alone therapy.

What BA is · Rationale · Evidence · Indications

What: a structured approach that reverses the depressive spiral of withdrawal and avoidance by systematically increasing contact with activities that provide pleasure, mastery, and meaning (values), chosen by function rather than mood ("act according to a plan, not according to how you feel").

Rationale: depression reduces rewarding person–environment interactions (Lewinsohn); avoidance gives short-term relief but deepens depression (Martell). Re-engagement restores reinforcement, mastery and routine, and mood follows behaviour.

Evidence Strong: BA is recommended in NICE NG222 and the VA/DoD 2022 guideline; meta-analyses show large effects vs controls (e.g. Hedges' g ≈ 0.8 vs inactive controls) and it is broadly comparable to full CBT — while being simpler to train and deliver. In India, the Healthy Activity Program (HAP) — a BA-based brief treatment delivered by lay counsellors — was effective for moderate-to-severe depression in primary care (Patel et al., Lancet 2017).

Indications: most depressed clients; particularly first-line when inactivity, anhedonia, withdrawal and low energy dominate, and in severe depression. Less appropriate / adapt: when a medical condition limits activity (adapt goals), in acute mania (not depression), or where activity itself is unsafe — and never as a substitute for risk management.

Therapist explanation: "Before we change anything, let's understand your week. I'd like you to note, hour by hour, what you did and rate your mood 0–10. We're looking for the link between what you do and how you feel." Rationale: builds the client's own evidence for the activity–mood link and reveals avoidance patterns.

4 · Pleasure, mastery & values-based activity

Depression strips both enjoyment (pleasure) and sense of achievement (mastery). Deliberately schedule both, plus activities tied to the client's values (being a good parent, learning, faith, health). When a planned activity doesn't happen, treat it as information: was it too big? badly timed? blocked by a thought ("no point")? That tiny success — and the gap between predicted and actual — became the evidence that "doing helps even when I don't feel like it," and the platform for the next step.

Part 6 — Cognitive Techniques

Core CBT · Module 6

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

To help the client notice the automatic negative thinking depression generates, and to relate to those thoughts differently — examining them as hypotheses rather than facts. The aim is not "positive thinking"; it is accurate, balanced, and flexible thinking. Cognitive restructuring has strong evidence as a component of CBT.

Read this before you "challenge" any thought

Do not treat cognitive work as proving the client wrong. Many depressive thoughts contain real truth (a job was lost; a relationship did end). The therapist's job is to test whether the thought is accurate and helpful, and to loosen distortion — never to invalidate a legitimate negative experience. If a thought is largely true, you move to problem-solving (Part 9), acceptance, or coping — not disputation. Sequencing note: usually begin cognitive work after some behavioural activation, and in less severe/cognitively-able states.

Stage 1 · Identifying automatic thoughts (ATs)

ATs are the quick, involuntary appraisals that pop up in situations. Teach the client to catch them by anchoring to emotion shifts: "When your mood dropped, what went through your mind?"

  • Situation identification — when/where/who; be specific.
  • Emotion identification — name it and rate intensity 0–100%.
  • Automatic thought identification — words/images at the moment; rate belief 0–100%.

If a negative thought is accurate and the situation is genuinely bad, you validate and move to problem-solving/coping — you do not slap a "distortion" label on real pain.

Part 7 — Core Beliefs and Intermediate Beliefs

Core CBT · Module 7

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

To reach the deeper cognitive structures that generate the recurring automatic thoughts — and to modify them so gains last and relapse risk falls. This is usually later-stage work, once the client can catch and examine ATs and has some behavioural momentum.

The cognitive levels

LevelDefinitionExample
Automatic thoughtsSituation-specific, surface"He thinks my work is useless."
Intermediate beliefs (rules, attitudes, assumptions)Cross-situational conditional rulesRule: "I must always perform perfectly." Assumption: "If I fail, people will reject me." Attitude: "Failure is unbearable."
Core beliefs / schemasGlobal, absolute beliefs about self/others/world"I'm worthless" · "I'm unlovable" · "I'm helpless."

Beck grouped depressive core beliefs broadly into helplessness ("I'm incompetent/weak") and unlovability ("I'm undesirable/will be abandoned"); a third cluster is worthlessness/badness.

When to do this — and when it's premature

Premature belief work is a classic trainee error. Going for "I'm worthless" in session 2, with a severely depressed, hopeless client who can barely get out of bed, can amplify shame and confirm the belief. Do belief work when: the client can identify/examine ATs, has some behavioural gains and stability, risk is managed, and there is a solid alliance. In brief therapy you may only identify beliefs and do light work; deep schema change needs time.

Techniques

Downward arrow (identifying beliefs)

Take a believable AT and repeatedly ask, in effect, "If that were true, what would it mean about you?" until you reach the core belief.

Therapist"You thought 'my boss thinks my work is useless.' If that were true, what would it mean to you?"

Client"That I'm not good at my job."

Therapist"And if you're not good at your job, what would that mean?"

Client"That I'm failing at everything."

Therapist"And if that were true, what would it say about you as a person?"

Client"…That I'm worthless."

Therapist"Thank you — that took courage. That belief, 'I'm worthless', is what we can start to examine together."

Modifying beliefs — a toolkit

  • Historical exploration: where did this belief come from? "Made sense as a child in that home, but is it true of you as an adult now?" — separates origin from present truth.
  • Evidence examination: gather evidence for/against the belief across life, not just the depressive lens.
  • Continuum technique: replace binary "worthless vs worthy" with a graded scale; define what points mean; locate self realistically.
  • Positive data log: a daily log of evidence for a new, balanced belief (e.g. "I have worth/value") — because depressed clients filter out disconfirming data, they must actively collect it.
  • Behavioural experiments (Part 8): test rules ("If I ask for help, I'll be rejected") in real life.
  • Alternative balanced belief: craft a credible new belief ("I'm a worthwhile person who, like everyone, has strengths and struggles") and build evidence for it over weeks.
  • Schema modification (advanced): for entrenched, early beliefs — imagery rescripting, chair-work, and schema-therapy methods — typically beyond brief CBT and requiring specific training/supervision.

Part 8 — Behavioural Experiments

Core CBT · Module 8

What is the therapist trying to accomplish?

To let the client test a belief or prediction in real life and learn from experience. Experiential learning changes beliefs far more powerfully than verbal disputation — this is often the most potent technique in CBT. It also unites the behavioural and cognitive strands.

The structure of a behavioural experiment

  1. Purpose & target belief: which thought/rule are we testing? (e.g. "Nobody cares about me.")
  2. Specific prediction: operationalise it — what exactly do you predict will happen, and how likely (0–100%)? ("If I text two friends, none will reply within a day" — 90%.)
  3. Design the experiment: what will you do, when, where, how will you record the outcome? Include an alternative belief to test against ("some people do care").
  4. Anticipate obstacles & safety behaviours (things that would rig the result).
  5. Do it & collect data.
  6. Review outcome vs prediction.
  7. Draw the learning & update the belief (re-rate).

Do not let experiments become reassurance

A behavioural experiment tests reality and lets the client update their own belief; reassurance hands the client relief supplied by the therapist and keeps them dependent. Signs you've slipped into reassurance: the client repeatedly asks "but will it be okay?" and you keep answering; the "experiment" is really you promising a good outcome. Instead: "What's your prediction? Let's find out — and you record what actually happens." Drop safety behaviours (over-preparing, seeking reassurance, escape routes) or the test is rigged.

Therapist"You believe 'nobody cares.' Rather than me trying to talk you out of it, shall we test it? What's your honest prediction if you text two friends?"

Client"Neither will reply. Maybe 85% sure."

Therapist"Great — a clear prediction. Text them today exactly as you would, no pre-apologising, and note what happens. We'll look at the results next week and see how the belief holds up."

Mistakes to avoid

Part 9 — Problem-Solving Therapy (PST)

Core CBT · Module 9

What & why

Depression frequently coexists with real, unsolved life problems (debt, conflict, workload) and with a demoralised problem orientation ("nothing will help; why try?"). PST teaches a structured method to tackle solvable problems, restoring agency. Moderate–Strong evidence (Cuijpers et al. meta-analysis; D'Zurilla & Nezu). It pairs naturally with BA and is well-suited to Indian primary-care and time-limited settings.

The steps

  1. Positive problem orientation: reframe problems as normal and (often) solvable; "a problem is a challenge, not proof I'm useless."
  2. Problem identification & definition: state the problem concretely and specifically ("I owe ₹40,000 and can't meet this month's payment"), separate facts from assumptions, and set a realistic goal.
  3. Break large problems down: divide into smaller, workable sub-problems.
  4. Brainstorming: generate many options without judging (quantity first; even "silly" ideas).
  5. Evaluate options: weigh pros/cons, effort, likely effectiveness, and impact on self/others.
  6. Decision-making: choose one or a combination.
  7. Action planning: concrete steps, who/what/when.
  8. Implementation.
  9. Review outcomes: did it work? refine or pick the next option — reinforcing that effort matters.

Part 10 — Self-Criticism, Low Self-Esteem, Guilt & Shame

Targeted · Module 10

Why this needs its own module

Self-attack, worthlessness, shame and guilt are among the most painful and treatment-resistant features of depression, and standard cognitive disputation sometimes backfires (the self-critic argues back, or the client feels invalidated). This module blends CBT with self-compassion / compassion-focused methods. Emerging–Moderate evidence: self-compassion interventions and CFT reduce self-criticism, shame and depressive symptoms (meta-analyses).

Presentations & matched techniques

PresentationHelpful approaches
Excessive self-criticismDouble-standard technique; compassionate-self imagery; noticing the "inner critic" as a habit, not truth.
WorthlessnessCore-belief work (Part 7); positive data log; continuum of "worth".
Shame (global "I am bad")Compassion-focused therapy (CFT): understanding the "tricky brain", the three-circles model (threat/drive/soothing), building the soothing system.
Guilt (specific "I did bad")Responsibility pie chart (reattribution); test the "should"; repair where realistic; distinguish guilt (behaviour) from shame (self).

Key techniques in practice

Responsibility pie (for guilt / self-blame)

List all factors that contributed to a bad outcome, allot each a slice of a 100% pie, and add the client's share last. Clients are routinely startled that their slice is small once all causes are visible.

Compassionate-self & double standard (for self-criticism/shame)

Therapist"You said 'I'm pathetic for struggling.' If your closest friend were this depressed and said that about themselves, what would you say to them?"

Client"I'd say they're ill, not pathetic, and they're doing their best."

Therapist"What's it like to consider offering yourself the same understanding you'd give them?"

CFT explains that self-criticism often feels protective ("it keeps me from getting lazy/rejected") but keeps the threat system inflamed; the work is to build the soothing/affiliative system (soothing rhythm breathing, compassionate imagery, compassionate letter-writing) — not to "win" an argument with the critic.

Guilt vs shame — a crucial distinction

Guilt = "I did something bad" (about behaviour; can motivate repair). Shame = "I am bad" (about the self; corrosive, drives hiding). Convert global shame into specific, addressable guilt where appropriate, and treat shame with compassion-based methods.

Watch-outs

With high self-critics, going straight for compassion can trigger "fear of self-compassion" ("I don't deserve it / it's weak"). Name and normalise this; go gently and gradually. Don't argue with the critic; change the client's relationship to it.

Key concepts

Blend CBT with self-compassion/CFT · guilt (behaviour) vs shame (self) · responsibility pie for blame · build the soothing system, don't debate the critic · watch for fear of self-compassion.

Clinical pearls

Add the client's slice to the pie last. · "What would you say to a friend?" is your bridge to self-compassion. · Perfectionism yields best to behavioural experiments on "good enough".

Part 11 — Rumination & Avoidance

Targeted · Module 11

Why this matters

Rumination (repetitive, passive dwelling on problems, causes and feelings) is a core engine that prolongs and deepens depression and predicts relapse (Nolen-Hoeksema). Avoidance (behavioural and cognitive) is its behavioural twin. Both are processes, so we target the how of thinking, not only its content.

Productive processing vs unproductive rumination

"Why am I like this? What's wrong with me?" (abstract, evaluative)"What specifically can I do about X, and how?" (concrete, action-focused)

Part 12 — Interpersonal Techniques & IPT

Targeted · Module 12

What & why

Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) is a first-line, well-established therapy for depression that works on the link between current relationships/roles and mood. You don't have to be a full CBT therapist to recognise when a client's depression is chiefly interpersonal — and IPT techniques enrich any therapist's repertoire. Strong evidence (Cuijpers et al. comprehensive meta-analysis, 2016; effective acutely and in relapse prevention; comparable to CBT).

Theory, structure & course

Theoretical basis: depression is understood in an interpersonal context — not "caused by" personality but occurring in relation to current life events and relationships. Improve the interpersonal situation and communication, and depression lifts. Rooted in attachment and interpersonal theory (Klerman & Weissman).

Structure: time-limited (typically ~12–16 sessions), in three phases:

  • Initial (1–3): assess depression & give the "sick role", take an interpersonal inventory (key relationships), and select one (or two) focal problem area(s).
  • Middle: work the chosen focal area with IPT techniques.
  • Termination: consolidate gains, review, anticipate future, address ending (itself a role transition).

The four focal problem areas

AreaWhen chosenFocus
Grief (complicated bereavement)Depression linked to a death/lossFacilitate mourning; reconstruct the relationship; re-engage with life/relationships.
Role transitionsDepression around a life change (job loss, migration, marriage, parenthood, retirement, illness)Mourn the old role, develop the new one, build mastery and support in it.
Interpersonal disputesConflict with partner/family/colleagueClarify the dispute stage, expectations, communication; renegotiate or resolve.
Interpersonal deficits / sensitivitiesLongstanding social isolation, few relationshipsReduce isolation; build social skills and new connections (uses the therapy relationship as practice).

Major techniques

  • Communication analysis: examine a specific recent conversation in detail — what was said, meant, heard — to spot unhelpful patterns.
  • Role-play: rehearse difficult conversations (e.g. expressing a need to a spouse).
  • Decision analysis: weigh interpersonal options.
  • Encouragement of affect: help the client access and express feelings appropriately.
  • Assertiveness & expression of needs.
  • Interpersonal inventory & relationship patterns: mapping supports and recurring dynamics.
  • Mobilising social support.

Therapist"Your low mood began around the time you moved cities for marriage — a big role transition. In IPT we'd look at what you lost in that change (your job, your friends, your independence) and how to build a satisfying new role here, with support. Would it help to focus there?"

How IPT differs from CBT

IPT does not primarily target automatic thoughts or run thought records; it targets relationships, roles and communication in the here-and-now. Both are structured and time-limited; both are first-line. Choose IPT when an interpersonal problem area is the clear driver, when the client is more relationally than cognitively oriented, or per preference/availability.

Therapist skills & common mistakes

Skills: maintaining the interpersonal focus, linking mood to interpersonal events week to week, and staying warm but structured. Mistakes: drifting into open-ended exploration without an interpersonal focus; choosing too many focal areas; neglecting the mood–event link; treating IPT as "just support".

Key concepts

Mood ↔ current relationships/roles · time-limited, 3 phases, interpersonal inventory · four focal areas (grief, transitions, disputes, deficits) · communication analysis + role-play · strong evidence; differs from CBT by focusing on relationships not cognitions.

Part 13 — Other Evidence-Based Therapies (Structured Overview)

Targeted · Module 13

Purpose

To give you a map of the therapies with evidence for depression, so you can recognise their logic, know when a clinician might choose one over CBT, and — importantly — not assume all are equally supported. Each card answers: theory · evidence · indications · structure · techniques · therapist skills · materials · limitations · how it differs from CBT · when to choose.

CBT / Cognitive Therapy Strong

Theory: cognition & behaviour maintain depression. Evidence: the most extensively researched; first-line in NICE & VA/DoD. Structure: ~12–20 sessions, structured. Techniques: Parts 4–8. Differs: the reference standard here. Choose: default first-line, esp. with prominent negative cognition.

Behavioural Activation Strong

Theory: loss of reinforcement + avoidance. Evidence: strong; non-inferior to CBT; works in Indian primary care (HAP). Structure: ~8–16 sessions, simple. Techniques: Part 5. Differs from CBT: minimal cognitive work. Choose: severe/low-engagement clients, non-specialist/low-resource settings, trainee-friendly.

Interpersonal Psychotherapy Strong

Theory: relationships/roles & mood. Evidence: strong; comparable to CBT. Structure: ~12–16 sessions, 3 phases. Techniques: Part 12. Differs: targets relationships not cognitions. Choose: interpersonal driver (grief, transition, dispute, isolation).

Problem-Solving Therapy Moderate–Strong

Theory: problem orientation & skills. Evidence: good meta-analytic support; brief. Structure: ~4–12 sessions. Techniques: Part 9. Differs: narrower, skills-focused. Choose: depression tied to concrete life problems; primary care; time-limited.

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy Strong (relapse prevention)

Theory: relapse involves reactivation of ruminative/avoidant modes; decentering from thoughts prevents this. Evidence: strong for preventing relapse in recurrent depression (≥3 episodes; IPD meta-analysis, Kuyken 2016); less for acute severe episodes. Structure: 8-week group. Techniques: mindfulness practices, decentering, relapse-signature work. Differs: changes relationship to thoughts, not their content. Choose: recurrent depression in remission, prominent rumination.

Acceptance & Commitment Therapy Moderate

Theory: psychological inflexibility & experiential avoidance; increase acceptance, defusion, values-based action. Evidence: moderate; recognised option. Structure: flexible. Techniques: defusion, acceptance, values, committed action, mindfulness. Differs: doesn't dispute thoughts; changes relationship to them & emphasises values. Choose: stuck in struggle with thoughts/feelings; values-driven clients.

Metacognitive Therapy Emerging

Theory: the Cognitive-Attentional Syndrome (worry/rumination) driven by metacognitive beliefs. Evidence: promising, fewer large trials. Techniques: attention training, detached mindfulness, challenging metacognitions. Differs: targets thinking process, ignores content. Choose: rumination-dominated presentations.

Self-Compassion & Compassion-Focused Therapy Emerging

Theory: over-active threat system + weak soothing system; shame/self-criticism. Evidence: meta-analyses show reduced self-criticism, shame, depressive symptoms; smaller base in primary MDD. Techniques: Part 10. Differs: builds affiliative/soothing system. Choose: high shame/self-criticism.

Short-term psychodynamic therapy Moderate

Theory: unconscious conflict, relational patterns. Evidence: moderate; a guideline option. Differs: less structured, insight-oriented. Choose: preference; unresolved relational/developmental themes.

Brief therapies & counselling Moderate

Evidence: counselling has some support for less severe/subthreshold depression; brief structured therapies useful in stepped care. Choose: mild presentations, first step in stepped care, access constraints.

Do not flatten the evidence

CBT, BA and IPT have the largest, most consistent evidence base for depression. Third-wave and compassion/metacognitive approaches are promising and valuable for specific mechanisms, but their evidence for primary MDD is smaller. Choose transparently, per formulation, severity, client preference, your competence, and availability.

  • Review homework: diary reveals morning inactivity → low mood; a walk lifted mood.
  • Work: present the shared 5-P/maintenance formulation on paper; introduce "act from a plan, not mood"; schedule 2–3 small graded activities balancing pleasure/mastery/values (5.2).
  • Homework: the schedule + continue diary.

Phase 2 — Cognitive work (Sessions 5–9)

  • Homework: daily thought records on "failure" thoughts.

  • Test "there's no point reaching out — nobody cares" and "if I go to the shopkeepers' meeting, they'll look down on me." Design predictions, drop safety behaviours, review (8.1).
  • Outcome typically disconfirms the catastrophic prediction → belief re-rated down.
Session 9 — Progress review & adjust

Part 16 — Therapist Language & Micro-Skills

Delivery · Module 16

Why micro-skills matter

Technique is delivered through language. The same intervention lands very differently depending on wording, timing and tone. Below are effective vs ineffective examples — study the why.

SkillEffective exampleIneffective & why
Open-ended questions"What was that like for you?""Were you sad?" — closed, leads.
Socratic questions"What might be another way to see that?""Don't you think you're overreacting?" — leading, judgemental.
Reflection"It sounds like you felt let down and then blamed yourself.""So you were upset." — flat, misses meaning.
Validation"Given what you believed, it makes complete sense you stayed home.""Everyone feels like that sometimes." — minimising.
Summarising"Let me check I've understood: three things stood out today…"(none) — session drifts, no consolidation.
Gentle challenging"I notice a gap — you say you're useless, yet you cared for your father all year. How do those fit?""That's just your depression talking." — dismissive.
Normalising"Many people with depression withdraw — it's part of the illness, not a flaw.""It's not a big deal." — invalidating.
Agenda setting"What shall we prioritise together today?""So, how's things?" — no structure.
Transitioning"Can we hold that and come back to it? I want to make sure we cover the diary."Abruptly changing topic without signposting.
Introducing homework"What would be a useful experiment to try before next week? How confident are you, 0–100?""Your homework is to do a thought record." — imposed, no buy-in.
Reviewing homework"What did you learn from trying it?"Forgetting to review — signals it doesn't matter.
Handling silenceWait; then "Take your time — what's going through your mind?"Rushing to fill it / interrogating.
Handling cryingPause, soften, "It's okay to feel this here. Take the time you need.""Let's move on." — shames the emotion.
Handling resistance"You seem unsure about this — tell me your reservations." (curiosity)Pushing harder — deepens the rupture.

Socratic questioning — the four levels (Padesky)

1) Informational questions (gather what the client knows), 2) empathic listening, 3) summarising, 4) analytical/synthesising questions ("How does this new information fit with your belief that…?"). Guided discovery ≠ leading the client to your answer; it uncovers their new perspective.

The line between outpatient therapy and higher care

Routine outpatient (with supervision): mild–moderate depression, manageable/low risk, no psychosis, engaged, stable. Needs urgent psychiatric/medical or higher care: active suicidal intent/plan/means, psychosis, severe self-neglect, mania, severe substance withdrawal, rapid deterioration. When in doubt, escalate — safety over completeness.

Part 18 — Cultural & Indian Context

Delivery · Module 18

Why & the anti-stereotype rule

Culturally responsive therapy improves engagement and outcomes — but "culture" is individualised, never assumed. India is enormously diverse; use a cultural formulation (ask this client about their beliefs, family, language) rather than applying blanket assumptions. Evidence: culturally adapted CBT and the India-developed Healthy Activity Program (BA, lay-counsellor delivered) show good outcomes (Patel et al., Lancet 2017); adaptation reviews support tailoring content, language and delivery.

Culturally salient themes to explore (not assume)

ThemeClinical implication
Family involvementFamily is often central to decisions & care; with consent, involve family in psychoeducation & support. Balance against the client's autonomy & confidentiality.
StigmaFear of being labelled "mad"; frame in de-stigmatising, functional terms; normalise; consider language like "stress"/"tension" if that aids engagement, without minimising.
Somatic presentations & idioms of distressDistress often expressed as body symptoms ("gas", "weakness", "heaviness", "tension") — legitimate cultural idioms, not evasion. Work with the idiom, then bridge to the mood–body link.
Religious/spiritual beliefsCan be a major protective/coping resource (faith, prayer, community); integrate respectfully; watch for guilt framed as "punishment/karma".
Gender rolesExpectations around marriage, dowry pressures, in-law dynamics, women's autonomy, men's "provider" role (cf. Rahul); explore, don't presume.
Socio-economic barriersCost, travel, time off work → favour brief, low-intensity, session-efficient approaches (BA, PST); flexible scheduling.
Joint-family systemsBoth support and stressor; privacy for sessions may be limited; problem-solve confidentiality.
Privacy/confidentialityFamily may expect information; clarify limits early and sensitively.
Treatment access & task-shiftingShortage of specialists; lay-counsellor-delivered brief treatments (HAP model) are evidence-based and scalable.
LanguageDeliver in the client's preferred language/register; explain concepts in everyday words, not jargon.

Part 19 — From Case Formulation to Treatment Plan (Practice)

Practice · Module 19

How to use these cases

For each fictional case: first formulate it yourself using Worksheet 3.1, working through the 12 steps below. Then open the "model answer" and note what you missed. This is how clinical reasoning is trained — attempt, compare, refine.

The 12 steps: 1 Assessment · 2 Risk · 3 Diagnosis/differential · 4 Formulation · 5 Goals · 6 Treatment hierarchy · 7 Choice of intervention · 8 Session planning · 9 Worksheet selection · 10 Homework · 11 Progress monitoring · 12 Relapse prevention.

Case 1 — Mild (Sunita, 29)

Teacher; 6 weeks of low mood after a miscarriage; tearful, guilty ("my fault"), sleeping poorly, still working and seeing family. PHQ-9 = 9. No suicidal ideation. No bipolar/psychosis/substance issues.

Try it yourself, then open the model answer

Risk: low but assess guilt/hopelessness & monitor. Dx/differential: mild MDD vs adjustment vs grief — the miscarriage is a real loss; watch the guilt theme. Formulation: loss (precipitant) activating "I failed / it's my fault" (guilt/self-blame); maintained by rumination and mild withdrawal; protective: supportive family, working, help-seeking. Goals: reduce self-blame, restore sleep & enjoyment, process the loss. Hierarchy/intervention: low-intensity — psychoeducation, light BA (pleasure/values), grief-sensitive cognitive work on guilt (responsibility pie), consider IPT-grief. Worksheets: mood log, activity schedule, responsibility pie, thought record. Monitoring: PHQ-9 fortnightly. Relapse prevention: brief. What trainees often miss: mislabelling normal grief as MDD (or vice versa), and not addressing the guilt attribution directly.

Case 2 — Moderate (Imran, 45)

Accountant; 4 months low mood, anhedonia, poor concentration threatening his job, withdrawn from friends, "burden to everyone", occasional passive thoughts of death, no plan. PHQ-9 = 18. Drinks more than before. Third low period in his life.

Model answer

Risk: assess passive ideation fully, hopelessness, protective factors; monitor closely; supervision. Dx: recurrent MDD, moderate; screen bipolar & medical; note alcohol. Formulation: recurrent vulnerability + work stress (precipitant) → withdrawal, rumination, alcohol, "burden" belief → maintaining cycle. Hierarchy: (1) risk + reduce alcohol; (2) BA; (3) cognitive work on "burden/worthlessness"; (4) beliefs; (5) relapse prevention (recurrent). Intervention: full CBT; coordinate re: medication given severity/recurrence. Worksheets: BA suite, 7-column records, behavioural experiments, positive data log, relapse plan. Monitoring: PHQ-9 each session. Missed by trainees: under-weighting alcohol as maintaining + risk factor; not planning relapse prevention despite recurrence.

Case 3 — Severe (Lakshmi, 34)

Homemaker; 2 months, barely leaving bed, not eating well, profound anhedonia, believes she has "ruined her children's lives", says "they'd be better off without me", poor sleep, no energy. PHQ-9 = 24. Item 9 positive. Possible guilt of near-delusional intensity.

Model answer

Risk: HIGH — active-passing ideation + severe hopelessness + possible near-delusional guilt + dependants. Immediate action: full risk assessment, do not leave alone, involve family with consent, urgent psychiatric assessment same day, safety plan, means restriction; assess for psychotic features. Level of care: likely needs psychiatric input ± medication ± higher care — beyond routine trainee outpatient CBT alone. Formulation: severe episode with guilt/worthlessness; too slowed for cognitive work initially. Intervention once safe: begin with minimal BA + coordinate medication; defer cognitive restructuring. Missed by trainees: proceeding with routine CBT instead of escalating; missing possible psychotic-level guilt; not involving psychiatry/family.

The reasoning habit

Notice the pattern: severity + risk + formulation jointly determine level of care and sequencing. Mild → low-intensity, cognitive/grief work fine. Severe → safety and psychiatry first, behavioural before cognitive, escalation over ambition.

Part 21 — Ethics & Scope of Practice

Practice · Module 21

Frame

Ethical practice is not an add-on; it is the container for everything else. As a trainee, the recurring principle is: practise within your competence, under supervision, and refer/escalate when a situation exceeds your scope.

DomainWhat it requires · trainee note
Informed consentExplain the approach, likely course, risks/benefits, confidentiality limits, that you are a trainee under supervision, and voluntariness. Ongoing, not one-off.
Confidentiality & its limitsProtect information; explain limits (risk to self/others, legal, supervision) at the start. In family-oriented settings (Part 18), clarify what is shared.
DocumentationAccurate, timely, objective notes; risk assessments & decisions recorded; store securely.
Risk managementAssess, respond, document, escalate; know local crisis pathways; never manage serious risk alone as a trainee.
Boundaries & dual relationshipsMaintain professional boundaries; avoid dual roles; manage gifts/social contact thoughtfully (culturally aware but boundaried).
CompetencePractise only what you're trained/supervised for; specialist presentations (psychosis, trauma, personality) → refer/co-work.
SupervisionCentral to trainee practice; bring risk, ruptures, stuck cases, and your own reactions. Non-negotiable.
ReferralKnow when & how — severity, risk, non-response, out-of-scope.
Psychiatric consultation & medication coordinationCoordinate for moderate-severe/recurrent/high-risk depression; you don't prescribe, but you liaise and monitor.
EmergenciesHave a plan & contacts ready before you need them.
Cultural competenceIndividualised, humble, curious (Part 18); avoid stereotyping; use interpreters properly when needed.
Appropriate for a trainee (under supervision) vs not

Appropriate under supervision: assessment, formulation, delivering CBT/BA/PST/IPT techniques for mild–moderate depression, measurement-based monitoring, safety planning as part of a supervised plan. Not for independent trainee practice: managing high/imminent suicide risk alone, treating psychosis, standalone trauma processing, complex personality work, prescribing, or making sole decisions about level of care. Escalate these.
