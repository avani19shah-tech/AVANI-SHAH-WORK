RESEARCH SYNOPSIS

TITLE

"Demoralization in HIV-Positive versus HIV-Negative Opioid-Dependent Inpatients: A Projective Assessment Using the Indian Adaptation of TAT (Uma Chaudhary)"

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

Opioid dependence remains one of the most significant public health challenges in India. According to recent data, India has approximately 850,000 people who inject drugs (PWID), most of whom are at high risk for acquiring HIV (World Drug Report, 2024). The HIV prevalence among PWID in India ranges from 4.5% to 28% depending on the region, with the highest rates in the northeastern states (Manipur, Nagaland) and parts of Punjab. In many de-addiction centres across rural India, patients discover their HIV-positive status for the first time upon admission when routine blood investigations are conducted.

A common clinical observation made by treating psychiatrists and psychologists at de-addiction centres is that upon receiving an HIV-positive diagnosis, many opioid-dependent patients exhibit a dramatic shift in their attitude toward recovery. These patients often verbalize beliefs such as: "If I am HIV positive, there is no point in stopping drugs. I am going to die anyway, so I might as well continue using." This response reflects a collapse of motivation for recovery, a loss of perceived meaning in sobriety, and a fatalistic acceptance of an early death — all of which constitute the core elements of demoralization.

1.2 Demoralization: The Construct

Demoralization, as originally described by Jerome Frank (1974) and later refined by Kissane and colleagues (2001, 2004), is a clinical syndrome characterized by:

  • A persistent inability to cope with stressors
  • Feelings of helplessness and hopelessness
  • A loss of meaning and purpose in life
  • Subjective incompetence (feeling unable to influence one's situation)
  • Existential distress and diminished self-esteem

Critically, demoralization is distinct from clinical depression. While depressed patients experience anhedonia (inability to experience pleasure), demoralized patients can enjoy present moments but have lost all hope about the future (Kissane, 2002; Clarke & Kissane, 2002). A demoralized patient may laugh at a joke today but firmly believes "there is no point trying because nothing will change." This distinction makes demoralization particularly relevant to the clinical scenario described above — opioid-dependent patients who learn of their HIV status are not necessarily depressed, but they have experienced a collapse of perceived futurity and purposefulness.

Meta-analyses indicate that demoralization affects approximately 30% of the medically ill and 50% of the mentally ill (Robinson et al., 2022). Prevalence rates in cancer patients range from 36% to 52% (Kissane et al., 2023). However, despite its high clinical relevance, demoralization has never been studied in:

  1. Opioid-dependent populations
  2. HIV-positive individuals in Indian settings
  3. Rural de-addiction centre inpatients
  4. Using projective assessment techniques

1.3 Projective Assessment and Demoralization

While self-report measures such as the Demoralization Scale-II (DS-II; Robinson & Kissane, 2016) exist, they are limited by:

  • Social desirability bias (patients may not admit to "giving up")
  • Language and literacy barriers (particularly in rural Indian populations)
  • Cultural reluctance to disclose hopelessness directly
  • Inability to capture unconscious/implicit aspects of meaning-making

The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), particularly the Indian Adaptation by Dr. Uma Chaudhary (1960), overcomes these limitations by eliciting spontaneous narratives in response to culturally relevant ambiguous pictures. Through story-telling, patients project their implicit beliefs about the future, their sense of agency, their perceived ability to cope, and their fundamental orientation toward life or surrender — all core components of demoralization.

The Uma Chaudhary Indian Adaptation consists of 14 cards depicting Indian characters and cultural scenarios, making it more ecologically valid for the present study population than the original Murray TAT. The cards include depictions of Indian family structures, social situations, and religious contexts that are familiar to rural Indian populations.

1.4 Need for the Study

Despite the well-documented intersection of HIV and opioid dependence in India, and the widely observed clinical phenomenon of "giving up" upon learning HIV status, no study has:

  1. Formally assessed demoralization in this specific dual-diagnosis population
  2. Compared demoralization between HIV-positive and HIV-negative opioid-dependent patients
  3. Used projective techniques to assess implicit demoralization themes
  4. Explored this phenomenon in rural Indian de-addiction settings

The present study addresses all of these gaps simultaneously.

2. REVIEW OF LITERATURE

2.1 Demoralization in Medical Illness

Kissane et al. (2001) proposed demoralization syndrome as a diagnostic entity distinct from major depression, characterized by hopelessness, loss of meaning, and existential distress in palliative care settings. Clarke & Kissane (2002) demonstrated that demoralization can be differentiated from anhedonic depression and grief in severely ill patients. Fava et al. (2005) documented high prevalence of demoralization among medically ill patients using the Diagnostic Criteria for Psychosomatic Research (DCPR), finding it more common than major depression. A 2020 study on demoralization in rural Australia found that chronic illness significantly predicted demoralization scores, with rural isolation as an aggravating factor.

2.2 HIV and Psychological Distress

Lyons et al. (2001) found that purpose in life was lower in PLHIV than normative samples, and was a stronger predictor of depressive symptoms than HIV disease severity. Avoidance, meaning deficits, and grief have been identified as key psychosocial factors influencing disengagement from HIV care. Intersectional stigma of HIV and substance use creates compounded demoralization effects, particularly in rural settings. Suicidal ideation has been documented among injecting drug users in India, suggesting underlying demoralization processes (Sarin & Singh, 2013).

2.3 Opioid Dependence and Psychological States

Future time perspective is foreshortened in heroin/opioid addicts compared to controls (Lennings, 1993). Recovery capital studies in India suggest that opioid-dependent patients have lower psychological resources for recovery compared to alcohol-dependent patients. Post-incarceration relapse among HIV-positive opioid users is associated with "lack of concern for HIV care" and a fatalistic orientation.

2.4 Projective Techniques in Substance Use Research

Projective techniques have been used with children of drug addicts to assess family dynamics. TAT has been used to assess implicit motives (achievement, affiliation, power) in various clinical populations (McClelland, 1987; Schultheiss & Brunstein, 2010). The Rorschach has been applied to alcohol addiction treatment planning. However, no study has used TAT to assess demoralization themes in any substance use or HIV population.

2.5 Uma Chaudhary's Indian Adaptation of TAT

Developed in 1960 at the Department of Anthropology, Indian Museum, Calcutta. Consists of 14 cards depicting Indian characters, modified from Murray's original to reflect Indian cultural patterns. Includes cards representing joint family dynamics, religious fantasy, and social situations specific to Indian culture. Uses live models and imaginary pictures culturally suited to Indian populations. Widely used in Indian clinical and research settings for personality assessment.

3. AIM OF THE STUDY

To assess and compare demoralization (as reflected through projective narrative themes) in HIV-positive versus HIV-negative opioid-dependent inpatients at a rural de-addiction centre, using the Indian Adaptation of TAT (Uma Chaudhary).

4. OBJECTIVES

  1. To assess the level of demoralization themes in TAT narratives of HIV-positive opioid-dependent inpatients.
  2. To assess the level of demoralization themes in TAT narratives of HIV-negative opioid-dependent inpatients.
  3. To compare demoralization themes between the two groups.
  4. To explore the qualitative content of TAT stories in relation to themes of meaning, purpose, helplessness, and perceived futurity.
  5. To examine the relationship between demoralization scores and socio-clinical variables (duration of opioid use, time since HIV diagnosis, number of past treatment attempts).

5. HYPOTHESES

H1: HIV-positive opioid-dependent inpatients will demonstrate significantly higher demoralization scores on TAT narrative analysis compared to HIV-negative opioid-dependent inpatients.

H2: TAT narratives of HIV-positive patients will show significantly greater themes of foreshortened future, helplessness, and loss of meaning/purpose compared to HIV-negative patients.

H3: There will be a significant positive correlation between duration since HIV diagnosis and demoralization scores among HIV-positive patients.

6. METHODOLOGY

6.1 Research Design

Cross-sectional, comparative (two-group) design.

6.2 Setting

Inpatient de-addiction centre in a rural area.

6.3 Participants

Sample Size

GroupDescriptionN
Group A (HIV-Positive)Opioid-dependent inpatients who are HIV-positive30
Group B (HIV-Negative)Opioid-dependent inpatients who are HIV-negative30
Total60

Sampling Method

Purposive sampling

Inclusion Criteria (Both Groups)

  1. Age: 18–50 years
  2. Gender: Male
  3. Diagnosis of Opioid Dependence Syndrome as per ICD-10 (F11.2)
  4. Currently admitted as inpatient at the de-addiction centre
  5. Minimum 7 days of inpatient stay (to ensure detoxification and cognitive clarity)
  6. Rural background (residing in a rural area as defined by Census of India)
  7. Willing to give informed consent

Additional Inclusion for Group A

  • Confirmed HIV-positive status (documented by ELISA/rapid test at the centre)
  • HIV diagnosis received during or after admission to the de-addiction centre (i.e., newly diagnosed at the centre OR diagnosed within the past 6 months)

Exclusion Criteria (Both Groups)

  1. Active psychosis or severe cognitive impairment
  2. Co-morbid dependence on substances other than opioids and tobacco (polysubstance use excluding tobacco)
  3. Pre-existing diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder (to differentiate demoralization from depression)
  4. History of intellectual disability
  5. Medical condition causing acute distress unrelated to HIV/opioid use (e.g., acute surgical emergency)
  6. Inability to communicate adequately in the local language

B. Thematic Apperception Test — Indian Adaptation (Uma Chaudhary, 1960)

Cards Selected: 6 cards from the 14-card set, chosen for their relevance to themes of coping, future orientation, interpersonal connection, and existential meaning.

Card No.Theme ElicitedRationale for Selection
Card 1Achievement, aspiration (tanpura)Elicits themes of future goals, motivation, purpose
Card 2Rural/agricultural scene, familyElicits themes of social connection vs. alienation, meaning through relationships

Administration:

  • Individual administration in a quiet room
  • Standard instructions as per Uma Chaudhary's manual
  • Each card presented for 20 seconds of observation
  • Participant asked to tell a story: "What is happening? What led up to this? What are the characters feeling? What will happen next?"
  • Stories audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim
  • Time allowed per card: 5 minutes maximum
  • Total administration time: approximately 30–40 minutes

C. Demoralization Scoring Framework for TAT Narratives (Researcher-Developed)

Each story scored on 5 dimensions of demoralization, rated on a 3-point scale (0, 1, 2):

DimensionScore 0 (Absent)Score 1 (Partial)Score 2 (Prominent)
1. Loss of Meaning/PurposeCharacter has clear goals, life feels meaningfulGoals are vague or questionedExplicit purposelessness — "no point", "why bother"
2. HelplessnessCharacter takes active steps, demonstrates agencySome struggle; character wavers or feels uncertainComplete inability to act; "nothing can be done"
3. Giving Up / SurrenderCharacter perseveres despite difficultyCharacter considers giving up but is conflictedCharacter abandons effort entirely; capitulation
4. Foreshortened FuturePositive or open-ended future outcome in storyFuture is uncertain or absent from narrativeDeath, ending, or "no future" explicitly stated
5. Perceived EntrapmentCharacter perceives multiple options and choicesLimited options perceived; constraints acknowledgedComplete entrapment — "no way out", "stuck forever"

Scoring:

  • Each of 6 cards scored on all 5 dimensions
  • Maximum score per card: 10 (5 dimensions × 2)
  • Total possible Demoralization Score: 60 (6 cards × 10)
  • Score interpretation:
    • 0–15: Low Demoralization
    • 16–35: Moderate Demoralization
    • 36–60: High Demoralization

Inter-Rater Reliability:

  • Two trained raters will independently score all protocols
  • Cohen's Kappa and Intra-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) will be calculated
  • Training sessions with practice protocols will be conducted prior to data collection
  • Disagreements resolved through consensus discussion

D. Sacks Sentence Completion Test (SSCT) — Selected Stems (Supplementary Tool)

To triangulate findings from TAT (implicit/projective) with a semi-structured projective measure. The following 12 stems will be administered:

Self-Concept Related:

  1. My future seems...
  2. I feel that life...
  3. When I think about my health...
  4. I believe I am able to...
  5. The thing I want most in life is...
  6. If I could start my life over...

Goals and Purpose Related:

  1. My greatest wish...
  2. What keeps me going is...
  3. I would be perfectly happy if...
  4. My biggest fear is...

Perceived Futurity Related:

  1. In five years, I see myself...
  2. Death makes me think of...

Scoring: Qualitative content analysis of responses, categorized into:

  • Positive/Hopeful responses
  • Ambivalent/Uncertain responses
  • Negative/Demoralized responses

6.5 Procedure

  1. Ethical Approval: Obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee.
  2. Permission: Obtained from the Medical Superintendent/Head of the de-addiction centre.
  3. Identification of Participants: Patients meeting inclusion criteria identified from admission records with assistance from treating team.
  4. Informed Consent: Written informed consent obtained from each participant after explaining the nature and purpose of the study in the local language. Emphasis placed on voluntary participation and confidentiality.
  5. Assessment Sequence:
    • Session 1 (Day 1): Socio-demographic and clinical data collection (15–20 minutes)
    • Session 2 (Day 2 or 3): TAT administration (30–40 minutes)
    • Session 3 (Same day as TAT or next day): SSCT administration (15–20 minutes)
  6. Transcription: Audio recordings of TAT stories transcribed verbatim within 48 hours.
  7. Scoring: Two independent raters score TAT transcripts using the Demoralization Scoring Framework. Raters blinded to group membership (HIV status).
  8. Data Entry and Analysis.

6.6 Ethical Considerations

  1. Informed consent obtained from all participants.
  2. Right to withdraw at any time without affecting treatment.
  3. Confidentiality of HIV status and all personal information maintained.
  4. No identifying information on test protocols — coding system used.
  5. Participants experiencing significant distress during assessment offered immediate psychological support and referral.
  6. Findings shared with treating team (with patient consent) if clinically indicated.
  7. No financial compensation; however, participation acknowledged.

8. EXPECTED OUTCOMES

  1. HIV-positive opioid-dependent patients are expected to demonstrate significantly higher demoralization scores compared to HIV-negative opioid-dependent patients.
  2. Themes of "foreshortened future" and "giving up" are expected to be markedly more prominent in the TAT narratives of HIV-positive patients.
  3. SSCT responses of HIV-positive patients are expected to reflect more fatalistic, purposeless, and trapped self-concepts.
  4. Duration since HIV diagnosis may correlate with demoralization — patients diagnosed more recently may show more acute demoralization.
  5. The study will establish the utility of TAT as a culturally sensitive projective tool for assessing demoralization in Indian clinical populations.

9. IMPLICATIONS OF THE STUDY

Clinical Implications

  • Identification of demoralization as a distinct therapeutic target (separate from depression) in HIV-positive opioid users.
  • Helps explain why newly HIV-diagnosed patients relapse or refuse treatment — the issue is not "lack of willpower" but a collapse of perceived meaning.
  • Informs the development of meaning-centered interventions (e.g., Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy, Dignity Therapy) adapted for Indian de-addiction settings.
  • Highlights the need for psychological preparation before HIV testing and structured post-test counselling in de-addiction centres.

Research Implications

  • First study to assess demoralization through projective techniques.
  • Establishes a novel TAT scoring framework for demoralization that can be used in future research.
  • Opens avenues for studying demoralization in other chronic illness + addiction populations (e.g., Hepatitis C + opioid dependence).

Policy Implications

  • Supports the integration of mental health services within de-addiction and ART centres.
  • Advocates for mandatory psychological assessment (beyond depression screening) upon HIV diagnosis disclosure in de-addiction settings.
  • Highlights the unmet psycho-existential needs of rural populations with co-morbid HIV and substance dependence.

10. LIMITATIONS

  1. Small sample size (N=60) limits generalizability.
  2. Cross-sectional design prevents causal inference.
  3. Male-only sample limits generalization to female opioid users.
  4. Projective scoring involves inherent subjectivity, partially addressed by inter-rater reliability.
  5. Newly developed scoring framework lacks prior validation data — this is an exploratory instrument.
  6. Rural setting may limit recruitment pace.
  7. Results may not generalize to urban populations or non-injecting opioid users.

APPENDIX A: TAT Demoralization Scoring Manual (Brief Version)

Instructions for Raters

  1. Read the entire story for each card before scoring.
  2. Score each dimension independently.
  3. Base scoring on the overall narrative thrust, not isolated words.
  4. When in doubt between scores (e.g., 1 vs 2), consider whether the demoralization theme is central to the story (score 2) or peripheral (score 1).
  5. The presence of a resolution or positive ending reduces the dimension score.
  6. Score based on the PROTAGONIST'S experience (the character the patient identifies with).

Scoring Examples

Dimension: Foreshortened Future

  • Score 0 (Absent): "He will study hard and one day become a doctor. His family will be proud."
  • Score 1 (Partial): "He is thinking about tomorrow... but he doesn't know what will happen. Maybe it will be okay, maybe not."
  • Score 2 (Prominent): "There is no tomorrow for him. He knows he will die soon. Nothing matters anymore."

Dimension: Giving Up / Surrender

  • Score 0 (Absent): "Despite the difficulties, he decided to try one more time."
  • Score 1 (Partial): "He thought about giving up but his mother's face came to mind. He is confused."
  • Score 2 (Prominent): "He threw everything away. He stopped trying. What is the use?"
