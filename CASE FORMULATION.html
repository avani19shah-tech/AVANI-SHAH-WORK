UNIT I — Introduction to Psychotherapy

1. Definition and nature of psychotherapy

FROM SOURCES The word psychotherapy has Greek roots — psyche (mind/soul) and therapeutikos (healing/attending to). Daniel Hack Tuke first used “psychotherapeutics” in 1872, and in his dictionary (Tuke, 1892) defined psychotherapy as “treatment of disease by the influence of the mind on the body.” Historically, Breuer’s patient Anna O. is credited with coining the phrase “talking cure.” (S1, S2)

Traditionally the term refers to the treatment of mental disorders by psychological techniques within a client–therapist relationship. Three classic definitions anchor the concept:

  • Wolberg (1967): psychotherapy is “a form of treatment for problems of an emotional nature in which a trained person deliberately establishes a professional relationship with a patient for the purpose of (a) removing, modifying or retarding existing symptoms, (b) mediating disturbed patterns of behaviour, and (c) promoting positive personality growth and development.” (S1)
  • Frank & Frank (1991): psychotherapy comprises three components — a healing agent, a sufferer, and a healing (therapeutic) relationship. (S1)
  • Norcross (1990): the informed and intentional application of clinical methods and interpersonal stances derived from established psychological principles, to assist people to modify their behaviours, cognitions, emotions and other personal characteristics in directions the participants deem desirable. (S2)

FROM SOURCES The mutual cognitive and emotional engagement of client and therapist is the foundation of effective work (S1). Psychotherapy is a unilateral professional relationship, circumscribed by limits on time, frequency, content and intimacy; confidentiality is assured (within ethical/legal limits); and it must not be compromised by unethical dual relationships (S2). An extensive review concluded that roughly 70–80% of those treated improve (Smith et al., 1980; S2).

Why the three verbs matter (conceptual) Wolberg’s “remove / modify / retard” maps onto a realistic hierarchy of goals: cure where possible, symptom modification where cure is blocked (poor motivation, low ego-strength, financial limits), and retardation of decline in malignant conditions such as dementia — where therapy preserves the client’s contact with reality rather than curing. This distinction is a favourite short-note and prevents the naïve equation “therapy = cure”.

2. Scope, objectives and goals

FROM SOURCES Psychotherapy is “more than a talk between two people” — it is a collaborative undertaking on a professional level toward specific objectives (S1). Wolberg’s five objectives are:

  1. Removing existing symptoms — eliminate distressing symptoms.
  2. Modifying existing symptoms — when cure is impractical (motivation, ego-strength, finances).
  3. Retarding existing symptoms — delaying inevitable deterioration (e.g., dementia).
  4. Mediating disturbed patterns of behaviour — correcting disturbed interpersonal/occupational/marital patterns.
  5. Promoting positive personality growth — resolving blocks in psychosocial development toward creative self-fulfilment.

FROM SOURCES Additional aims (S1): strengthen motivation to do the right things; reduce emotional pressure through expression of feeling; release the potential for growth; change maladaptive habits; modify cognitive structure; foster self-knowledge; and facilitate interpersonal relations and communication.

Kleinke’s (1994) six universal goals (S2) Across virtually all psychotherapies: (1) overcoming demoralisation and gaining hope; (2) enhancing mastery and self-efficacy; (3) overcoming avoidance; (4) becoming aware of one’s misconceptions; (5) accepting life’s realities; and (6) achieving insight. These are useful as a “common-factors” checklist in a goals answer.

FROM SOURCES S2 notes an important ethical complication in goal-setting: whose goals? CBT may make patients feel better quickly by removing symptoms, whereas psychodynamic therapy may make patients “feel worse before they feel better.” The therapist and patient may hold different goals (e.g., patient wants all negative emotion gone; therapist wants to reduce excess emotion). This makes informed consent genuinely complex, and it is grounded in respect for autonomy.

3. Factors important to psychotherapy outcome

FROM SOURCES In 1952, Hans Eysenck published a controversial review concluding that ~74% of neurotic patients across 24 studies improved — a rate he claimed was no better than untreated controls over two years. This spurred decades of outcome research (S1). Findings fall into three categories:

(a) Patient variables

It is often the patient who most determines a successful outcome. Relevant variables (Hersen & Sledge, 2002): severity of disturbance (the single most predictive), motivation and expectancy, capacity to relate, degree of integration/coherence, perfectionism, and the ability to recognise and verbalise focalised problems. Types and severity of mental illness also shape outcome (Sadock & Sadock, 2005). (S1)

(b) Therapist variables

Age, emotional well-being and aptitude matter; key qualities include empathy, unconditional positive regard, genuineness, flexibility, objectivity and sensitivity. Popescu (2012) classifies therapist variables as:

  • Relational: empathy, attachment style, unconditional positive regard, respect, warmth, genuineness.
  • Professional: theoretical orientation, training level, experience, view of psychotherapy, activity/neutrality, caseload, supervisory status.
  • Demographic & diversity: age, gender, race, ethnicity, values, client–therapist similarity.
  • Personality: traits, attitudes, flexibility, kindness, trustworthiness, adjustment.
  • Developmental & personal: level of personal development, functioning, self-efficacy, self-directed hostility, interpersonal problems, introjects.

(c) Common factors & specific intervention variables

FROM SOURCES S2 describes psychotherapy as resting on four major common factors: (1) a relationship in which the client trusts a competent, caring therapist; (2) shared expectancy/hope of benefit; (3) an underlying conceptual framework (rationale) with a set of procedures requiring both parties’ participation; and (4) a socially sanctioned healing setting. The integrative movement is validated by “equivalence” findings — therapies from different theoretical traditions are roughly equally effective and clearly superior to no-treatment/placebo (the “Dodo bird” pattern), attributed partly to these shared factors.

4. Training, competence and supervision of therapists

FROM SOURCES Training is a basic component of practitioner competence (“possession of required skill, knowledge, qualification or capacity”). Faculty bear primary responsibility, but trainees share it (S1). Key ethical issues in training:

  • Selection of trainees: clear, published selection criteria; a two-way screening process (faculty screen candidates; candidates screen the programme).
  • Content of a programme: a range of techniques for diverse clients; required areas include professional orientation & ethics, social/cultural diversity, human growth & development, career development, counselling relationships, group work, assessment & testing, and research/programme evaluation. Programmes must weave didactic and experiential learning together, not teach academics alone.
  • Training for a digital culture: online services require knowledge of the ethical/legal complexities of tele-therapy and cross-jurisdiction work.
  • Evaluation & the gatekeeper role: faculty must protect future consumers by identifying and remediating problematic trainees; dismissal is a last resort after remediation fails (Corey, Corey & Corey, 2019).

FROM SOURCES Supervision recurs as a core professional safeguard: therapists should have regular suitable supervision to develop skills, monitor performance and provide accountability; and should seek supervision before any post-termination relationship with a former client (S1).

5. Ethics and the moral principles of practice

FROM SOURCES Each major mental-health organisation has a code of ethics. Common themes (Corey, Corey & Corey, 2019): client welfare; practising within one’s competence; respecting clients’ cultural values; distinguishing personal from professional values; avoiding harm and exploitation; maintaining professional boundaries; protecting confidentiality and privacy; working within an ethical–legal framework; avoiding discrimination; striving for the highest ethical standard; and recognising self-care as a basis for competent practice.

(Avasthi & Grover, 2022)

Tjelveit’s (1999) 14 approaches to ethical reasoning (S2) Casuistry; classic liberal individualism; communitarianism; critical psychology; feminist ethics; hermeneutics; narrative; naturalistic; pragmatic; radical; rational; religious; romantic; and virtue ethics. The key teaching point: ethical dilemmas must be resolved — the therapist’s overarching duty is to make the best-quality decision possible.

6. Legal issues in psychotherapy (with cases)

FROM SOURCES With 450+ schools of psychotherapy, negligence claims have risen. The therapist’s duty to safeguard confidentiality arises from four sources (S2): professional ethical codes; licensure/privilege statutes; developing case law (analogous to attorney–client privilege, though less stringent); and constitutional privacy guarantees.

  • Jaffee v. Redmond (1996): the US Supreme Court recognised a psychotherapist–patient privilege in federal courts — such communications are confidential and need not be disclosed in federal trials (the ruling does not bind all state courts). (S2)
  • Informed consent & risk: all psychotherapies carry risks (e.g., untoward transference reactions, mismanaged transference/countertransference, regressive dependency, temporary worsening). Failure to obtain informed consent can be the basis of malpractice liability. (S2)
Supplementary — Tarasoff & the duty to protect Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1976) established the clinician’s duty to protect/warn identifiable third parties from a serious, foreseeable threat — the classic exception to confidentiality (“protective privilege ends where the public peril begins”). Your sources mention “protecting clients and their potential threatened victims” as a confidentiality exception (S1) and discuss breach of confidentiality for third-party risk (S2); the named case is added here for completeness.

7. Rights and responsibilities in therapy

FROM SOURCES Therapists uphold ethical principles that commit them to promote client welfare and assure client rights (APA, 1977: psychologists respect the dignity and worth of the individual). Rights and responsibilities converge on freedom of choice, disclosure of treatment information, and protection of human dignity (Hare-Mustin et al., 1979). Importantly, assuring clients’ rights places corresponding responsibilities on clients — to choose wisely, use the information provided, and assume control of their participation (Arbuckle, 1977). (S1)

Therapist responsibilities to the client (S1)
  • Undertake therapy only with professional intent; keep contracts realistic and clear.
  • Take reasonable steps to avoid harm; seek supervision or refer when a case exceeds competence.
  • Promote client autonomy; maintain professional boundaries; avoid detrimental dual relationships.
  • Maintain confidentiality for all verbal, written, recorded or computer-stored material (continuing after the client’s death, barring overriding legal considerations).
  • Never engage in sexual relations with a client (any physical contact for sexual gratification); do not exploit clients financially, sexually or emotionally; do not pay/accept referral fees.
  • Obtain consent from a legally authorised person when a client cannot give informed consent; keep publicity accurate.
Responsibilities to self (S1)
  • Maintain one’s effectiveness, resilience and ability to help; monitor personal functioning; refrain from practice when impaired by illness, personal/emotional difficulty, alcohol or drugs; use regular supervision.

8. Informed consent, confidentiality and its limits

FROM SOURCES Informed consent is the first step in protecting client rights — a shared decision-making process in which the practitioner gives adequate information so the client can decide about participating (Corey et al., 2019). It requires that the client understands the information, consents voluntarily, and is competent to consent (Wheeler & Bertram, 2015). Disclosures must be in simple, culturally sensitive language, and the client must have the chance to raise questions. A client merely seeking help is not “implied consent.”

FROM SOURCES Informed-consent content a client should receive (S1): the nature of the therapeutic process (including that long-term work can produce disruptions — e.g., individual progress may disrupt a marriage; Hurvitz, 1967); the right to refuse specific techniques; the assessment process; the therapist’s background and credentials; costs and missed-appointment policy; length of therapy and the termination phase; consultation with colleagues; benefits and risks (especially for non-traditional techniques); alternatives to therapy; recording/live-observation (requires documented consent, with a right to decline); and the right of access to files (clients may legally inspect records — ideally with the clinician present to interpret them).

Confidentiality and its exceptions

FROM SOURCES Confidentiality is the foundation of psychotherapy — without it, clients cannot be expected to reveal damaging information. Exceptions (S1):

  1. Release without patient consent when required — e.g., reporting abuse; protecting clients and threatened third parties; self-defence against threatening client behaviour.
  2. When information must be submitted as evidence in legal proceedings.
  3. When the client threatens the therapist’s life or files a case against the therapist.

In exceptional breaches, attempts should be made to seek the client’s permission first; agreements continue after death unless overridden by law. Fisher (2008/2016) stresses clients have a right to be informed of the limits of confidentiality before consenting (S1).

Consent example Before the first session, Dr. S explains to a new client the fee and missed-appointment policy, the likelihood that early sessions may raise distress before improvement, the confidentiality limits (risk to self/others, abuse reporting, court order), and the right to decline recording. The client asks questions, then signs. This operationalises autonomy, veracity and fidelity.

9. Professional boundaries, record-keeping & documentation

FROM SOURCES There are no absolute answers to boundaries, but certain behaviours are violations in any context — sexual relationships; self-disclosure of personal fantasies/dreams/sexual information; wearing seductive clothing (S1). S2 distinguishes boundary crossings (may be benign or unintended — lateness, altered sessions, self-disclosure, accidental out-of-session contact, physical contact) from boundary violations (therapist-initiated, harmful — abrupt termination, excessive self-disclosure, sexual contact).

Documentation (S1)

  • Keeping no record is unethical; records should be concise, factual, accurate, up-to-date and respectful (distinguishing fact from opinion).
  • Include: notable events in treatment or the patient’s life; clinical observations; efforts to obtain collateral information; investigation/test findings; medication information; suicidal ideation with intent; child abuse; threats to others; consultations; and basic continuity-of-care information.
  • Use clinical judgement for sensitive content (intimate relationships, fantasies, dreams, third-party information); avoid documenting mere hypotheses or speculation.
Where & how long to keep records Data-protection law and the duty of confidentiality require secure storage (locked cabinet, encrypted/password-protected electronic or cloud storage). The retention period varies by context, and clients should be told how long records are kept (Feltham, Hanley & Winter, 2017; S1). A golden rule (S1): write every note as though it may one day be read in court — or by the client.

Booster sessions are periodic additional sessions to assess status and reinforce gains, giving continuity to the targeted behaviour change (N. Sam, 2013).

11. Assessment, case formulation & outcome monitoring

FROM SOURCES Assessment serves two decisions: (1) whether any psychological treatment is appropriate; and (2) which method. These draw on both evidence-based practice (research knowledge) and practice-based evidence (systematic clinical experience). Two processes operate (Margison & Brown, 2007): the idiographic (a unique formulation of this person) and the nomothetic (inferences from research on similar individuals). (S1)

  • Before the interview: contact itself can be therapeutic; a preliminary questionnaire and standardised measures may be used.
  • Malan’s (1979) assessment checklist: think psychiatrically (diagnosis, symptoms, medication); psychodynamically (triangles of conflict and of person); psychotherapeutically (forecasts/predictions); practically (what is possible); take care of the session (rapport); take care of the patient (avoid pointless anxiety); be wary of your impact.
  • Common measures: SCL-90-R (Derogatis, 1983); Inventory of Interpersonal Problems (Horowitz et al., 1988); CORE (Barkham et al., 1998).
  • Model-specific assessment: behavioural therapy → ABC functional analysis of antecedents–behaviours–consequences; cognitive therapy → diaries targeting dysfunctional cognitions; group approaches → the patient’s impact on the group; in-patient/therapeutic-community → multiple assessors.
Formulation as an “anchor point” (S1) A case formulation functions as an anchor for therapy: the therapist keeps it “in the back of the mind” during free-flowing conversation, and periodically reviews progress against agreed goals. Aveline (1980): a formulation should address cause and effect, maintaining factors, factors promoting change, and predictions. Cognitive Analytic Therapy (Ryle, 1990) is the exemplar of formulation-led therapy.

13. Psychotherapy in India

FROM SOURCES Western/scientific psychology entered India in 1905 at Calcutta University; a full department followed in 1915, and Girindrashekhar Bose became the first Indian doctorate in psychology (1921) and founded the Indian Psychoanalytic Society (1922). Post-independence, NIMHANS (Bangalore) and the Central Institute of Psychiatry (Ranchi) became the earliest higher-education centres for psychiatry, psychiatric social work and clinical psychology (Bhatt, 2015). (S1)

FROM SOURCES Culturally, the egalitarian stance of Western therapy has been debated as sometimes unhelpful in the Indian context (Neki, 1979); older indigenous healing practices remain popular; and the Eastern view resists a strict separation of psychology and spirituality (Varghese, 1998). Western psychotherapy can “strengthen an ego which can only then be surrendered in the spiritual quest” (Varma, 2004); mindfulness and yoga are now embedded in many Western therapies. This provides the conceptual bridge to the complementary practices in Unit IV.

UNIT II — The Therapeutic Relationship

1. Meaning, nature and importance

FROM SOURCES The therapeutic relationship is the core vehicle of psychotherapy. S1/S2 treat the mutual cognitive and emotional engagement of client and therapist as the foundation of effective work, and list a trusting relationship with a competent, caring therapist as the first of the four common factors. In psychodynamic/interpersonal approaches the relationship itself becomes the effective therapeutic agent: the therapist offers a “corrective emotional experience”, responding in ways that do not replicate the client’s earlier hurtful or non-supportive relationships, so the client internalises more adaptive relational schemes (S2).

Why the relationship matters (conceptual) The “equivalence”/common-factors literature (S2) suggests that a substantial portion of outcome variance is carried by relational factors shared across schools, rather than by technique alone. Hence the maxim: technique is delivered through the relationship, not instead of it.

2. The therapeutic alliance and its components

FROM SOURCES A good working alliance is considered essential to therapeutic change in cognitive/behavioural as well as humanistic traditions (S2). In person-centred work the alliance is understood through the core conditions, though many hold that these are necessary but not sufficient for change (Bohart, 1990; Tausch, 1990; S2).

Supplementary — Bordin’s (1979) tripartite model of the working alliance The most examinable alliance model (lightly covered in your sources, so added here) describes three interdependent components:
  • Bond — the affective attachment: mutual trust, acceptance, liking and confidence.
  • Goals — agreement on the aims/targets of therapy.
  • Tasks — agreement on the in-session and between-session activities believed to produce change.
A strong alliance exists when bond, goals and tasks are mutually agreed and congruent. Alliance quality early in therapy is one of the most consistent predictors of outcome across modalities.

3. Client characteristics affecting therapy

FROM SOURCES As in Unit I, the client is often the strongest determinant of outcome. Relevant client variables (Hersen & Sledge, 2002; Sadock & Sadock, 2005; S1): severity of disturbance (most predictive), motivation and expectancy, capacity to relate, degree of integration and coherence, perfectionism, psychological-mindedness and capacity for symbolism, and the ability to recognise and verbalise focal problems. Social-support networks and the nature of the presenting problem also influence engagement (S2).

4. Therapist characteristics affecting therapy

FROM SOURCES Popescu’s (2012) five-cluster classification (S1) — relational, professional, demographic/diversity, personality, developmental/personal — organises these variables (see Unit I §3b). The relational cluster (empathy, UPR, warmth, genuineness) is the one most directly tied to a strong alliance.

5. Empathy, genuineness, warmth, acceptance & active listening

FROM SOURCES These are the Rogerian core conditions (fully elaborated in Unit IV §6). S2 provides crisp definitions:

  • Empathy — the ability to subjectively enter the client’s frame of reference, to “walk in their shoes” in the here-and-now, and convey this back to the client. Empathy is not sympathy (sympathy = feeling for; empathy = feeling with + accurate understanding). Carkhuff created a five-point empathy scale, with a Level-5 response as the most additively accurate.
  • Unconditional positive regard (UPR) / acceptance & warmth — prizing the client as a person of worth, without conditions of approval.
  • Genuineness / congruence — the therapist is real, integrated and non-defensive; outer behaviour matches inner experience.
  • Active listening & attending — see Unit III; the behavioural expression of empathy.
Carkhuff’s empathy scale (teaching point) Level 1–2 responses detract from or ignore the client’s expressed feeling; Level 3 is interchangeable (accurately reflects what the client expressed); Levels 4–5 are additive — they go slightly beyond, naming feelings/meanings the client only implied, deepening exploration. Aim for interchangeable-or-above.

FROM SOURCES Risks of mismanagement are real: S2 lists untoward transference reactions, mismanagement of transference/countertransference, and regressive dependency states among the recognised risks of psychotherapy that inform the consent process.

Illustration A client who was criticised harshly by her father becomes unusually anxious to please her male therapist and dreads his “disapproval” (transference). The therapist notices irritation when the client is deferential — mirroring his own impatience with his dependent sibling (countertransference) — and takes this to supervision rather than enacting it.

7. Resistance, trust, motivation & engagement

FROM SOURCES Motivation is notoriously difficult to assess and may be as much a function of social compliance as rational intent (Margison & Brown, 2007; Bloch, 1979); without at least a minimal agreement to attend, therapy is impossible (S1). Resistance can appear as lateness or absence (noted as a marker of mid-therapy negative transference in Mann’s brief therapy; see Unit IV §2) and is one condition that may indicate termination when it becomes an unresolvable impasse (Unit I §10). Trust is built through the core conditions and reliable boundaries; it is the precondition for disclosing “embarrassing, personally damaging information” — the basis of the confidentiality principle (S1).

8. Contextual factors (illness, techniques, culture, expectations)

FROM SOURCES The relationship is shaped by context: cultural differences affect alliance formation and how patients’ beliefs shape their thinking and reactions — different thinking styles must often be accommodated for progress (S2). Expectations (hope of benefit) are a common factor; the practice setting socially sanctioned as a place of healing is another (S2). The nature and severity of the illness shape engagement and the realistic goal (remove/modify/retard; Unit I). The chosen techniques are delivered through the relationship, not in place of it.

9. Alliance ruptures and repair

Supplementary (thin in sources) — ruptures & repair Your sources establish that a good alliance is essential and that resistance/negative transference occur, but do not systematically cover rupture–repair, so this standard material is added and labelled:
  • Rupture = a deterioration or strain in the alliance — a breakdown in collaboration, tension in the bond, or disagreement on goals/tasks.
  • Withdrawal ruptures: the client moves away/against contact (silence, vagueness, appeasement).
  • Confrontation ruptures: the client moves against the therapist (complaints, hostility, pressure).
  • Repair: the therapist non-defensively notices the rupture, invites exploration, acknowledges any contribution, and renegotiates goals/tasks. Successfully repaired ruptures are associated with improved outcomes — the repair itself can be therapeutic (a corrective relational experience).
This dovetails with your sources’ “corrective emotional experience” (S2) and with managing negative transference.

1. Objectives and functions of clinical interviewing

FROM SOURCES Clinical interviewing has been defined narrowly and broadly (S2):

  • Keats (2000): “a controlled situation in which one person, the interviewer, asks a series of questions of another person, the respondent.”

Functions include: establishing rapport and a working relationship; gathering information (presenting problem, history, relationships, psychological-mindedness, motivation); assessment/diagnosis and formulation; and laying the groundwork for intervention. 3. Interviewing microskills

FROM SOURCES S2 details question types and attending behaviour as the backbone of microskills.

(a) Types of questions (S2)

  • Open questions — facilitate verbal output; cannot be answered yes/no; typically begin with How or What (Where/When/Why/Who are sometimes included but are narrower). Purpose: encourage elaboration and exploration.
  • Closed questions — answerable yes/no or with specific facts (often Who/Where/When); restrict verbalisation. Useful to control an over-talkative client or obtain precise diagnostic detail.
  • Swing, indirect/implied, and projective questions — additional types that shape the kind of response elicited.

(b) Attending behaviour — Ivey & Ivey’s four dimensions (S2)

FROM SOURCES Ivey & Ivey (1999) call attending behaviour the foundation of interviewing; it is primarily non-verbal, and “the importance of listening well is spectacularly uncontroversial.” The four dimensions:

  1. Eye contact — more constant while the client speaks, less constant while they are emotionally aroused; varies greatly by culture and individual.
  2. Body language (kinesics & proxemics) — kinesics = movement of body parts (eyes, face, hands); proxemics = personal space/distance. Positive: leaning slightly toward the client, relaxed-but-attentive posture, unobtrusive legs. Mirroring (synchrony) can enhance rapport but, done poorly, is “disastrous” and may feel mocking.
  3. Vocal qualities — matching volume/tone to the client to convey interest, empathy and emphasis; tone can gently lead the client toward content or feeling.
  4. Verbal tracking — accurately following and occasionally repeating key words/phrases; the clearest sign that the interviewer is listening.
Negative attending behaviour (S2): “overuse breeds contempt” Excessive head-nods (the “wobbly spring”), too much eye contact (feels like scrutiny/intimidation), parroting a single last word (client feels over-analysed), and clumsy mirroring (feels like mockery) all inhibit expression. Positive behaviours become negative when overused.

FROM SOURCES Communication is heavily non-verbal (S2, citing Hall). Silence SUPPLEMENTARY is itself a skill: it allows reflection, signals space to continue, gives the client control of pacing, and lets emotion surface. Non-verbal channels (kinesics, proxemics, vocal qualities) often carry the affective truth that words conceal.

FROM SOURCES Positive attending behaviours open up communication and encourage free expression; negative ones inhibit it (S2). Rapport is built by congruent eye contact, warm vocal tone, accurate verbal tracking and well-judged body language — always calibrated to cultural and individual variation (there are “few universals”; S2). Active listening is the integration of attending + reflecting + accurate empathy (links to Unit II).

UNIT IV — Affective Psychotherapies

Overview — what “affective psychotherapies” means

FROM SOURCES While every therapist works with thoughts, feelings and behaviour, the affective therapies place primary emphasis on emotion/affect — “the outward expression of a feeling or emotion.” The guiding idea is that a person can be helped by focusing on emotions and taking responsibility for their feelings (S3). Key principles the sources attach to affective counselling (S3):

  • Priority on a good, trusting, respectful relationship.
  • Helping the client become aware of and communicate feelings (e.g., “I” statements).
  • Helping the client take responsibility for their own feelings rather than blaming others.
  • An optimistic, non-judgemental, client-led stance, trusting an innate drive toward self-actualisation.
Evidence-caution for this unit Your master instruction (and the sources) rightly separate established psychological interventions (psychodynamic, brief, humanistic/person-centred, Gestalt, existential, Adlerian, TA, reality, supportive, hypnotherapy, play, psychodrama) from complementary mind–body practices (yoga, meditation, shavasana, pranic healing, Reiki, tai chi) whose evidence base is mixed, preliminary or (for energy-healing practices) scientifically contested. Each chapter’s “Research & status” section states this honestly.

1. Psychodynamic Psychotherapy

A. Definition & introduction. FROM SOURCES Psychodynamic therapy — the oldest form of psychotherapy, pioneered by Sigmund Freud — treats present difficulties as expressions of unconscious conflict rooted in early relationships (S1, S2). Its long tradition yields enormous diversity in emphasis (the unconscious, dreams, the transference relationship) (S2).

B. History & contributors. Freud (psychoanalysis); later relational/interpersonal turns increased the importance of the therapeutic relationship and reconceptualised transference/countertransference (S2). The Freudian view of human nature is deterministic: behaviour is shaped by irrational forces, unconscious motivation, and biological/instinctual drives evolving through psychosexual stages in the first six years (S1).

C. Theory & assumptions. FROM SOURCES Core tenets (S2): the universal existence of inner conflict and how people manage it; defences that contain intolerable anxiety arising from conflict; a tripartite structure of mind — id (unconscious), ego and superego (conscious/preconscious); the primacy of unconscious processes; and the therapeutic aim of moving unconscious material into awareness for “greater freedom and authenticity.” Instincts are central: libido (life instincts/pleasure) and the death instinct (aggression) (S1).

D. Core concepts. Unconscious; id/ego/superego; defence mechanisms; psychosexual stages; transference (early relational templates re-activated with the therapist) and countertransference; resistance; dreams; interpretation; insight (see Unit II for transference detail).

E. Formulation. Present patterns are read as repetitions of early relational templates — a “prism through which we view the world.” When a caregiver withheld interest, the client may “close down” when upset; this very pattern is enacted in the room as transference, giving direct insight into the past (S2). Malan’s “triangles” (of conflict: defence–anxiety–impulse; of person: current other–therapist–parent) organise the formulation (S1 assessment §; S3 brief-therapy §).

F. Goals. Insight into unconscious conflict; working through defences; a corrective emotional experience; promoting personality growth (Wolberg’s fifth objective).

G. Roles. FROM SOURCES Freud held the therapist should avoid criticism, encouragement, reassurance and advice; the relationship therefore lacks ordinary social dynamics and can feel “cold and uninviting” at first. The therapist does not seek friendship (though basic warmth is not absent) and primarily interprets and confronts, attending to how conflict and control play out in the relationship (S2).

H. Assessment. Malan (1979): the suitable client has at least one sustained personal relationship, can hold back impulsive/suicidal/violent acts, has conflicts at a neurotic (not all-pervasive) level, can stay focused on one theme, is motivated, and can withstand interpretation and confrontation (S2).

I. Procedure. Sessions last 50 minutes, frequency weekly up to five times/week, in individual, couple or group formats (S2). Phases follow Wolberg’s beginning–middle–termination (Unit I §10).

J. Techniques.

  • Free association — say whatever comes to mind; bypasses censorship to surface unconscious material. Rationale: relaxing conscious control reveals conflict. Example: a slip or sudden silence flags a defended topic.
  • Interpretation — the therapist offers meaning linking current feeling, defence and underlying impulse/conflict. Precaution: timing/dosage — premature interpretation raises resistance.
  • Dream analysis — the “royal road to the unconscious.”
  • Analysis of transference & resistance — the central curative work (see Unit II).

K. Stages/process. Alliance → emergence & interpretation of transference/resistance → working through → termination.

L. Indications. Neurotic-level conflict; relational/characterological difficulties; psychologically-minded, motivated clients.

M. Contraindications/risks. Severe/psychotic/addictive presentations; poor impulse control; crisis states. Recognised risks (S2): untoward transference reactions, mismanaged transference/countertransference, and regressive dependency.

N–O. Outcomes & research. FROM SOURCES RCT support (S1): Salzer et al. (2014) — psychodynamic therapy (PDT) improved comorbid conduct/emotional disorders in adolescent inpatients; Leichsenring et al. (2013) — both CBT and PDT efficacious in social anxiety, with some advantage for CBT; Johansson et al. (2017) — internet-based affect-focused PDT promising for social anxiety (2-year follow-up).

P. Advantages/disadvantages. + depth, durable change, strong theory of the relationship; − time- and resource-intensive; weaker/slower symptom relief than CBT in some conditions; demands a specific, suitable client.

Q. Comparison. See the psychodynamic vs. humanistic and brief vs. long-term tables.

R. Clinical illustration A client repeatedly arrives late and becomes dismissive whenever closeness grows in sessions. Rather than reassure, the therapist interprets: “As we get closer, something in you seems to pull back — I wonder if that’s how you learned to protect yourself at home.” The transference pattern becomes the material for insight.

S. Exam summary

Long-answer: “Discuss the theoretical basis and techniques of psychodynamic psychotherapy.” Short-notes: id/ego/superego · free association · interpretation · Malan’s selection criteria · transference as formulation. Confused pair: transference vs. countertransference; interpretation vs. confrontation.

2. Brief Psychotherapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES Brief (planned short-term) therapy is a generic label for any therapy in which time is an explicit element of treatment planning (S2). It is an umbrella for short-term, solution-oriented approaches that emphasise (1) a specific focus and (2) direct, active intervention; the therapist works pro-actively, favouring the present and the factors sustaining a problem over historical causes (S3). It is brief by design, not by default (dropping out ≠ brief therapy) (S2).

B. History. Early psychoanalysis was itself brief and active; as free association, transference work and dream analysis developed, treatment lengthened. The modern era began in the 1960s when Sifneos and Malan independently created the first theoretically coherent short-term therapies; WWII and events such as grief work after the Coconut Grove fire also shaped the field (S3).

C. Theory & four schools. FROM SOURCES Four general schools (S3): (1) psychodynamic, (2) cognitive-behavioural, (3) interpersonal, (4) eclectic — with no conclusive evidence that any one is superior. Common elements across brief therapies (S2): sharp focus; high therapist activity (catalyst, not blank screen); active client involvement with homework; careful client selection; enhanced experiencing (via hypnosis, relaxation, confrontation, exposure, role-play); emphasis on readiness for change (Prochaska & DiClemente); and impact (corrective emotional experience) over exhaustive insight.

D. Key models (contributors).

Sifneos — short-term anxiety-provoking therapy12–20 sessions; detached, didactic therapist; provokes & uses anxiety as a lever; suits a small (~2–10%) high-functioning subgroup (S3)

E–F. Formulation & goals. A single, agreed focal problem/pattern; goals are circumscribed and reached by streamlining change — advancing readiness and delivering emotionally impactful interventions rather than broad personality overhaul (S2).

G. Roles. Active, directive therapist; engaged, task-doing client.

H. Assessment / suitability (S2). Depends on: duration (chronic over-learned patterns need more work); interpersonal history (brevity needs a quick, durable alliance — harder after abuse/neglect); plus motivation and a focal problem. Not appropriate for chronic/severe mental illness.

I–J. Procedure & techniques. Time-limited contract (fixed number or stop-date); focus-holding; interpretation/confrontation (dynamic models) or Socratic questioning, activity scheduling, cognitive rehearsal and homework (cognitive models); between-session tasks.

K. Stages (Mann’s arc, S3). Early outpouring & idealisation → ~session 4 disillusionment/return of symptoms (first interpretation of avoidance of time-limit) → mid-therapy deepening → after midpoint (session 6) overt resistance/negative transference → termination work on separation; the client moves from neurotic separation-fear toward realistic, autonomous optimism.

L–M. Indications/contraindications. Indicated for focal, situational, neurotic-level problems in higher-functioning, motivated clients; contraindicated for chronic/severe illness, severe trauma histories, poor alliance capacity.

N–O. Outcomes & research. No single brief therapy proven superior to another (S3). Solution-Focused Brief Therapy evidence (S1): Kim et al. (2018), Beauchemin (2018), Wengao et al. (2022) support SFBT across substance use, student wellness and distress.

P. Advantages/disadvantages. + efficient, focused, cost-effective, good for motivated clients; − unsuitable for complex/chronic cases; demands skilled, active therapists; narrow focus may leave broader issues untouched.

R. Illustration (Mann) A client grieving a parent contracts for exactly 12 sessions. The therapist names the central issue — unresolved separation — and, as the ending nears, uses the time limit itself to re-evoke and master the feelings of loss.

S. Exam summary

Long-answer: “Describe brief psychotherapy, its common features and major models.” Short-notes: four schools of brief therapy · Mann’s 12-session model · Sifneos vs. Davanloo · brief by design vs. default. See brief vs. long-term table.

3. Humanistic Psychotherapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES A family of therapies emphasising self-actualisation, with the therapist facilitating that actualisation (Rowan, 2001). Though stereotyped as a 1960s reaction to psychoanalysis, they in fact have strong roots in psychodynamic theory and evolved alongside it (S2).

B–C. History & philosophical basis (S2). Four defining philosophical values:

  • Human potential & freedom — an optimistic view: people have freedom and responsibility to shape their lives and can find internal resources even in adversity (not a denial of genetics or circumstance).
  • Holism — the person is an indivisible unity of mind, body, action, feeling and spirit; no aspect can be split off.
  • Relationship — human relatedness is central; the therapeutic relationship is the most potent healing factor (Rogers, 1951).
  • Phenomenology — attending to the client’s experience without pre-judging, so new meaning can emerge.

D. Core concepts. Self-actualisation; the “fully functioning”/“OK” person; here-and-now contact; subjective experience; the whole person (feeling–thinking–acting + spiritual); meaning-making “from cradle to grave” (S2).

E–F. Formulation & goals. Emotional problems are respected as attempts to get on with life; everyone can improve their quality of life. Goals: facilitate self-actualisation, awareness and authentic living; change paradoxically follows acceptance of who one is (S2).

G. Roles. The therapist is a facilitator providing the relational conditions; the client leads and is trusted as the expert on their own experience.

I–J. Procedure & techniques. Individual, couple and group formats; frequency from several times/week to monthly; applicable briefly or long-term (S2). Techniques are relationship-based: empathic reflection, the core conditions, here-and-now contact, experiential exercises (see Gestalt & person-centred).

K. Integrative development (S2). Safran & Messer (1997) identify three forms of integration: technical eclecticism (selecting effective techniques across approaches), common-factors integration (privileging the therapeutic relationship), and theoretical integration (building a unified theory).

L–M. Indications/limits. Classically for reasonably functional clients with specific life-area problems (relationships, work), increasingly extended to deeper problems incl. personality disorder (Johnson 2000; Bulmer 2003; Shah 2004). Critique: openness/flexibility can be seen as dilution of a complex task (Rowan, 1983) (S2).

N–O. Research. FROM SOURCES S2 notes that because many principles derive from philosophical values and include a spiritual dimension, building a research evidence base has been a particular challenge for the humanistic therapies (an honest limitation).

S. Exam summary

Long-answer: “Discuss the philosophical foundations of humanistic psychotherapy.” Short-notes: the four humanistic values (potential/freedom, holism, relationship, phenomenology) · self-actualisation · three forms of integration (Safran & Messer). See psychodynamic vs. humanistic.

4. Existential Therapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES A philosophically grounded approach addressing the “givens” of existence — freedom, responsibility, meaning, isolation and mortality — rooted in existential philosophy (S2).

B. History & contributors (S2). Philosophical roots in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Sartre and Tillich. Clinical lineage: Karl Jaspers (General Psychopathology, 1913; phenomenological observation; human relatedness as a therapeutic tool); Martin Buber (existence is dialogical; “I–Thou” contactful relating); Viktor Frankl (meaning as the key to survival — from the concentration camps; focus on the here-and-now and the future, freedom of will and finding meaning); R.D. Laing (UK; psychotic experience as meaningful and amenable to help); Emmy van Deurzen (UK); Rollo May and Irvin Yalom (USA).

C–D. Theory & concepts. Freedom and responsibility; authenticity vs. “bad faith”; meaning/meaninglessness; here-and-now and the future over early history (Frankl); the dialogical (I–Thou) relationship (Buber); phenomenological understanding (Jaspers).

E–F. Formulation & goals. Distress is understood as a confrontation with existential givens (e.g., avoiding freedom/responsibility, or living without meaning). Goals: enable authentic, responsible, meaning-filled living; help the client “own freedom of will” (Frankl).

G. Roles. An authentic, present fellow-traveller rather than a technical expert; the I–Thou encounter is itself therapeutic.

J. Techniques SUPPLEMENTARY. Existential therapy is deliberately light on prescribed “techniques”; it works through phenomenological exploration, confronting the givens, Socratic dialogue, and (in logotherapy) Frankl’s paradoxical intention and dereflection to find meaning. (Your source names Frankl/logotherapy; the specific technique labels are standard supplements.)

L–M. Indications/limits. Suited to clients grappling with meaning, loss, mortality, major life transitions; less suited where structured symptom-relief or crisis stabilisation is the priority. A recognised limitation (S2): the humanistic/existential family’s philosophical, spiritual character makes a research evidence base harder to build.

R. Illustration (Frankl) A bereaved widower feels life is pointless. Rather than removing the grief, the therapist helps him find meaning in honouring his late wife through the way he now lives — converting unavoidable suffering into a stance he can own.

5. Gestalt Therapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES Gestalt (German) = whole/form/shape. An experiential humanistic therapy focused on present awareness and full contact with oneself and the environment (S2).

B. History. Originated by Fritz Perls and his wife Laura Perls. Perls (a neuropsychiatrist, briefly analysed by Karen Horney) trained as a psychoanalyst, encountered Gestalt psychology and existentialism in the 1920s, fled Nazi Germany (South Africa, 1936), moved to the USA and in 1947 — with Laura — began the first group from which Gestalt therapy emerged; Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality (Perls et al., 1951) (S2).

C–D. Theory & concepts. Phenomenological; people are self-regulating organisms with an inbuilt drive toward development and need-meeting. Change is inevitable when a person is in contact with self and environment (the “paradoxical theory of change,” Beisser 1970 — one becomes what one is, not what one tries to be). When contact is interrupted (often early in life), the person cannot meet needs; these patterned interruptions to contact are the primary focus (S2). Later Gestalt added the dialogic relationship (Hycner, Jacobs, Yontef) — phenomenological exploration through the therapist–client dialogue.

F–G. Goals & roles. Raise awareness, enlarge choice, restore contact — not change or problem-solving per se. Perls stressed self-support, challenging clients who leaned on the therapist (S2); the therapist is a facilitator of present experience.

J. Techniques SUPPLEMENTARY. (Your source gives the theory; these classic techniques are standard additions.) Empty-chair / two-chair dialogue (enacting conflicting parts or unfinished business with another person); exaggeration; staying with the feeling; “I” language and present-tense talk; awareness experiments; attending to unfinished business. Rationale: make the implicit explicit and restore here-and-now contact.

L–M. Indications/limits. Good for over-intellectualised, affectively constricted clients; caution with fragile/psychotic clients where intense affect-arousal may destabilise.

S. Exam summary

Short-notes: paradoxical theory of change · unfinished business · empty-chair · contact & interruptions · Perls. See Gestalt vs. person-centred.

6. Person-Centred Therapy

A–B. Definition & history. FROM SOURCES Developed by Carl Rogers (influenced by Rank and the child-guidance movement from the 1930s; a landmark 1940 University of Minnesota talk critiqued existing approaches). Rogers’ radical idea: providing a respectful, empathic and authentic relationship will in itself enable the client to activate their own capacity to become a more fully functioning, responsible self (Rogers, 1951, 1961). He was also a pioneer of systematic psychotherapy research (S2).

C–D. Theory & concepts. An actualising tendency (an innate drive toward growth) that life experiences may thwart but the right conditions release; the self-concept, conditions of worth, and congruence/incongruence between self and experience. The Rogers–Buber dialogue (1957): Rogers resonated with Buber’s I–Thou but saw implicit judgement where positive potential is doubted (S2).

E–F. Formulation & goals. Distress arises from incongruence and introjected conditions of worth; the goal is to restore congruence and movement toward the fully functioning person — self-directed by the client.

G–J. Roles & the core conditions (the “technique”). FROM SOURCES Person-centred therapy is non-directive; the therapist’s stance is the method — the core conditions (see Unit II): congruence/genuineness, unconditional positive regard, and empathic understanding conveyed to the client (empathy, not sympathy; Carkhuff’s scale) (S2). Many hold these are necessary but not sufficient (Bohart 1990; Tausch 1990).

L–M. Indications/limits. Broadly applicable; especially where self-direction and relationship are central. Limits: may be insufficient alone for severe/complex conditions; non-directiveness can frustrate clients wanting structure.

R. Illustration A client says, “I should be grateful — I have no right to feel sad.” The therapist responds with UPR and empathy: “It sounds like you’re feeling sad and telling yourself you shouldn’t be.” Accepted without judgement, the client gradually owns the feeling — congruence increases.

7. Adlerian Therapy (Individual Psychology)

A–B. Definition & history. FROM SOURCES Founded by Alfred Adler, who emphasised that human beings are social in nature and that behaviour is goal-directed (teleological) — ideas that also seeded reality therapy (S2). The “primary indication of mental health” in the Adlerian view is connectedness with all of life (social embeddedness), which provides a genuine sense of security (S2).

C–D. Theory & concepts SUPPLEMENTARY (named standard terms). Inferiority feelings and striving for superiority/competence; social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) — the hallmark of mental health; style of life (lifestyle) formed in early childhood; fictional finalism (guiding goals); birth order/family constellation; the creative self. (Your source names the social, goal-directed, lifestyle, and connectedness ideas; these classic Adlerian labels are added.)

H–I. Assessment & procedure — six phases (S2).

  1. Establishing the therapeutic relationship — a warm, caring, empathic, cooperative bond; the client may first need to express distress with little interruption.
  2. Assessment — of the lifestyle (incl. early recollections, family constellation).
  3. Encouragement & clarification.
  4. Interpretation — of the lifestyle and mistaken goals (with insight).
  5. Redirection of the lifestyle — reorientation toward social interest and useful goals.
  6. Meta-therapy.

Adlerian therapy is “a creative process in which the therapist invents a new therapy for each client” (S2).

F–G. Goals & roles. Foster social interest, courage and useful goal-striving; the therapist is an encouraging, collaborative educator.

J. Techniques SUPPLEMENTARY. Early recollections; lifestyle/family-constellation analysis; encouragement; confronting the “basic mistakes”; “the question” (“How would life be different if you were well?”); acting as-if; spitting in the client’s soup (making a payoff conscious); task-setting.

L–M. Indications/limits. Broadly applicable, strongly used in child guidance, parenting and education; less emphasis on biological/unconscious drive than classical analysis.

S. Exam summary

Short-notes: social interest · inferiority & striving · style of life · the six phases · encouragement. See Adlerian vs. other individual-focused.

8. Transactional Analysis (TA)

A–B. Definition & history. FROM SOURCES Developed in the 1950s–60s in the USA by Eric Berne (a Canadian doctor and psychoanalyst). Rooted in humanistic and existential values, its core is the ego-state model — a sophisticated model of intrapsychic structure evolved from psychoanalysis (Berne, 1961). TA has absorbed technique from Gestalt (Goulding & Goulding, 1979) and later self-psychology/neuroscience (Hargaden & Sills, 2002), and is well-regarded within psychotherapy integration (S2).

C–D. Theory & concepts. FROM SOURCES The three ego states (S3): Parent (introjected parental figures — blaming/praising), Adult (reality-oriented processing of here-and-now), and Child (archaic feelings/behaviours). A healthy person flexibly uses all three; problems arise from contamination or exclusion of ego states.

D (continued) SUPPLEMENTARY: transactions (complementary, crossed, ulterior); strokes (units of recognition); life positions (I’m OK/You’re OK, etc.); games (repetitive ulterior-transaction sequences with a payoff — from Games People Play); and the life script formed in childhood. (Your source names ego states & the humanistic/existential grounding; these are the standard TA terms.)

F–G. Goals & roles. Attain autonomy (awareness, spontaneity, capacity for intimacy); strengthen the Adult; decontaminate ego states; a contractual, egalitarian therapist–client partnership.

J. Techniques (S3 + supplement). Structural & transactional analysis (diagramming ego states and transactions); decontamination (the therapist explains inconsistencies to free the Adult — illustrated in your source’s dialogue); game & script analysis; contracts; redecision work.

R. Illustration (from source) A client demands a stronger tranquilliser and complains of being “bad-tempered.” The counsellor points out the inconsistencies in the client’s Parent–Adult–Child communication — a decontamination that frees the Adult to appraise reality (S3).

S. Exam summary

Long-answer: “Describe the ego-state model and techniques of TA.” Short-notes: Parent–Adult–Child · transactions (complementary/crossed/ulterior) · strokes · games · life script · life positions. See TA vs. reality therapy.

9. Reality Therapy

A–B. Definition & history. FROM SOURCES Founded by William Glasser; roots in Adler (humans are social; behaviour is goal-centred). Glasser extended these ideas to include genetic instructions / human needs as sources of behaviour, holding that behaviour is chosen — not thrust on us by family, environment or childhood conflict; “human beings originate their own behaviour” (S2).

C. Theoretical basis — Choice Theory (S2). Reality therapy is grounded in Choice Theory (originally framed via control-system theory). Behaviour is a continual attempt to satisfy basic needs; we control our own behaviour.

D. Core concepts SUPPLEMENTARY. The five basic needs (survival, love/belonging, power/achievement, freedom, fun); the “quality world” (our pictures of need-satisfying people/things); total behaviour (acting, thinking, feeling, physiology); and a present-, responsibility-focused stance. (Source gives choice/needs/present focus; these labels are standard additions.)

F–G. Goals & roles. Help clients make effective choices to meet needs responsibly, strengthening need-satisfying relationships. The therapist “becomes part of the client’s inner discourse” — a caring, needed, involved figure (S2); directive but warm, focused on present behaviour, not blaming the past.

I–J. Procedure — the WDEP system (S2 + supplement). The procedures are tools to help clients clarify wants, evaluate behaviour, and plan change:

  • W — Wants: explore wants, needs and perceptions (the quality world).
  • D — Doing/Direction: examine current total behaviour and overall direction.
  • E — Evaluation: the client makes a self-evaluation — “is what you’re doing getting you what you want?” (the pivotal step).
  • P — Planning: make a specific, achievable, committed plan for effective change.

Glasser emphasised no excuses, no punishment, no giving up.

L–M. Indications/limits. Widely used with adolescents, in schools, addictions and corrections; present-focused and empowering. Limits: de-emphasises the past, the unconscious and biological/environmental constraints — a critique of the strong “behaviour is chosen” position.

S. Exam summary

Short-notes: Choice Theory · five basic needs · WDEP · total behaviour · “behaviour is chosen.” See TA vs. reality therapy.

10. Supportive Psychotherapy

Source noteYour Unit IV PDF refers to “supportive” work but does not give a dedicated, cleanly-extractable chapter on supportive psychotherapy as a formal modality. The following is standard, authoritative material, labelled accordingly; the supportive–expressive continuum idea and the structure-of-therapy material are grounded in your sources (S1/S2).

A. Definition. SUPPLEMENTARY Supportive psychotherapy aims to maintain or restore the client’s best level of functioning and relieve distress by strengthening existing adaptive defences and coping, rather than by uncovering unconscious conflict. It sits at the supportive end of the supportive–expressive continuum.

C–D. Theory & stance. Draws on psychodynamic understanding but reverses the uncovering stance: it bolsters defences and self-esteem instead of challenging them. Core aims: reduce anxiety, enhance reality-testing, improve adaptive skills, and sustain the therapeutic relationship as a stabilising force.

F–G. Goals & roles. Symptom relief, ego-support, prevention of relapse/deterioration (links to Wolberg’s “modify/retard” objectives, S1). The therapist is active, warm, reassuring and directive — the opposite of the neutral psychodynamic stance.

J. Techniques SUPPLEMENTARY. Reassurance; praise and encouragement; psychoeducation; advice and guidance; environmental intervention; naming and normalising affect; reinforcing adaptive defences; problem-solving; rehearsal/anticipatory guidance. Interpretation and transference analysis are used sparingly if at all.

L–M. Indications/limits. Indicated for clients with limited ego-strength or in crisis — severe/chronic illness, acute stress, psychosis in stabilisation, low psychological-mindedness. Limitation: does not produce deep characterological change (that is not its aim).

11. Clinical Hypnotherapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES Hypnosis is treated as a normal phenomenon and, per your source, “a valid and reliable clinical tool in the treatment of many health problems” — an accepted therapeutic medical tool (S3). Clinical hypnotherapy uses a focused, absorbed state (trance) with heightened suggestibility for therapeutic ends.

B. History (S3). From early practitioners through the 19th century; from the 1880s the study of hypnosis passed from surgeons to a broader medical/psychological field; despite debates (e.g., Charcot’s claim that hypnosis was a manifestation of hysteria rather than a cure), it continued to be accepted and practised. SUPPLEMENTARY Key names standardly cited: Mesmer (animal magnetism), James Braid (coined “hypnosis”), Charcot, Bernheim, and Milton Erickson (modern clinical hypnosis).

C–D. Theory & concepts. Trance; suggestibility; focused attention and dissociation; ideomotor responses; post-hypnotic suggestion. Debate persists between “state” (altered-state) and “non-state/socio-cognitive” accounts of hypnosis.

I–J. Procedure & techniques SUPPLEMENTARY. Assessment of hypnotisability and suitability → induction (e.g., eye-fixation, progressive relaxation) → deepening → therapeutic suggestion / imagery / age-regression / ego-strengthening → post-hypnotic suggestion → re-alerting. Often combined with CBT (“cognitive-behavioural hypnotherapy”).

L–M. Indications/contraindications. Indicated: pain management, anxiety, habit disorders (smoking), IBS, procedural distress, some psychosomatic conditions. Caution/contraindication: psychosis, severe dissociative disorders, low trust; risk of iatrogenic false memories if used for “memory recovery” — a serious ethical/legal concern. Requires trained, consented practice.

N–O. Research & status (S3 + supplement). Your source affirms hypnosis as a significant clinical tool in medical practice; the strongest empirical support is as an adjunct (e.g., for pain and procedural anxiety), not a stand-alone cure-all.

S. Exam summary

Short-notes: trance & suggestibility · induction–deepening–suggestion–re-alerting · Braid/Erickson · false-memory risk · hypnosis as adjunct.

12. Play Therapy

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES “The systematic use of a theoretical model to establish an interpersonal process wherein trained play therapists use the therapeutic powers of play to help clients prevent or resolve psychosocial difficulties and achieve optimal growth and development” (Association for Play Therapy, 2015; S1). 13. Psychodrama

A–B. Definition & history. FROM SOURCES Developed by psychiatrist Dr J.L. Moreno, psychodrama was the first recognised method of group psychotherapy, is practised in many countries, and is fully accredited as a psychotherapy by the UKCP (S3). (As early as 1908 Moreno drew on existential ideas of personal responsibility, using drama techniques in groups to help patients process early family conflicts — S2.)

C–D. Theory & concepts. FROM SOURCES Central concepts include spontaneity, catharsis, surplus reality, and the roles of protagonist and director (all present in S3). SUPPLEMENTARY Also: tele (two-way empathic flow in the group), the auxiliary ego (group members enacting significant others), the audience, and the stage.

G. Roles. Protagonist (the client whose situation is enacted); director (the therapist who guides the drama); auxiliary egos (play significant figures); audience (the group, offering resonance and sharing).

I. Procedure — three phases.

  1. Warm-up — the group becomes ready and a protagonist/theme emerges.
  2. Action (enactment) — the protagonist dramatises the situation on the “stage,” using techniques below, often reaching catharsis.
  3. Sharing — group members share (not analyse) how the enactment resonated with their own lives, re-integrating the protagonist.

J. Techniques. Doubling (an auxiliary voices the protagonist’s unspoken inner experience); mirroring (the protagonist watches an auxiliary replay their behaviour, as in a mirror); role reversal (the protagonist takes the role of the other, building empathy and perspective); soliloquy; empty-chair. Rationale: enactment makes implicit relational dynamics explicit and enables new, spontaneous responses (surplus reality lets the client replay the past “as it should have been”).

L–M. Indications/limits. Group settings; trauma, relational and family-of-origin issues, skill rehearsal. Caution: emotionally powerful — needs a skilled director and a contained group; fragile clients may be overwhelmed.

S. Exam summary

Long-answer: “Describe psychodrama: its structure, roles and techniques.” Short-notes: Moreno · protagonist/director/auxiliary ego · warm-up/action/sharing · doubling, mirroring, role reversal · spontaneity & catharsis.

16. Shavasana (Corpse / Relaxation Pose)

A. Definition. FROM SOURCES A yogic deep-relaxation posture. Your source explains its rationale physiologically: the nervous system has a voluntary part and an involuntary part (respiration, circulation, digestion). Under fear/stress the sympathetic system drives “fight or flight”; chronic or imaginary-threat activation becomes a harmful drain on energy (S3).

C. Mechanism (S3). Shavasana and meditation activate the parasympathetic nervous system, normalising adrenaline, noradrenaline and corticosteroid secretion. As a result: heart rate, blood pressure and blood sugar normalise; blood “thins” and arteries dilate; lactic-acid formation falls (said to retard ageing); endorphins and neuropeptides are released (bringing “peace and joy”); and slow rhythmic alpha activity appears over the frontal lobe. These chemical changes are said to promote healing by improving immune status (S3).

I–J. Procedure. SUPPLEMENTARY Lie supine, limbs relaxed and slightly apart, eyes closed; systematically relax each body region; slow natural breathing; passive awareness; typically 10–20 minutes.

F–L. Goals & indications. Relaxation, stress-reduction, recovery; used as the concluding relaxation of a yoga session and as a standalone destressing practice; useful adjunct for anxiety, hypertension (as complementary), insomnia.

M. Precautions. Generally safe; those who find lying still with eyes closed distressing (e.g., some trauma survivors) may need modification.

S. Exam summary

Short-notes: sympathetic vs. parasympathetic · the physiological changes in shavasana · alpha activity · shavasana as relaxation adjunct.
