2. PREDISPOSING FACTORS (The Second "P")

Factors that made the patient vulnerable to developing OCD:

A. Biological Predisposition

• Age of onset: Onset in adolescence (approx. age 15), consistent with typical age-of-onset patterns for OCD.

• Temperamental sensitivity: Assessment findings (TAT) reveal a highly emotionally sensitive, introspective temperament with heightened responsiveness to distressing internal experiences — a known vulnerability factor for obsessional difficulties.

B. Psychological Predisposition

• Heightened sense of responsibility and guilt-proneness: SSCT and TAT findings converge on a harsh, guilt-inducing superego; the patient readily internalises blame and experiences self-disgust for intrusive thoughts that are involuntary in nature.

• Low self-esteem and self-concept disturbance: SSCT Self-Concept score (16, significant disturbance) indicates uncertainty about personal abilities and future, and chronic self-doubt.

• Interpersonal insecurity: Elevated Interpersonal Relationships score (20) with prominent Fear subscore (7) indicates trait-level social anxiety, mistrust, and heightened emotional reactivity to interpersonal stress — consistent with a cognitive style prone to catastrophic misinterpretation.

• Dependency and attachment needs: TAT reveals strong unmet needs for affiliation, nurturance, and emotional validation, with a tendency to idealise relationships and fear abandonment.

• Avoidant/passive coping style: Defensive functioning dominated by fantasy, withdrawal, repression, rationalization, denial, and suppression rather than active problem-solving — a coping repertoire that facilitates reliance on mental neutralising strategies (e.g., not swallowing saliva) rather than direct engagement with distress.

C. Social/Environmental Predisposition

• Family emotional climate: Mild-to-moderate disturbance in the family domain, particularly in relation to the mother, suggesting emotional dependency and unmet emotional needs during development.

• Restricted exposure to opposite-sex relationships: Mild conflict in the sexual domain regarding females, with discomfort forming friendships with them — likely limiting normalising social learning around sexual thoughts and increasing the perceived "dangerousness" and shame attached to such thoughts when they intrude.

D. Cognitive Vulnerability (Schema-Level, per Salkovskis' Model)

• Core dysfunctional beliefs (inferred from projectives and history): "Having a bad thought is as bad as acting on it" (thought–action fusion); "I am responsible for preventing harm to my family, even harm caused only by my thoughts"; "Sexual thoughts about girls mean something is wrong/immoral about me."

• Inflated responsibility: An exaggerated belief that he is personally responsible for bringing about, or failing to prevent, harmful outcomes — the central cognitive distortion described by Salkovskis (1985) as driving compulsive neutralising behaviour.

• Intolerance of uncertainty and need for control: Underlying inability to tolerate ambiguity around whether a thought might "come true," fuelling repetitive checking-type rituals (eye rubbing, saliva avoidance).

3. PRECIPITATING FACTORS (The Third "P")

Triggers that activated the obsessive-compulsive episode:

A. Primary Precipitant: Conflict with Paternal Uncle

The onset of the patient's difficulties is directly associated with a conflict involving his paternal uncle, approximately 4 years prior to assessment, particularly significant because:

• The conflict introduced acute interpersonal threat and moral/emotional distress within an already guilt-prone, family-oriented young person.

• Following the conflict, he began to develop repetitive thoughts specifically about harm and negative consequences befalling others — consistent with a guilt-driven, responsibility-laden cognitive style being activated by a real interpersonal rupture.

• This represents a critical incident in which his pre-existing schema of inflated responsibility for others' welfare was catastrophically activated.

B. Secondary Precipitation: Emergence of Sexual and Death-Related Intrusions

• Over time, the initial harm-related obsessions generalised to intrusive thoughts related to death, disturbing events, and sexual themes — a common progression in OCD in which anxious apparaisal of one intrusion sensitises the person to appraise further normal intrusive thoughts (which everyone experiences) as personally significant and dangerous.

• Adolescent developmental stage (emerging sexuality, identity formation) likely intersected with pre-existing guilt-proneness and interpersonal insecurity around females, making sexual intrusions particularly shame-laden and salient.

C. Cognitive Pathway to OCD (Salkovskis' Model, 1985/1999)

Triggering Event: Conflict with paternal uncle → normal intrusive thought about harm to a family member (universal/normal cognitive phenomenon)

↓

Catastrophic Misinterpretation: "This thought means I am responsible for preventing this harm / this thought reveals something bad about me" (inflated responsibility + thought–action fusion)

↓

Emotional Response: Intense guilt, anxiety, disgust, and distress

↓

Neutralising Behaviour: Eye rubbing (to "remove" the thought); avoidance of swallowing saliva (belief that doing so lets the thought "go inside")

↓

Temporary Relief → Reinforcement: Anxiety reduces briefly after the ritual, reinforcing the belief that the ritual is necessary and that the thought was genuinely dangerous

↓

Maintenance: Increased frequency/salience of intrusions due to heightened self-monitoring (thought suppression paradox), generalisation to new content (sexual, death-related thoughts), and worsening guilt/depression

4. PERPETUATING FACTORS (The Fourth "P")

Factors maintaining the obsessive-compulsive condition and preventing recovery:

A. Patient-Level Maintaining Factors

• Compulsive neutralising (eye rubbing, saliva avoidance): These behaviours provide short-term relief but prevent disconfirmation of the belief that the thoughts are dangerous, thereby strengthening the obsessive–compulsive cycle (classic negative reinforcement loop).

• Thought–action fusion and inflated responsibility: Continued appraisal of intrusive thoughts as morally equivalent to actions maintains high guilt and drives further ritualising.

• Thought suppression attempts: Deliberate efforts to push away or "not think" the thoughts paradoxically increase their frequency and intrusiveness (well-established thought-suppression rebound effect).

• Rumination and overthinking: The patient is described as highly sensitive to emotional experiences and prone to overthink/ruminate about distressing events and interpersonal situations, prolonging distress after triggers.

• Avoidant coping style: Reliance on fantasy, withdrawal, suppression, and rationalisation (per TAT) rather than direct engagement with feared thoughts prevents habituation and maintains anxiety.

• Comorbid depression: Severe depressive symptoms (BDI = 30) — guilt, hopelessness, low mood — reduce motivation and energy for resisting compulsions and further entrench negative self-appraisals ("I am a bad person for having these thoughts").

• Interpersonal insecurity and social withdrawal tendencies: Fear of rejection and discomfort with females reduce corrective social feedback and opportunities for normalising discussion of intrusive thoughts, allowing catastrophic beliefs to persist unchallenged.

B. Environmental/Family-Level Maintaining Factors

• Ongoing academic pressure: Currently in 12th standard; academic demands compound stress and provide less time/energy for symptom management.

• Limited psychoeducation to date: Family and patient may not yet fully appreciate that intrusive thoughts are involuntary and do not reflect actual intentions or moral character, allowing shame and secrecy to persist.

• 4-year duration prior to treatment-seeking: A prolonged duration of untreated OCD has allowed the obsessive–compulsive cycle, guilt schema, and secondary depression to become deeply entrenched.

6. INTEGRATIVE FORMULATION NARRATIVE

Developmental Formulation

Rahul Ahirwar is a 19-year-old unmarried male, currently completing 12th standard, presenting with a 4-year history of severe Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (Y-BOCS = 33) with comorbid severe depressive features (BDI = 30) and moderate anxiety (BAI = 24).

Premorbid personality and cognitive vulnerability: Projective assessment (SSCT, TAT) reveals a young man with a pre-existing psychological profile characterised by heightened guilt-proneness, a harsh and guilt-inducing superego, low self-esteem, and significant interpersonal insecurity — particularly fear of rejection and discomfort around females. He shows strong unmet needs for affiliation, nurturance, and emotional validation, alongside a mild-to-moderate disturbance in his relationship with his mother reflecting emotional dependency. His characteristic coping style is avoidant and internalising — relying on fantasy, withdrawal, suppression, and rationalisation rather than direct, active problem-solving. This combination of inflated personal responsibility, guilt-proneness, and an avoidant coping repertoire constitutes the core cognitive-behavioural vulnerability profile described in Salkovskis' cognitive theory of OCD, in which ordinary, universal intrusive thoughts become catastrophically misinterpreted as personally significant and dangerous.

Precipitation and onset: The disorder was precipitated by a conflict involving his paternal uncle roughly four years ago. Given his premorbid cognitive style (high responsibility for others' welfare, guilt-proneness), this interpersonal rupture appears to have catastrophically activated obsessional thinking about harm and negative consequences befalling family members — a normal, universal intrusive thought that was misappraised as personally meaningful and dangerous. Over time, through generalisation processes common to OCD, this obsessional focus broadened to include intrusive thoughts about death, disturbing events, and sexual themes, the latter interacting with his pre-existing interpersonal insecurity around females to produce marked guilt and self-disgust.

Progression and maintenance: To manage the intolerable distress produced by these intrusions, Rahul developed two principal neutralising behaviours: repeated eye rubbing (intended to physically "remove" the thought) and deliberate avoidance of swallowing saliva (based on a magical/thought–action-fusion belief that swallowing would let the negative thought enter his body). Both rituals produce brief relief, which negatively reinforces their repetition and prevents him from learning that the intrusive thoughts are harmless and that anxiety naturally subsides without ritualising (habituation). Over four years, this maintenance cycle has been compounded by chronic rumination, thought-suppression attempts (which paradoxically increase intrusion frequency), and a secondary severe depressive episode marked by guilt, hopelessness, and emotional withdrawal. Interpersonal insecurity and discomfort with females have further limited his access to normalising social feedback that could challenge his catastrophic beliefs about the meaning of his thoughts.

Understanding the Symptom Profile

Why OCD rather than a psychotic or purely anxiety-based presentation: The clinical presentation is dominated by intrusive, unwanted, ego-dystonic thoughts (obsessions) that the patient recognises as products of his own mind and finds distressing and irrational — consistent with good insight and intact reality testing (confirmed by TAT and behavioural observation), and clearly distinguishing this from a psychotic process. The presence of clearly identifiable neutralising rituals (eye rubbing, saliva avoidance) directly and functionally linked to specific obsessional content, together with a Y-BOCS score in the severe range, confirms a primary OCD diagnosis. The severe depressive and moderate anxiety features are best conceptualised as secondary/comorbid, arising from the chronic burden, guilt, and functional impairment produced by four years of unremitting obsessive–compulsive symptoms.

Cognitive Model Formulation (Salkovskis, 1985, 1999)

Early experiences: Emotionally dependent relationship with mother → guilt-prone, high-responsibility schema → avoidant/internalising coping style

Core schemas: "I am responsible for preventing harm to others, even harm I merely think about" | "Having a bad or sexual thought is as morally wrong as acting on it" | "I must control/eliminate my thoughts to be a good person"

Dysfunctional assumptions: "If I don't neutralise this thought, something bad will happen and it will be my fault" | "Normal people don't have thoughts like this" | "I cannot tolerate not knowing whether the thought is dangerous"

Critical incident: Conflict with paternal uncle

Intrusions: Universal, normal intrusive thought about harm to a family member (everyone experiences occasional intrusive thoughts of this nature)

Misinterpretation of intrusions: Catastrophic appraisal as personally significant, morally revealing, and requiring urgent action (inflated responsibility + thought–action fusion), rather than being dismissed as normal "mental noise"

Neutralisation: Eye rubbing; saliva-avoidance ritual — providing short-term relief but preventing disconfirmation

Maintenance: Selective attention/hypervigilance for intrusions, thought suppression (rebound effect), avoidance of triggering situations (e.g., interactions with females), and secondary depression reducing capacity to resist rituals

8. STRESS-VULNERABILITY MODEL APPLICATION (Zubin & Spring, 1977; adapted for OCD)

Vulnerability Factors (Enduring):

• Guilt-prone, high-responsibility cognitive schema

• Low self-esteem and interpersonal insecurity

• Avoidant/internalising coping repertoire (fantasy, withdrawal, suppression)

• Emotionally dependent early relationship pattern with mother

Stressors (Triggering):

• Conflict with paternal uncle

• Adolescent developmental pressures (identity, emerging sexuality)

Threshold Exceeded:

The combination of a guilt-prone, high-responsibility cognitive style with the acute stress of the family conflict exceeded the patient's threshold for adaptive coping, resulting in catastrophic misinterpretation of a normal intrusive thought and the onset of ritualistic neutralising behaviour. His narrow, avoidant coping repertoire (fantasy, withdrawal, suppression) was insufficient to manage the resulting distress, allowing an obsessive–compulsive cycle to take hold rather than natural habituation.

Current Balance:

Currently, the balance is tilted toward symptom maintenance due to: four years of unremitting compulsive neutralising (deeply reinforced habit), secondary severe depression reducing coping resources, and ongoing academic/interpersonal stressors. However, the balance is more favourable than in many chronic psychiatric presentations because of the patient's good insight, intact reality testing, motivation for treatment, and family's active help-seeking — all of which are strong positive prognostic indicators once formal treatment (pharmacological + ERP) begins.

10. TREATMENT IMPLICATIONS OF THE FORMULATION

A. Pharmacotherapy (Priority 1)

• Psychiatric consultation recommended for initiation and management of medication, particularly SSRIs, given the severity of OCD symptoms with comorbid anxiety and depressive features.

• Regular follow-up to monitor improvement, side effects, and treatment adherence.

• Rationale from formulation: Pharmacological treatment can reduce baseline anxious/depressive load, increasing the patient's capacity to engage in and tolerate ERP.

B. Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) (Priority 2)

• Gradual, graded exposure to intrusive negative and sexual thoughts (and disturbing visual triggers) without engaging in eye rubbing or saliva-avoidance rituals.

• Hierarchy construction starting with lower-anxiety triggers, progressively working toward higher-anxiety obsessional content.

• Rationale from formulation: Repeated exposure while preventing the ritualistic response allows habituation, weakens the belief in thought–action fusion and inflated responsibility, increases tolerance of uncertainty, and directly targets the negative-reinforcement loop identified in the functional analysis.

C. Cognitive Therapy Component (Targeting Salkovskis' Core Beliefs)

• Normalising intrusive thoughts: Psychoeducation that intrusive thoughts (including harm-related and sexual) are universal and experienced by the general population; the problem is the meaning attached to them, not the thoughts themselves.

• Challenging inflated responsibility: Socratic questioning and responsibility "pie-charts" to redistribute perceived responsibility for feared outcomes.

• Addressing thought–action fusion: Behavioural experiments to test the belief that thinking a thought (or swallowing saliva during one) has real-world consequences.

• Guilt and self-concept work: Given elevated guilt-proneness and low self-esteem (SSCT), incorporate self-compassion and cognitive restructuring around moral self-judgment.

D. Psychoeducation

• For patient and family: Education regarding the nature of OCD, intrusive thoughts, compulsions, and associated anxiety/depressive symptoms, emphasising that intrusive thoughts are involuntary and do not reflect actual intentions or moral character.

• Rationale from formulation: Reduces shame and secrecy that have perpetuated the 4-year delay in treatment-seeking, and improves family capacity to respond supportively rather than reinforcing avoidance.

E. Anxiety and Stress Management

• Relaxation techniques (deep breathing, grounding, mindfulness, progressive muscle relaxation) to reduce moderate anxiety (BAI = 24) and general emotional distress.

• Stress management training to support overall emotional functioning alongside ERP.

F. Social Skills and Interpersonal Intervention

• Address interpersonal insecurity, fear of rejection, and discomfort in interactions with females identified on SSCT/TAT.

• Improve communication skills, self-confidence, and graded social engagement to reduce isolation and provide corrective interpersonal feedback that can help disconfirm catastrophic beliefs about his sexual thoughts.

G. Relapse Prevention

• Identify early warning signs (increase in ritual frequency, renewed thought suppression attempts) with patient and family.

• Develop a written action plan for managing setbacks and maintaining ERP gains.

• Regular psychiatric and psychological follow-up to monitor symptom progression, treatment response, and continuity of care.

12. FORMULATION SUMMARY (Brief Version for Clinical Records)

Rahul Ahirwar is a 19-year-old unmarried male with severe Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (Y-BOCS = 33) of 4 years' duration, presenting with intrusive negative and sexual thoughts, disturbing visual preoccupations, and compulsive eye rubbing and saliva-avoidance rituals, alongside comorbid severe depressive symptoms (BDI = 30) and moderate anxiety (BAI = 24).

Predisposing factors include a guilt-prone, high-responsibility cognitive schema, low self-esteem, marked interpersonal insecurity (particularly around females), an avoidant/internalising coping style, and a mildly disturbed, emotionally dependent relationship with his mother.

The disorder was precipitated by a conflict with his paternal uncle approximately four years ago, which catastrophically activated a normal intrusive thought about harm to family members into an obsessional focus, subsequently generalising to sexual and death-related intrusions.

Perpetuating factors include the negatively reinforcing neutralising rituals (eye rubbing, saliva avoidance), thought–action fusion and inflated responsibility beliefs, thought-suppression rebound effects, chronic rumination, secondary depression, and interpersonal avoidance limiting corrective social feedback.

Protective factors include good insight, strong treatment motivation, intact reality testing, young age, and an actively help-seeking family.

Formulation-based treatment plan prioritises SSRI pharmacotherapy alongside Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) targeting the specific obsessions and rituals identified, supplemented by cognitive therapy addressing inflated responsibility and thought–action fusion, psychoeducation for patient and family, anxiety management, and social-skills/interpersonal work, with structured relapse-prevention planning.
