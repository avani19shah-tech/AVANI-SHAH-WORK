## 1.1 What is Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT)?

**Simple version.** EFT is a psychotherapy that treats emotions as useful information, not noise. Instead of arguing with thoughts (as in CBT) or only changing behaviour, EFT helps a person *approach*, *feel*, *make sense of*, and *transform* their emotions. The core claim: people get stuck not because they feel too much, but because they cannot fully process what they feel — they avoid it, misname it, or get flooded by it.

**Academic version.** Emotion-Focused Therapy (also historically called process-experiential therapy) is a neo-humanistic, empirically-informed approach developed principally by **Leslie Greenberg**, with **Robert Elliott** and **Jeanne Watson**. It integrates client-centred (Rogerian) relational conditions with gestalt and emotion-theory-based active interventions (e.g., two-chair and empty-chair work). It rests on affective neuroscience and emotion theory: emotions are rapid, adaptive, action-oriented appraisals of situations relative to needs.

**Evidence grade for EFT as a treatment: Strong-to-Moderate** for depression and some interpersonal/trauma problems (multiple RCTs, meta-analytic support). **Its evidence in opioid dependence specifically is Limited/Emerging** — do not overclaim this in your dissertation.

Foundational sources (verified — see Module 6): Greenberg (2002, 2015); Greenberg & Paivio (1997); Elliott, Watson, Goldman & Greenberg (2004).

## 1.2 What is Emotion-Focused Skills Training (EFST), and how does it differ from full EFT?

This distinction matters for how you describe your intervention in the viva, so be precise.

| Dimension | **Full EFT (therapy)** | **EFST (skills training) — what your study delivers** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary goal | Resolve specific emotional injuries, transform maladaptive emotion schemes | Teach a set of transferable *emotional skills* (awareness, labelling, differentiation, expression, regulation) |
| Structure | Process-guided, follows the client's emerging emotional process; markers dictate interventions | Curriculum-based, session-by-session, psychoeducation + practice |
| Depth of experiential work | High — intensive chair work, deep emotional processing of core pain | Moderate/light — experiential exercises used carefully and briefly, prioritising stabilisation |
| Duration | Often 16–20+ sessions | Brief (yours: 8) |
| Who it suits | Clients ready for deep affective work | Clients who first need the *building blocks* of emotional competence — exactly the alexithymic profile |
| Therapist demand | High (specialised EFT training) | Moderate (structured manual reduces demand, though supervision still needed) |

> **Important framing for your thesis:** EFST is best described as *"an EFT-informed, structured emotional-skills curriculum,"* NOT as "brief EFT." Calling 8 structured sessions "EFT" would be criticised by an examiner who knows EFT. Note that "EFST" is also used by Greenberg and colleagues for a specific **parent/caregiver** training program; make clear in your write-up that you use the term generically for an emotion-skills curriculum for patients, and cite the emotion theory (EFT) as the source, not that specific parent program. **Evidence grade for "EFST" as a named, manualised patient treatment in addiction: Limited** — this is a construction, and you should own that.

## 1.3 Major principles / theoretical foundations of EFT

1. **Emotions are fundamentally adaptive.** They orient us to needs and prompt action (fear→protect, anger→set a boundary, sadness→seek comfort, shame→repair belonging).
2. **Emotional processing, not emotional venting, is curative.** The aim is to *symbolise* (put into words), make meaning, and transform — not merely discharge.
3. **"You have to arrive before you can leave."** You must first *reach* an emotion (feel it, name it) before it can change. This is the single most relevant principle for alexithymia work.
4. **Emotion changes emotion.** A maladaptive emotion (e.g., shame) is transformed by accessing a more adaptive one (e.g., healthy anger at mistreatment, or self-compassion), not by reasoning alone.
5. **The therapeutic relationship is the foundation.** Empathic attunement, validation, and safety are prerequisites — especially with shame-prone, stigmatised, trauma-exposed patients.
6. **A dialectical-constructivist view of the self:** meaning is co-constructed from bodily-felt experience plus reflection.

## 1.4 The role of emotions in psychological functioning

Emotions (a) rapidly appraise situations, (b) prepare action ("action tendencies"), (c) communicate to others, and (d) organise the self. When this system works, emotion is *information*. When someone cannot access or interpret this information (alexithymia), they lose an internal guidance system — and may substitute an external regulator (a drug). This is the theoretical hinge of your entire study.

---

## 1.5 Primary, secondary, maladaptive, and instrumental emotions

This taxonomy is core EFT and you should be able to explain it fluently.

- **Primary adaptive emotion** — the first, gut-level, *helpful* response that fits the situation. *E.g., sadness at a genuine loss; fear at real danger; anger at a real violation.* Target: help the client reach and use it.
- **Primary maladaptive emotion** — a first response that is *over-learned and no longer helpful*, usually from past injury. *E.g., core shame ("I am worthless"), or fear/distrust triggered by safe people.* Target: access it, then transform it with a new adaptive emotion.
- **Secondary reactive emotion** — a response *to* another emotion; it obscures the primary. *E.g., anger that covers hurt; anxiety that covers shame; numbness that covers fear.* Target: explore *past* it to the primary emotion underneath.
- **Instrumental emotion** — an emotion expressed (often out of awareness) to influence others. *E.g., "crocodile tears," intimidating anger to get compliance.* Target: gently make the function explicit.

**Why this matters for opioid dependence:** clinically you will very often meet **secondary anger or numbness masking primary shame, hurt, or fear**, and the drug used to shut all of it down. A large part of your work is helping the patient get *underneath* the secondary layer — but (safety point) only after basic awareness skills and stabilisation are in place.

## 1.6 Emotion schemes and emotional processing

An **emotion scheme** is EFT's central internal structure: a bundled memory network linking a situation + bodily sensation + emotion + belief/meaning + action tendency + need. It fires as a whole, fast, and often outside awareness. *Example:* [being criticised] → [chest tightens, face hot] → [shame] → ["I'm defective"] → [urge to hide/withdraw] → [unmet need: acceptance].

**Emotional processing** = activating the scheme in a tolerable way, putting it into words (symbolising), reflecting on it, and letting new experience update it. Alexithymia is, in this language, a **failure at the symbolising step**: the bodily-affective part fires, but it is never converted into a named, reflected-upon emotion. EFST is essentially *training the symbolising step*.

## 1.7 Why emotional awareness/processing may be impaired in substance-use disorders

Several non-exclusive explanations (grade in brackets):

- **Trait vulnerability / developmental** — poor early emotion socialisation, invalidating or traumatic environments, so emotion-labelling was never learned (**Moderate**).
- **Self-medication** — substances are recruited to damp down unbearable, undifferentiated affect; over time the person relies on the drug instead of internal regulation, and the skill atrophies (Khantzian) (**Theoretical/Moderate** — influential but hard to test causally).
- **Neuroadaptation** — chronic opioid use and repeated withdrawal affect reward, stress (HPA), and prefrontal/insular circuits involved in interoception and emotion; blunted interoception → weaker emotional signal (**Emerging**).
- **State effects** — intoxication, withdrawal, early abstinence, sleep disruption, and depression all *transiently* flatten or distort emotion. This is a **major confound** for you (see Module 5): some "alexithymia" post-detox is state, not trait.

## 1.8 Alexithymia and substance use — the relationship

**Established (Moderate–Strong):**
- Alexithymia is markedly more prevalent in SUD than the general population (your synopsis figure of ~40–67% in opioid dependence vs 8–10% is consistent with the literature).
- Meta-analytic work shows a reliable association between alexithymia and psychoactive substance use, with **stronger effects in diagnosed SUD and for opioids/depressants/alcohol** (Westwood et al., 2023 — verified).
- Emotion dysregulation and substance use are meta-analytically related (r ≈ 0.19; Weiss et al., 2022 — verified), and poor **distress tolerance / inability to tolerate negative emotion** predicts return to use (Berking et al., 2011, alcohol — verified).
- Alexithymia in heroin users is linked to impaired **emotion recognition** (Ferrari et al., 2016 — verified).

**Not established / be careful:**
- That alexithymia *causes* opioid dependence (mostly cross-sectional; direction unclear).
- That reducing alexithymia *reduces relapse* (plausible, mechanistically attractive, but **not demonstrated** — do not assert it as fact; your study does not test relapse anyway).

---

## 1.9 Why alexithymia may drive craving, relapse, impulsivity, avoidance, interpersonal difficulty, poor coping

A mechanistic chain (label it **theoretical/mechanistic**, supported in parts):

- **Craving/relapse:** an unlabelled negative state ("I feel bad, I don't know why") cannot be addressed at its source, so the fastest available fix (the drug) is used. Naming and understanding the state opens non-drug options.
- **Impulsivity:** without an emotional "signal" that can be reflected on, there is a shorter path from feeling to action.
- **Avoidance/experiential avoidance:** if inner states are confusing and aversive, the person avoids attending inward at all (this *is* the EOT component) — which prevents learning.
- **Interpersonal difficulty:** if you cannot describe your feelings (DDF) or read others', relationships become conflictual or distant, generating more negative affect → more use.
- **Poor coping:** coping requires appraising *what* is wrong; alexithymia blocks the appraisal step.

## 1.10 How improving emotional awareness could *theoretically* improve substance-use outcomes — and the mechanisms by which EFST could reduce alexithymia

**Proposed mechanisms of EFST → lower alexithymia** (all **Theoretical/Emerging** — this is your thesis's mechanistic argument, not settled fact):

1. **Interoceptive re-training** (Sessions on body–emotion link): repeatedly directing attention to bodily sensation and pairing it with an emotion word strengthens the sensation→label mapping → targets **DIF**.
2. **Vocabulary acquisition** (emotion-word building): you cannot name what you have no word for; expanding the lexicon directly targets **DDF** and enables DIF.
3. **Repeated labelling practice** ("affect labelling"): naming a felt state is itself mildly regulating and, with repetition, becomes more automatic → DIF + regulation. (Affect-labelling has independent experimental support — **Moderate** — though mostly in non-addiction samples.)
4. **Differentiation practice:** learning to separate "bad" into anger/shame/fear/sadness increases *emotional granularity*, which is associated with better regulation → DIF/DDF.
5. **Attention re-orientation inward** (against EOT): structured curiosity about inner experience counteracts the externally-oriented habit → targets **EOT** (note: **EOT is the hardest and slowest to move** — see Module 5).
6. **Corrective relational experience:** an attuned therapist naming and validating feelings models symbolising and reduces the shame that keeps emotion suppressed.
7. **Substitution of regulation strategy:** as internal labelling/regulation improves, the *functional need* for the drug-as-regulator theoretically weakens.

Keep the honesty line visible: **mechanisms 1–6 are individually plausible and partly supported; the packaged claim "8 sessions of EFST lowers trait alexithymia in opioid-dependent inpatients" is what you are testing, not something you can assume.**

## 1.11 ALEXITHYMIA IN DEPTH (your request section 2)

### 1.11.1 Definition & conceptualisation
Alexithymia (Greek *a-lexis-thymos* = "no words for emotion"; Sifneos, 1973) is a **dimensional personality trait** (not a diagnosis) characterised by difficulty identifying and describing feelings and an externally-oriented cognitive style. It reflects a deficit in the **cognitive processing and regulation of emotion**, not an absence of emotion. People with alexithymia still have physiological emotional arousal — they just can't symbolise/interpret it.

### 1.11.2 The three components (as measured by TAS-20)
- **DIF — Difficulty Identifying Feelings:** can't tell *which* emotion is present, or can't separate an emotion from a bodily sensation of arousal. ("I feel something in my chest but I don't know if it's fear or anger.")
- **DDF — Difficulty Describing Feelings:** can identify *that* there is a feeling but can't put it into words to others. ("I know I feel something, I just can't explain it.")
- **EOT — Externally Oriented Thinking:** attention is habitually on external, concrete, factual matters rather than inner experience; low "psychological mindedness." ("Why talk about feelings? I just get on with things.")

> Clinical nuance: **DIF and DDF tend to move first and most** with intervention; **EOT is the most stable / hardest to shift** and, in some samples, barely moves. Your Module 5 monitoring must expect this pattern.

### 1.11.3 How alexithymia presents clinically
- Vague, global affect words ("bad," "stressed," "not okay," "tension") with no differentiation.
- Long, concrete external narratives ("then he said, then I went, then the bus was late") with the emotional content absent.
- Rapid pivot from feeling questions to facts/logistics.
- Prominent **somatic** complaints (aches, restlessness, "gastric," palpitations) standing in for emotion.
- Puzzled or irritated response to "How did you feel?" ("What do you mean, how did I feel?").
- Limited fantasy/imaginative life; dreams reported as flat or absent.

### 1.11.4 How it can look *different* in substance-dependent patients
- **Masked by drug talk:** feelings collapsed into craving language ("I just wanted to use") — the drug word replaces the emotion word.
- **Masked by "fine":** institutional/defended presentation ("I'm sorted now, sir, no problem").
- **State overlay:** post-detox low mood, anhedonia, poor sleep and concentration mimic and worsen alexithymia.
- **Shame-driven concealment** can look like EOT but is actually avoidance — you must distinguish them (below).
- **Restlessness/craving** presented as the *only* internal experience available.

### 1.11.5 Recognising alexithymia in the clinical interview — what you'll observe & hear
Typical patient statements:
- "I don't know." / "Nothing." / "I'm fine." / "Normal."
- "I was just angry." (single undifferentiated label for everything)
- "My body was restless / heavy / hot." (somatic only)
- "I wanted to use." (craving substituted for emotion)
- "I don't think about feelings." / "What's the point of this?"
- "I can't explain it." / "There's nothing to talk about."

**Behavioural signs:** flat or incongruent affect; discomfort/impatience with emotion questions; over-focus on practical detail; difficulty free-associating; better at describing others' actions than own inner states.
**Cognitive signs:** concrete, externally-oriented reasoning; low curiosity about causes of own reactions; "just happens" attributions.
**Somatic manifestations:** headaches, GI distress, muscle tension, restlessness, fatigue, sleep problems presented without emotional context.
**Interpersonal manifestations:** conflict from unexpressed needs; partners/family say "he never talks"; misreading others; withdrawal or sudden outbursts.

### 1.11.6 Distinguishing alexithymia from look-alikes (critical for your DV validity)
| Look-alike | How to tell it apart from alexithymia |
|---|---|
| **Emotional suppression** | Suppressor *knows* the feeling but chooses not to show it ("I felt hurt but I hide it"). Alexithymic genuinely can't identify it. |
| **Depression** | Depression is episodic, mood-congruent, and responds to mood over time; alexithymia is a stable style and can be present when mood is euthymic. They co-occur — hence you use **BDI-II as a covariate**. |
| **Anxiety** | Anxious patients often over-report/over-monitor internal sensations; alexithymic ones under-interpret them. |
| **Limited vocabulary / low literacy / education** | Give a *feelings-word choice* (cards/faces). If they can pick the right word when offered, it's vocabulary, not alexithymia. |
| **Intellectual disability** | Screened out by your IQ<70 exclusion; still consider borderline functioning. |
| **Withdrawal/intoxication / early abstinence** | State-bound and time-limited; re-assess after stabilisation (your ≥7-day post-detox rule helps but does not fully remove this). |
| **Cultural/linguistic norms** | In many Indian settings, somatic idioms of distress ("tension," "gas," "heaviness in head") are *normal* ways to express emotion, not deficits. Interpret against local norms and in the patient's own language; use the validated Hindi TAS-20. |

**What a therapist might observe → and say:**
- *Observe:* patient answers "how did you feel?" with a 3-minute account of what his brother did.
  *Say:* "You've told me clearly what happened — that helps. I want to slow down on one thing: while your brother was saying that, what was going on *inside you*, in your body?"
- *Observe:* "I felt bad."
  *Say:* "'Bad' is a good starting point. Let's zoom in — was it more the heavy kind of bad, the angry kind, the scared kind, or the ashamed kind?" (offer options — scaffolding, not leading).

## 1.12 Technique → target → mechanism → expected change (your request section 3, overview)

The full toolbox is in Module 3; here is the logic map so you can see how each skill maps to DIF/DDF/EOT.

| Technique | Primary alexithymia target | Mechanism | Expected change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body-sensation noticing / focusing | DIF | Strengthens interoception; reconnects sensation to affect | Can locate & describe bodily correlates of emotion |
| Emotion vocabulary building | DDF (then DIF) | Supplies labels needed to symbolise | More/differentiated feeling words used spontaneously |
| Emotion labelling ("name it") | DIF | Affect labelling; sensation→word mapping | Faster, more accurate naming; mild in-session calming |
| Emotion differentiation | DIF/DDF | Increases emotional granularity | "Bad" splits into anger/shame/fear/sadness |
| Intensity rating (0–10) | DIF | Adds a graded dimension to a binary sense | Can scale feelings, notice change over time |
| Trigger identification | DIF/EOT | Links external event → internal state | "It just happened" becomes "X led to feeling Y" |
| Emotion function ("what is it for?") | EOT | Builds psychological mindedness | Sees emotions as meaningful signals |
| Underlying/primary emotion work | DIF (deep) | Gets beneath secondary anger/numbness | Recognises hurt/shame under anger |
| Unmet-need identification | EOT | Connects emotion to need | "I was angry because I needed respect" |
| Emotional expression skills | DDF | Practises symbolising to another | Communicates a feeling in words |
| Self-soothing / regulation | regulation (enables DIF) | Down-regulates arousal so feeling is tolerable | Can stay with emotion without flooding/using |
| Reducing experiential avoidance | EOT | Approach instead of avoid inner experience | Turns toward feelings; less "I don't want to think about it" |
| Substitution for substance | outcome-level | Replaces drug-as-regulator | Uses a named coping response instead of using |

**Most directly relevant per component:**
- **DIF:** body/focusing, labelling, differentiation, intensity rating.
- **DDF:** vocabulary building, expression skills, describing-to-others practice.
- **EOT** (hardest): trigger identification, emotion-function work, reducing experiential avoidance, curiosity-building. Expect the least movement here.

## 1.13 EFT vs other therapies (your request section 13)

| Approach | Core mechanism | Overlap with EFST | Distinctly its own | Usefulness for alexithymia | Don't mislabel as EFT |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **EFT** | Access & transform emotion via experiencing | — | Chair work, emotion transformation, marker-guided process | High (deep processing) | — |
| **EFST (your intervention)** | Teach emotional skills (aware→name→differentiate→express→regulate) | shares EFT emotion theory | curriculum/skills format | High for DIF/DDF (that's its point) | It IS EFT-informed; don't call it "brief EFT" |
| **CBT** | Change thoughts/behaviour to change emotion | psychoeducation, monitoring, homework, thought–feeling links | cognitive restructuring, behavioural experiments | Moderate — labelling/monitoring help; less experiential depth | Thought records are **not** EFT |
| **DBT** | Skills to regulate intense emotion + acceptance | emotion labelling, distress tolerance, "describe" skill | dialectics, TIPP, chain analysis, mindfulness modules | High — DBT "Emotion Regulation/Mindfulness: Describe" directly builds DIF/DDF (Moderate evidence for reducing alexithymia) | DBT skills are **not** EFT even when similar |
| **Motivational Interviewing** | Resolve ambivalence, build change motivation | empathic, reflective stance; useful *engagement* front-end | change talk, rolling with resistance | Low direct effect on alexithymia; high for engagement | Reflective listening ≠ EFT emotion work |
| **Relapse Prevention** | Identify triggers, plan coping | trigger identification, coping planning | high-risk situation analysis, cognitive-behavioural coping | Moderate — trigger/emotion links help DIF/EOT | RP coping plans are **not** EFT |
| **ACT** | Acceptance + values + defusion; reduce experiential avoidance | acceptance of emotion, reducing avoidance | defusion, values, committed action | Moderate — especially for EOT/avoidance | Defusion/values are **not** EFT |
| **Standard psychoeducation** | Give information | the didactic portions of early sessions | information delivery only | Low alone — necessary but insufficient; must be paired with practice | Info-giving is **not** EFT |

**Take-away for your write-up:** Your intervention is **EFT-informed emotional-skills training**. It legitimately *borrows compatible techniques* (labelling and describing overlap with DBT; trigger work overlaps with RP; acceptance/anti-avoidance overlaps with ACT). Be transparent about this borrowing — examiners respect precision more than purity. Reserve the label "distinctly EFT" for: working from bodily-felt sense (focusing), distinguishing primary/secondary/maladaptive emotions, getting to the underlying primary emotion and unmet need, and (if used) chair work.
