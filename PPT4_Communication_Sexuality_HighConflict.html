Section 1

Communication & Conflict Resolution

20 Minutes

Levels of Communication in Couples

Three Levels of Every Message

LevelWhat It IsExample
ContentThe literal words / topic being discussed"You didn't call me back."
ProcessHOW the message is delivered (tone, timing, pattern)Said with eye-roll at dinner table in front of in-laws
RelationalWhat it says about the relationship / attachment"I don't matter to you. You don't prioritize me."
Clinical Insight: Couples fight about content but are hurt at the relational level. Effective therapy addresses ALL three levels simultaneously.

The Speaker-Listener Technique

Rules for the Speaker (holds "the floor")

  • Use "I" statements ("I feel..." not "You always...")
  • Keep statements brief (1-2 sentences)
  • Pause after each statement for paraphrase
  • Stay on ONE topic at a time

Rules for the Listener

  • Paraphrase what you heard (don't rebut)
  • Don't offer solutions yet
  • Ask: "Did I get that right?"
  • Request the "floor" to respond when ready

Active Listening Components

Six Core Skills for Couples

SkillWhat It Looks LikeExample
AttendingEye contact, open posture, noddingPutting phone down, turning toward partner
Reflecting ContentParaphrasing the facts"So you're saying the meeting went badly..."
Reflecting FeelingsNaming the emotion beneath words"It sounds like you felt humiliated."
SummarizingPulling together key themes"So overall you're feeling overwhelmed and unsupported."
ValidatingAcknowledging the emotion makes sense"Given what happened, of course you'd feel that way."
Open QuestionsInviting elaboration without judgment"What was the hardest part for you?"
Clinical Tip: Validation ≠ Agreement. You can validate your partner's FEELING without agreeing with their INTERPRETATION. This distinction is crucial to teach couples.

Gottman's Repair Attempts

The Secret Weapon of Successful Couples

A repair attempt is ANY statement or action that prevents negativity from escalating out of control during conflict.

CategoryExamples
Feelings"I'm getting scared." / "This is important to me."
Responsibility"My part in this was..." / "I overreacted."
De-escalation"Can we take a break?" / "Let me try again."
Appreciation"I know this isn't easy for you either." / "Thank you for listening."
HumorInside joke, silly face, pet name (when appropriate)
PhysicalReaching for hand, hug offer, reducing physical distance
Key Research Finding: The success of repair attempts is the PRIMARY difference between "masters" and "disasters" of relationships (Gottman, 1999). It's not that happy couples don't fight — it's that their repairs LAND.

Conflict Resolution: Solvable vs. Perpetual

Gottman's Discovery: 69% of all couple conflicts are PERPETUAL — they will never be fully resolved. Only 31% are solvable problems.

Solvable Problems

  • Situational, specific, addressable
  • Strategy: Problem-solving steps
  • 1. Softened startup
  • 2. Accept influence
  • 3. Repair & de-escalate
  • 4. Compromise
  • 5. Process any injuries

Example: "Who does the dishes tonight?"

Perpetual Problems

  • Rooted in personality or value differences
  • Strategy: "Dreams Within Conflict"
  • 1. Explore underlying dream/hope/value
  • 2. Understand WHY it matters so deeply
  • 3. Find areas of flexibility
  • 4. Establish temporary compromise
  • 5. Revisit periodically with dialogue

Example: "One wants kids, the other doesn't"

Softened Startup Formula

The Formula: "I feel... about... I need..."

Research: 96% of the time, the way a conversation STARTS determines how it will END (Gottman, 1999). Harsh startup = escalation guaranteed.

Before & After Examples

Harsh Startup (Criticism)Softened Startup
"You NEVER help with the kids! You're so selfish!""I feel overwhelmed with bedtime alone. I need us to share that responsibility."
"You always spend money on useless things!""I feel anxious about our finances. I need us to discuss a budget together."
"Your mother runs our life and you're too weak to stop it!""I feel unsupported when decisions are made without me. I need us to be a team."
Teaching Tip: Give couples a "formula card" to keep in their wallet:
"I feel [emotion] about [specific situation]. I need [positive need]."

Rewrite These Criticisms as Softened Startups

With your partner, transform each harsh criticism into a softened startup using "I feel... about... I need..."

1. "You're always on your phone! You don't care about this family!"

2. "You never stand up for me in front of your parents. You're a coward."

3. "You only think about sex. You don't even ask how my day was!"

1. "I feel lonely when we don't talk in the evenings. I need us to have 20 minutes of phone-free time together."

2. "I feel hurt and unprotected when my concerns are dismissed. I need to know you'll speak up for us as a couple."

3. "I feel disconnected when we don't check in emotionally. I need us to talk about our days before being intimate."

Sexual & Intimacy Counselling

20 Minutes

Models of Sexual Response

Three Key Models

ModelPhasesKey Feature
Masters & Johnson (1966)Excitement → Plateau → Orgasm → ResolutionLinear; based on physiological response; same for both sexes
Kaplan (1979)Desire → Excitement → OrgasmAdded DESIRE as separate phase; 3-phase; allowed for desire disorders
Basson (2000)Emotional intimacy → Responsive desire → Arousal → Satisfaction (circular)CIRCULAR model; most clinically useful for women; desire can follow arousal
Clinical Implication: Basson's circular model explains why many women (and some men) don't experience "spontaneous" desire. Responsive desire is NORMAL — educating couples about this reduces pathologizing.

WHO Components of Sexual Health

  • Physical capacity for sexual response
  • Freedom from organic disorders/diseases
  • Freedom from psychological issues that inhibit response
  • Ability to have pleasurable experiences without fear, shame, or guilt

Common Causes in Indian Context

  • Arranged marriage anxiety: First night pressure, strangers expected to be intimate
  • Lack of sex education: No school-based or family-based education for most
  • Cultural shame: Sex = dirty/sinful; women not "supposed" to enjoy it
  • Joint family/privacy issues: Thin walls, shared rooms, constant surveillance
  • Porn-based expectations: Unrealistic standards from internet pornography

Sensate Focus Protocol (Masters & Johnson)

Four Progressive Stages

StageActivityKey Principle
Stage 1Non-genital touching (back, arms, legs, face)Focus on sensation, NOT arousal; no breasts or genitals
Stage 2Including genitals & breasts (exploration only)Still NO goal of arousal or orgasm; curiosity-driven
Stage 3Mutual touching simultaneouslyCommunication about what feels good; give-and-receive
Stage 4Non-demand intercourse (containment without thrusting)Gradual; ONLY when both ready; no performance pressure
Three Key Principles:
1. Curiosity over performance — "What does this feel like?" not "Am I doing it right?"
2. Mindfulness — Stay present with sensation, not outcome
3. No goal — Explicitly ban orgasm/intercourse in early stages to remove pressure

Indian Context: Sexual Counselling Considerations

Important: Many Indian couples presenting with "sexual dysfunction" actually have NO sexual education. The first intervention is often psychoeducation, not therapy.

Key Considerations

  • Consummation pressure: Family may ask "any good news?" within weeks of marriage; couple may feel defective
  • Joint family privacy: Homework assignments (sensate focus) may be impossible if couple shares room or has no lock on door
  • Cultural shame: Women may never have seen or touched their own genitalia; men may rely solely on pornography for "education"
  • Language barriers: Many Indian languages lack non-vulgar words for genitals; develop comfortable vocabulary WITH the couple
  • Gender role expectations: "Good women don't initiate"; men must "perform" or feel emasculated
Clinical Approach:
1. Permission-giving: "It's okay to talk about this. It's okay to enjoy this."
2. Psychoeducation: Anatomy, arousal, responsive desire (normalize)
3. Graduated exposure: Sensate focus adapted to available privacy
4. Include both partners: Education must be joint to avoid blame

Section 3

High Conflict Couples: DV, Infidelity & In-Laws

20 Minutes

Emotional Abuse & Coercive Control

⚠ SAFETY FIRST: Couple therapy is CONTRAINDICATED when there is ongoing violence, fear, or coercive control. Individual safety planning takes priority over relationship work.

Forms of Coercive Control

  • Verbal abuse: Name-calling, humiliation, constant criticism
  • Gaslighting: "That never happened" / "You're crazy"
  • Isolation: Cutting off from family, friends, support
  • Monitoring: Checking phone, tracking location, controlling movements
  • Economic abuse: Controlling finances, preventing employment
  • Threats: To harm, leave, take children, involve family
  • Using children: Alienation, using as messengers/spies
  • Sexual coercion: Marital rape, forced acts, withholding

When Couple Therapy is CONTRAINDICATED

  • Ongoing physical or sexual violence
  • One partner expresses FEAR of the other
  • Pattern of coercive control (even without physical violence)
  • Active substance use with violent behaviour
  • Stalking or threats after separation

Infidelity: Types & Treatment

Types of Infidelity

TypeDefinitionKey Features
SexualPhysical/sexual contact outside relationshipMay be one-time or ongoing affair
EmotionalDeep emotional bond with another (without sex)Often perceived as greater betrayal by women
Cyber/OnlineSexting, porn use, dating apps, emotional online relationshipsIncreasingly common; boundary ambiguity

Gottman's Trust Revival Method (3 Phases)

Phase 1: Atonement — Unfaithful partner takes full responsibility. Answers ALL questions honestly (no trickle truth). Expresses genuine remorse. Cuts contact with affair partner.

Phase 2: Attunement — Rebuild emotional connection. Address WHY the affair happened (not to excuse, but understand). Work on the A-T-T-U-N-E system (Awareness, Turning toward, Tolerance, Understanding, Non-defensive, Empathy).

Phase 3: Attachment — Create new shared meaning. Build a new relationship narrative. Establish rituals of connection. Discuss "What did we learn about what we need?"

Indian Context: In India, infidelity carries extreme shame. Family honour (izzat) is at stake. Couples may present with "adjustment problem" rather than disclosing affairs. Confidentiality is paramount.

In-Law Conflicts (Indian-Specific)

Common Patterns in Indian Families

  • Mother-in-law vs. daughter-in-law: Power struggle over son/husband's loyalty and household control
  • Triangulation: Son caught between wife and mother; avoids taking sides
  • Boundary diffusion: No clear marital subsystem boundary; elders override couple decisions
  • Financial control: Joint family income managed by elders; young couple has no autonomy
  • Comparison & criticism: "My son could have married better" / constant complaints about bride

Therapeutic Strategies

Couple-Level Work

  • United front rule: Decisions made together, presented together
  • Blood-relative manages own family: He talks to his parents; she talks to hers
  • Clarify expectations: What are non-negotiable boundaries?

Structural Interventions

  • Boundary strengthening: Physical space (lock on door), decision-making autonomy
  • Hierarchy realignment: Couple = primary unit, not parent-child
  • Graduated separation: Move to separate floor/house if possible
Cultural Sensitivity: Don't frame it as "choose wife over mother." Frame it as: "How can you be a good son AND a good husband? Both roles require different responses."
⚠ SAFETY ASSESSMENT PRACTICE – 5 Minutes

Vignette: Is Couple Therapy Appropriate?

Case: Meera (29) and Vikram (34), married 4 years, one child (age 2). Meera says: "He controls everything – my phone, my money, who I can visit. Last month he pushed me against the wall during an argument. His parents say I provoke him. I'm here because my mother said to try counselling before doing anything drastic. I don't want to upset his family."

Vikram says: "She exaggerates. I just held her shoulders to calm her down. She's very dramatic. I check her phone because I don't trust her friend circle – they put ideas in her head."

Quick Safety Screen (Answer Yes/No)

  1. Is there physical violence (even "minor")? ___
  2. Does one partner express FEAR of the other? ___
  3. Is there a pattern of monitoring/control? ___
  4. Is the "problem" being minimized or blamed on the victim? ___
  5. Would honest disclosure in session PUT ONE PARTNER AT RISK? ___
Clinical Decision: Couple therapy is CONTRAINDICATED.

Rationale: Physical violence (pushing = violence). Pattern of coercive control (phone monitoring, financial control, isolation). Minimization and blame-shifting by perpetrator. Victim cannot speak freely in joint session without risk.

Appropriate Actions:
1. Individual session with Meera to assess safety & create safety plan
2. Referral to DV helpline (Women Helpline 181 / One Stop Centre)
3. Individual therapy for Vikram (anger management, accountability group)
4. Couple therapy ONLY after: violence stops, accountability established, Meera reports no fear

Hour 4: Key Takeaways

1. Communication: The Speaker-Listener technique + softened startup formula are foundational skills. 96% of conflicts end the way they start — teach couples to start softly.
2. Repair is Everything: Successful repair attempts are the #1 difference between happy and unhappy couples. Teach couples multiple repair strategies.
3. Sexual Health: Basson's circular model is most clinically useful. Many Indian couples need psychoeducation before therapy. Sensate Focus removes performance pressure systematically.
4. Safety First: ALWAYS screen for DV/coercive control before starting couple therapy. Couple therapy with active violence = dangerous. Individual safety planning comes first.
5. In-Law Work: In India, couple therapy often means family-system therapy. "Blood-relative manages own family" + united front + boundary strengthening are core strategies.
