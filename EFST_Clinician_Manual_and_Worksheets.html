PART 1 — The Clinician's Guide

Everything you need to understand and deliver EFST: the model, the technique toolbox, how to formulate each case, and what to do (and avoid) with opioid-dependent patients, including those living with HIV.

1. What alexithymia is & why it matters in your sample

Alexithymia (literally "no words for feelings") is a difficulty in the conscious experience and verbal description of emotion. At its core is a disruption of emotional awareness — the conscious feeling states central to mental life (Smith et al., "Alexithymia," PMC8456171). The TAS-20 you are using captures three facets:

TAS-20 factorWhat the patient struggles withEFST target
DIF — Difficulty Identifying FeelingsTells arousal from bodily sensation apart; "I feel bad but I don't know what it is."Interoceptive & emotional awareness (Sessions 1–2, 4)
DDF — Difficulty Describing FeelingsFinding words; communicating emotion to others.Emotion vocabulary & expression (Sessions 3, 5)
EOT — Externally-Oriented ThinkingAttention fixed on external events/facts, away from inner life.Shifting attention inward; empathy (Sessions 4, 6)
Why this matters clinically. In substance-dependent populations, emotional-processing deficits are linked to craving, poorer treatment adherence, and relapse — which is exactly why reducing alexithymia is a meaningful intervention target rather than just a trait to measure. Deficits in emotion recognition are a common, and importantly trainable, feature of alexithymia, so skills-based training is a logical treatment route (Pedrosa Gil et al., 2019 / smartphone emotion-recognition training, PMID 31110950). Encouraging evidence that alexithymia is modifiable. A 2024 systematic review found most psychological interventions significantly reduced TAS-20 scores across trials (Tsubaki & Shimizu, 2024, Behav. Sci. 14(12):1173, doi:10.3390/bs14121173). A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall medium effect of interventions versus control (g ≈ −0.52) (PMID 41525940). This supports your hypothesis that a brief structured EFST can shift a trait often assumed to be stable.

2. The EFST model & its evidence base

EFST adapts the principles of Emotion-Focused Therapy (Greenberg, 2002) into a structured, skills-based format suitable for brief clinical delivery. The working assumption is that emotions are adaptive sources of information; alexithymia is a breakdown in accessing and symbolising that information. Training rebuilds the chain: notice body → attend inward → name → describe → use the emotion adaptively.

Your protocol is consistent with published emotional-self-awareness trainings. A well-documented parallel is an eight-lesson programme combining psychoeducation and skill-building to teach emotional vocabulary, labelling and differentiating one's own emotions, interoceptive awareness, and distinguishing emotions from thoughts, actions and sensations (Neumann et al., 2017, Phase I trial, PMID 28060205; TREAT RCT, 2024, PMID 39154927). Content rephrased for licensing compliance.

Theoretical anchors (cite in your write-up):
  • Greenberg (2002) — Emotion-Focused Therapy; emotions as adaptive, workable in session.
  • Khantzian (1997) — Self-Medication Hypothesis: substances used to manage unbearable/unnamed affect; naming affect weakens that function.
  • Lane & Schwartz (1987) — Levels of Emotional Awareness: awareness develops in stages (bodily sensation → action tendency → single emotion → blends of emotion) — your skills ladder mirrors this.

3. Core EFST techniques — the toolbox

These are the transdiagnostic micro-skills you will rotate across the 8 sessions. Learn them as a therapist first; Part 2 operationalises each into a patient worksheet.

3.1 Empathic attunement & validation

The foundation. You continually reflect the patient's experience in slightly more emotionally-specific language than they used ("so there's a heaviness there... almost like a weight") — never correcting, always offering a tentative word they can accept or refine. This models emotional language and builds the safety alexithymic, trauma-exposed patients need.

3.2 Interoceptive / body-focused awareness

Guide attention to bodily sensations (chest tightness, stomach, throat, jaw, heat) as the raw data of emotion. For alexithymia specifically, awareness-of-sensation techniques improve interoception and downstream emotion processing (Herbert & Pollatos — motivated cue integration, PMC6524402). Use a slow "body scan" and a sensation-to-emotion bridge ("that tight chest — if it had a feeling name, what might it be?").

3.3 Emotion labelling & vocabulary building

Expand from the 4–5 words most alexithymic patients use ("fine," "bad," "tense," "angry") to a graded vocabulary, using an emotion wheel and intensity words. Labelling ("name it to tame it") itself reduces amygdala reactivity and is a core DDF intervention.

3.4 Differentiating emotion from thought, sensation & action

Directly targets EOT. Teach the four-column split — Situation / Thought / Body sensation / Emotion — so the patient stops substituting a fact or a plan ("I'll just leave") for a feeling.

3.5 The emotion-episode "unpacking" sequence

The workhorse. For a recent episode, walk: cue → body → first feeling → secondary feeling → what the feeling needed → what I did. This is the SPAARS/EFT-style tracking that converts a vague "bad day" into a named, workable emotion.

3.6 Expression & disclosure skills

Move DDF from private naming to interpersonal description. Teach "I feel ___ because ___, and I need ___" and rehearse it in session via role-play before real-world use.

3.7 Empathy / reading others' emotions

Use faces, scenarios and perspective-taking to build recognition of others' affect — strengthens social emotion processing and reduces the external focus of EOT.

3.8 Linking emotion to the addiction cycle

The clinical payload: map how unrecognised emotion → rising arousal → craving → use → short relief → shame. Inserting the new skill ("name + body + need") at the craving node is the relapse-prevention bridge.

4. Case formulation — step by step

Do this once per patient after your baseline battery (TAS-20, DERS, BDI-II, SDS, sociodemographic sheet). It makes the 8 sessions individualised rather than generic and gives you qualitative data for your discussion section.

  1. Presenting picture & baseline profile. Record TAS-20 total + factor scores (DIF/DDF/EOT), DERS subscales, BDI-II, SDS, HIV status and ART status. Note which factor is highest — that becomes your emphasis.
  2. Developmental & maintaining factors. Early emotional environment, trauma, when substance use started relative to emotional difficulties (self-medication framing, Khantzian).
  3. Function of the substance. Which emotions does opioid use switch off? (Often shame, fear, emptiness, boredom.) This names your treatment targets.
  4. The emotion→craving→use chain for this person — write their typical sequence in their words.
  5. Strengths & readiness. Literacy, motivation, cognitive capacity, support — informs pacing.
  6. HIV considerations (if HIV+). Neurocognitive load, fatigue, stigma, disclosure stress — may slow processing and require shorter inward-attention segments.
  7. Goals in the patient's language + the 2–3 skills you will prioritise.

5. Dos & Don'ts for this population

✓ DO

  • Start every session with a brief body check-in and end with a summary + homework.
  • Offer feeling words tentatively and let the patient choose/correct them.
  • Use concrete, recent, specific episodes — not abstract "how do you feel about life?"
  • Normalise "I don't know" as a starting point, not a failure.
  • Keep language simple and literacy-appropriate; use the emotion wheel and body diagrams.
  • Titrate intensity — small, tolerable doses of emotion with grounding available.
  • Explicitly link each skill back to craving/relapse so it feels relevant.
  • Watch for and respond to shame; validate before exploring.
  • Keep HIV status strictly confidential; follow your coded-ID procedure.
  • Document fidelity (which worksheet sections were completed) for your study.

✗ DON'T

  • Don't flood or push catharsis — deep emotion work can destabilise a post-detox patient.
  • Don't interpret or tell the patient what they "really" feel.
  • Don't treat intellectual insight as emotional awareness (that's EOT in disguise).
  • Don't rush naming before the body/sensation step — it reinforces guessing.
  • Don't moralise about use; shame fuels the cycle you're treating.
  • Don't open trauma you can't close within the session; contain and refer.
  • Don't ignore signs of acute distress, intoxication, or medical instability — pause and refer to the treating team.
  • Don't let HIV status change your warmth or create differential handling that the patient can sense.
  • Don't deviate from the 8-session structure in ways that threaten protocol fidelity.
  • Don't collect data from anyone who hasn't completed informed consent.

6. Managing distress, ruptures & HIV-specific issues

If a patient becomes acutely distressed: stop the exploration, ground (feet on floor, slow breaths, name 5 things in the room), validate ("this is hard, and stopping here is okay"), re-stabilise, and if distress persists refer to the treating psychiatrist/psychologist per your protocol. Note it in your records.

Alliance ruptures (withdrawal, irritation, "this is pointless") are expected with EOT-high, shame-prone patients. Name it gently, take responsibility for pacing, and slow down — don't push.

HIV-positive patients may present with fatigue, slowed processing, or disclosure-related anxiety. Shorten inward-attention segments, allow more repetition, and keep a non-stigmatising stance. Remember your study is specifically testing whether HIV status moderates response — so document qualitative differences (pace, engagement, fatigue) carefully.

The self-report paradox (note for your limitations section). Alexithymic patients may under-report their own deficits on the TAS-20. Watch for a gap between a "good" score and obvious in-session difficulty naming feelings, and record it as clinical observation.

Session 1 — What Are Emotions?

Goal: psychoeducation + permission to feel · Targets DIF/EOT · 60 min

Teach (say this simply): Emotions are signals — like messages from inside your body telling you something matters. There are no "bad" emotions; each one has a job. Anger says a line was crossed. Fear says watch out. Sadness says something was lost. Our goal is not to get rid of feelings but to hear them.

The 6 basic emotions & their "jobs":
😠 Anger → something's unfair😨 Fear → danger/threat😢 Sadness → loss😀 Joy → something good🤢 Disgust → keep away😲 Surprise → something new

Session 2 — The Body–Emotion Connection

Goal: interoceptive awareness · Targets DIF · 60 min

Teach: Feelings live in the body before we have words for them. A tight chest, a hot face, a heavy stomach — these are the first clues. If we learn to read the body, we can find the feeling.

Lead a 2-minute body scan first: eyes soft, feet on floor, slowly move attention from head → jaw → throat → chest → stomach → hands. "Notice any tightness, warmth, heaviness — no need to change it."

Session 4 — Identifying Feelings in Daily Life

Goal: separate feeling from thought/action · Targets DIF/EOT · 60 min

Teach: We often say a thought or a plan when asked how we feel ("I think he's wrong," "I'll just leave"). A feeling is one word (sad, angry, scared). Let's learn to tell them apart.

Session 5 — Describing Feelings to Others

Goal: express emotion interpersonally · Targets DDF · 60 min

Teach: Knowing a feeling inside is step one; saying it to someone is step two. A simple, safe way is the "I" statement: "I feel ___ because ___, and I need ___."

Session 7 — Emotions & the Addiction Cycle

Goal: link unrecognised emotion to craving/relapse · The clinical core · 60 min

Teach: Unnamed feelings build up like pressure. For many people, that pressure turns into craving, and using brings quick relief — but then shame returns and the cycle repeats. If we catch and name the feeling early, we can break the chain before craving takes over.

The cycle:   Trigger → unnamed feeling → rising tension → craving → use → short relief → shame/guilt → (back to trigger).
Where the new skill goes in: at "unnamed feeling" — stop, do a body scan, name it, name what it needs.
