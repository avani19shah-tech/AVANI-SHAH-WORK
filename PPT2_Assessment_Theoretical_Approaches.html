Module 3

The Assessment Process in Couple Therapy

25 Minutes

Assessment Structure: The Three-Session Model

SessionFormatFocusDuration
Session 1Conjoint (Both partners)Presenting concerns, observe interaction, relationship history, goals60–90 min
Session 2Individual (Partner A)Personal history, attachment, secrets, violence screening, individual goals45–60 min
Session 3Individual (Partner B)Same as above for other partner45–60 min
Session 4Feedback (Conjoint)Share formulation, recommend treatment plan, discuss goals & approach60 min
Key Point: NEVER skip individual sessions. They are essential for violence screening, secret-keeping assessment, and understanding each partner's attachment history.

What to Assess in the First Interview

Essential Areas

  • Presenting concerns – What brings you here today?
  • Observe interaction – How do they talk to each other? Who speaks? Who interrupts?
  • Safety – Any violence, threats, coercion?
  • Who wants what – Are both motivated? Is one coerced?
  • Precipitating event – Why NOW? What triggered help-seeking?

Additional Areas

  • Relationship timeline & history
  • Previous therapy attempts
  • Strengths & protective factors
  • Individual mental health concerns
  • Family involvement & cultural factors
  • Expectations from therapy
Clinical Tip: Watch the PROCESS more than the content. How they tell their story reveals the cycle.
⚠️ CRITICAL SAFETY PROTOCOL

Violence Screening – NEVER Skip This

🚨 ALWAYS conduct individual sessions for violence screening

Couple therapy is CONTRAINDICATED if there is ongoing intimate partner violence (IPV).

Screen For:

  • Physical violence – hitting, slapping, pushing, choking
  • Fear – "Are you afraid of your partner?"
  • Coercive control – financial control, isolation, monitoring
  • Dowry harassment – specific to Indian context (IPC 498A)
  • Sexual coercion – marital rape (not criminalized in India)
Tool: Use CTS-2 (Conflict Tactics Scale – Revised; Straus et al., 1996) for systematic screening. Ask in individual sessions ONLY.

👥 PAIR PRACTICE – 3 Minutes

Mock Intake: Circular Questions

With your partner, practice asking circular questions (questions that illuminate the relational pattern, not just individual experience):

Example Circular Questions

  • "When your wife becomes quiet, what do you do?"
  • "When he walks away, what happens inside you?"
  • "If your mother-in-law could see this argument, what would she say?"
  • "On a scale of 1–10, how close do you feel right now? What about your partner?"

Instructions

  1. One person plays "therapist," one plays "client"
  2. Therapist asks 3–4 circular questions
  3. Notice: How does it feel different from "What's wrong?"
  4. Switch roles after 90 seconds
Why Circular Questions? They shift from linear causality ("She causes my anger") to circular patterns ("We co-create this cycle"). This IS systems thinking in action.

Case Formulation

Putting It All Together: From Assessment to Treatment Plan

10 Minutes

Case Formulation Template (12 Components)

Description & Pattern

  1. Presenting problems – What each partner reports
  2. Negative cycle – The pursuer-withdrawer (or other) pattern
  3. Attachment needs – What each partner needs but can't ask for
  4. Predisposing factors – Family of origin, attachment history
  5. Precipitating factors – What triggered the current crisis
  6. Perpetuating factors – What maintains the cycle

Resources & Plan

  1. Protective factors – Strengths, commitment, shared values
  2. Cultural factors – Religion, caste, family system, migration
  3. Goals – Shared + individual goals for therapy
  4. Approach – Which model (EFT, CBCT, Structural, etc.)
  5. Duration – Estimated sessions (typically 12–20)
  6. Risks – Violence, suicide, separation, dropout
Remember: A good formulation is a shared hypothesis, not a fixed diagnosis. Update it as therapy progresses.
🧠 CASE FORMULATION EXERCISE – 5 Minutes

Practice: Formulate This Case

Case: Meera (34, teacher) and Suresh (38, IT professional). Love marriage, 8 years, one child (5 years). Living nuclear. Meera reports: "After our son was born, he became distant. He works 14-hour days, comes home and goes on his phone. I feel like a single mother. When I complain, he says I'm nagging." Suresh reports: "She doesn't understand the pressure I'm under. Everything I do is wrong. I provide well but she only notices what I don't do. I avoid coming home because it's just fights."

Identify with your neighbor (3 min):

  1. What is the negative cycle?
  2. What are the underlying attachment needs?
  3. What systemic/contextual factors are at play?
Cycle: Meera pursues (criticism, complaints) → Suresh withdraws (avoidance, phone, long hours) → Meera feels abandoned, pursues harder → Suresh feels inadequate, withdraws more.

Attachment: Meera – "Am I important to you? Are you here with me?" (need for connection, presence). Suresh – "Can I ever be enough for you?" (need for acceptance, approval).

Systemic: Transition to parenthood disrupted the dyad. Work-life imbalance. Gender role expectations (provider vs. emotional partner). No extended family support (nuclear). Perpetuating: his avoidance confirms her fear; her criticism confirms his inadequacy.

Module 4

Theoretical Foundations of Couple Therapy

15 Minutes

General Systems Theory

The foundational paradigm shift: from linear to circular thinking

Core Concepts

  • Circular causality – A affects B, B affects A (not just A causes B)
  • Homeostasis – System resists change to maintain stability
  • Boundaries – Who is in/out? Clear, diffuse, or rigid?
  • Triangulation – Two-person tension recruits a third
  • Feedback loops – Positive (escalating) vs. negative (stabilizing)

Clinical Application

  • Stop asking "Who started it?" – it's circular
  • Symptoms serve a function in the system
  • Change one part → whole system shifts
  • The "identified patient" carries the family's distress
  • Look for patterns, not pathology
Key Shift: "What's wrong with this person?" → "What's happening in this system?" This is the fundamental paradigm of couple & family therapy.

Bowen Family Systems Theory

Murray Bowen (1913–1990): The family as a multi-generational emotional unit

8 Interlocking Concepts

#ConceptKey Idea
1Differentiation of SelfAbility to maintain "I" while in emotional contact with others. LOW = reactive, fused
2TrianglesTwo-person system is unstable; under stress it triangulates a third (child, in-law, affair)
3Nuclear Family Emotional ProcessFour patterns: conflict, distance, over/underfunctioning, child focus
4Family Projection ProcessParents project anxiety onto most vulnerable child
5Multigenerational TransmissionPatterns repeat across generations (genogram reveals this)
6Emotional CutoffManaging anxiety by severing contact (not true differentiation)
7Sibling PositionBirth order affects relational patterns (Toman's profiles)
8Societal Emotional ProcessSociety mirrors family – regression under anxiety
Indian Context: Differentiation is especially challenging in collectivist cultures where "family first" is the norm. Low differentiation ≠ pathology in all contexts – but when it causes distress, it's treatable.

The Genogram: Mapping Family Patterns

What It Maps (3+ Generations)

  • Family structure (births, deaths, marriages, divorces)
  • Relationship quality (close, conflictual, cutoff, enmeshed)
  • Patterns (mental illness, substance use, violence, affairs)
  • Triangles and coalitions
  • Emotional cutoffs and fusion

Standard Symbols

  • □ = Male | ○ = Female
  • ⚊⚊ = Marriage | /⚊⚊ = Divorce
  • ~~~ = Conflict | ══ = Enmeshed
  • X = Deceased | …… = Cutoff

What to Look For

  • Repeating patterns across generations
  • Triangles (who gets pulled in during conflict?)
  • Cutoffs (unresolved relationships)
  • Role assignments (caretaker, scapegoat)

Indian Context Additions

  • Arranged marriage patterns & matchmaking
  • Caste & inter-caste marriages
  • Migration patterns (rural → urban, NRI)
  • In-law relationships & joint family dynamics
  • Dowry history
  • Gender preference patterns (son preference)

Sketch YOUR Family Genogram

On a piece of paper, draw your genogram (3 generations):

Grandparents → Parents → You & siblings

As you draw, notice:

Ask Yourself:

  • What patterns do you see repeating?
  • Any triangles? (Who gets pulled in during conflict?)
  • Any cutoffs? (Family members who don't speak?)
  • What was the model of marriage in your family?

Why This Matters:

  • Therapists carry their own family patterns into the room
  • Self-awareness prevents counter-transference blind spots
  • Bowen believed therapist's own differentiation is THE key tool
  • "You can only take clients as far as you've gone yourself"

Structural Family Therapy (Minuchin, 1974)

Core Concepts

  • Structure – Invisible rules organizing family interactions
  • Boundaries:
    • Clear – healthy (flexible, appropriate)
    • Diffuse – enmeshed (no privacy, over-involvement)
    • Rigid – disengaged (no connection, isolation)
  • Hierarchy – Parents in executive position; children below
  • Subsystems – Marital, parental, sibling subsystems

Therapeutic Techniques

  • Joining – Accommodate to family's style to enter system
  • Enactments – "Show me, don't tell me" (have couple interact in session)
  • Restructuring – Realign boundaries, clarify hierarchy
  • Unbalancing – Temporarily side with one member to shift power

Indian Context 🇮🇳

  • Joint family: Mother-in-law often in executive subsystem
  • Diffuse boundaries between marital & in-law subsystem
  • Goal: Strengthen couple boundary WITHOUT cutting off family

👥 GROUP ACTIVITY – 5 Minutes

Theory Application: Which Lens Would You Use?

Case: Anita (42) and Vikram (45), married 18 years, 2 teenagers. Presenting issue: Anita discovered Vikram's emotional affair (WhatsApp messages). Vikram says: "She's been so focused on the children for years, I felt invisible." Anita says: "I gave everything to this family and this is my reward?" Vikram's mother sides with him openly: "If you'd been a better wife, he wouldn't have looked elsewhere."

In groups of 3, discuss (2 min):

  • Through an EFT/Attachment lens – what do you see?
  • Through a Structural lens – what do you see?
  • Through a Narrative lens – what do you see?
EFT: Both have unmet attachment needs. Anita: "Do you see me? Value my sacrifice?" Vikram: "Am I enough? Do you desire me?" The affair = protest behaviour from withdrawal.

Structural: Mother-in-law in the marital subsystem (boundary violation). Parental subsystem has overtaken marital subsystem. Need to restructure hierarchy and strengthen couple boundary.

Narrative: Dominant story: "good wife/bad wife" narrative from MIL. Externalize the problem: "The disconnection" came between them. Find unique outcomes: times they DID feel close.

Takeaway: Multiple lenses enrich formulation. Most therapists integrate.

Hour 2: Key Takeaways

1. Assessment is Systematic: Use the 3-session model (conjoint → individual × 2 → feedback). NEVER skip individual sessions – violence screening is non-negotiable.
2. Use Standardized Tools: CSI for satisfaction, ECR for attachment, CTS-2 for safety. Let data complement clinical observation.
3. Case Formulation is Your Map: 12 components give you a comprehensive picture. Include the cycle, attachment needs, systemic factors, and cultural context.
4. Systems Thinking is Foundational: Circular causality, homeostasis, boundaries, triangles. The couple is a system within systems (family, culture, society).
5. Multiple Theories Enrich Practice: Bowen (differentiation, genogram), Structural (boundaries, hierarchy), Narrative (externalization), Strategic (directives). Integration is key.
